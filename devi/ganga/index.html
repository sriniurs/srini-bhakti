VIṢṆUPADĪ · BHĀGĪRATHĪ · JĀHNAVĪ · MOTHER RIVER

Gaṅgā

The River Called Mother · explained and illustrated

From the sky, through Śiva’s hair, to the sea

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Gaṅgā is: her names, her birth at Viṣṇu’s foot, the descent won by Bhagīratha, Śiva’s locks and Jahnu’s ear, the mother of Bhīṣma, her image, her festivals and the verse that brings her into every bath.

WHERE WE ARE

India has many holy rivers, but one is simply called Mother: Gaṅgā, the Ganges. She is a river that can be seen, measured and dammed, and a goddess who comes down from heaven, rides a sea creature, raised Bhīṣma, and is called into every ritual pot and every morning bath.

This guide tells her stories in order, reads her image, and shows how she is honoured, from Gaṅgotrī to Kāśī and from the Kāverī at Talakāvēri to a bucket of water at home. Her temple at the source is in our Gaṅgotrī guide.

With the blessings of the Devī,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: How can a river be a goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Ask anyone who lives by one, Zappy. She gives water, food, a road and, at the end, a resting place.

ZAPPY: Why does she sit on a crocodile?

PROF. BOLT: A makara, Zappy: a river creature, half crocodile, half fish. She rules the water and what lives in it.

The river called Mother.

HER NAMES

Each name holds a story

Gaṅgā. Usually explained as “the one who goes swiftly” (from gam, to go).

Viṣṇupadī. “Born from Viṣṇu’s foot”: in the Bhāgavata she starts where Vāmana’s toe pierced the shell of the universe (next page).

Bhāgīrathī and Jāhnavī. She is Bhāgīrathī after King Bhagīratha, who brought her down, and Jāhnavī after the sage Jahnu, who swallowed and released her. The river’s headstream is still called the Bhāgīrathī.

Tripathagā and Mandākinī. “Flowing in three worlds”: in heaven she is Mandākinī, on earth Bhāgīrathī, in the underworld Bhogavatī. In the night sky, many say, she is the Milky Way.

ZAPPY: Three worlds at once?

PROF. BOLT: Heaven, earth and below, Zappy. Wherever there is a thirst to be cleaned, she flows there.

One river, many names.

BORN AT VIṢṆU’S FOOT

The step that pierced the sky

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 5 (cosmography) and book 8 (Vāmana); see our Vāmana page.

The demon-king Bali ruled the three worlds. Viṣṇu came to his sacrifice as Vāmana, a dwarf student, and asked for three steps of land.
Bali agreed. Vāmana grew to fill the universe: one step covered the earth, the second the heavens.
The nail of his great toe split the outer shell of the universe, and the waters beyond creation poured in through the gap.
Brahmā, tradition adds, washed the Lord’s foot with water from his pot, and that water became Gaṅgā; washing the reddish-saffron foot, some say, gave her a pinkish tinge.
So she began in heaven as Viṣṇupadī, the water that touched God’s foot, and stayed there until a king on earth needed her.

ZAPPY: Water from God’s foot is holy?

PROF. BOLT: Water that has touched the one you love most, Zappy. That is why devotees sip the water used to bathe the deity.

She begins at the Lord’s foot.

SAGARA’S SIXTY THOUSAND SONS

Why she had to come down

The story

Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa (told by Viśvāmitra to young Rāma); also Mahābhārata and Purāṇas.

King Sagara of Ayodhyā, Rāma’s ancestor, began a horse sacrifice. Indra, afraid of the king’s growing power, stole the horse.
Sagara’s sixty thousand sons dug through the earth looking for it, and found it grazing beside the sage Kapila, who sat in meditation.
Taking him for the thief, they charged at him. Kapila opened his eyes, and his gaze burned all sixty thousand to ashes.
Sagara’s grandson Aṃśumān found the ashes and asked how they could be freed. Only the waters of heaven, Gaṅgā, flowing over the ashes, could purify them, he was told.
Aṃśumān and his son Dilīpa tried and failed. The task fell to Dilīpa’s son, Bhagīratha.

ZAPPY: Sixty thousand sons?

PROF. BOLT: A story number, Zappy, meaning a whole people. The point is that their pride needed the river’s mercy.

Pride burned; mercy needed.

BHAGĪRATHA BRINGS HER DOWN

Through Śiva’s hair and Jahnu’s ear

The story

Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa; Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 9. The phrase “bhagīratha prayatna”, a Bhagīratha-like effort, comes from this story.

Bhagīratha left his kingdom and did years of austerity. Brahmā granted that Gaṅgā would descend, but warned that the earth could not bear the force of her fall.
Bhagīratha then prayed to Śiva, who agreed to catch her. Proud Gaṅgā meant to sweep Śiva away; instead she vanished into his matted hair and wandered there for years.
Śiva released her gently, in streams. Bhagīratha drove his chariot ahead and the river followed him across the land.
On the way she flooded the āśrama of the sage Jahnu, who drank her up entirely. Bhagīratha pleaded again; Jahnu let her out through his ear, and she became Jāhnavī, his daughter.
At last she reached the sea and flowed over the ashes of Sagara’s sons, and they rose to heaven. Where she meets the ocean is Gaṅgāsāgar.

ZAPPY: Even Gaṅgā had to be humbled twice?

PROF. BOLT: By Śiva’s hair and Jahnu’s thirst, Zappy. Then she was gentle enough to carry the world’s sorrows.

Great power made gentle.

THE MOTHER OF BHĪṢMA

Gaṅgā and King Śantanu

The story

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan.

King Śantanu of Hastināpura met a beautiful woman on the bank of the Gaṅgā and asked her to marry him. She agreed on one condition: he must never question anything she did.
Each time a son was born, she carried the baby to the river and drowned it. Seven times Śantanu kept silent.
When she took the eighth child, he could bear it no more and stopped her. She revealed herself as Gaṅgā: the eight sons were the eight Vasus, cursed to be born human, and she had freed seven at once.
The eighth, who had to live a full life, she took away and raised, teaching him the Vedas and the arts of war, and later returned him to Śantanu as Devavrata.
Devavrata’s terrible vow of celibacy for his father’s sake earned him the name Bhīṣma, “the terrible”. Grandfather of the Kurus, he is the son of a river.

ZAPPY: She drowned her own babies?

PROF. BOLT: She freed them, Zappy, by the story’s logic: they were gods serving a short sentence. The one who had to stay became Bhīṣma.

The river’s son: Bhīṣma.

READING HER IMAGE

The makara, the water-pot and the white goddess

White and calm. She is fair as the moon, crowned and dressed in white, usually standing or seated on her makara.

In her hands. A full water-pot (kalaśa) and a lotus or water-lily; in four-armed forms also a rosary and the gesture of protection; in Bengal sometimes the conch and discus of Viṣṇu.

At temple doorways. From the Gupta period on, Gaṅgā on her makara and Yamunā on her tortoise flank temple entrances, so that every visitor passes, symbolically, through the two rivers and is cleansed before entering. Watch for them in old temples like those at Belūr and Haḷebīḍu.

In Śiva’s hair. In images of Śiva she appears as a small woman or a stream in his locks, beside the crescent moon: he is Gaṅgādhara, “bearer of Gaṅgā”.

ZAPPY: Rivers at the temple door?

PROF. BOLT: So you enter clean, Zappy, even if your feet are dusty. The architecture does the bathing.

Enter through the river.

IN THE VERSE

Gaṅge ca yamune

The verse that brings her into every bath
gaṅge ca yamune caiva godāvari sarasvatinarmade sindhu kāveri jale ’smin saṃnidhiṃ kuru
O Gaṅgā and Yamunā, Godāvarī and Sarasvatī, Narmadā, Sindhu and Kāverī: be present in this water.
WordMeaning
gaṅgeO Gaṅgā
yamuneO Yamunā
jale asminin this water
saṃnidhimpresence
kurumake, grant

Every bath a pilgrimage. Said while pouring water at a bath or filling the kalaśa for a pūjā, this verse invites the seven great rivers into a bucket or a pot. Gaṅgā is named first. For a Mysuru household, the Kāverī at the end is the river at the doorstep.

Text as given by shlokam.org (“while bathing”) and greenmesg.org; also found in the Nārada Purāṇa, Pūrva Bhāga 3.66.27.
Any water, made holy.

HER FESTIVALS

Gaṅgā Saptamī and Gaṅgā Daśaharā

Gaṅgā Saptamī. The seventh of the bright half of Vaiśākha (April–May) is kept as her birthday, Gaṅgā Jayantī, often told as the day Jahnu released her.

Gaṅgā Daśaharā. The tenth of the bright half of Jyeṣṭha (May–June) marks her descent to earth. Its name, “taking away ten”, is read as the removal of ten kinds of wrong-doing; crowds bathe at Haridvār, Prayāga and Kāśī.

Kārtika. In the bathing month of Kārtika and at Kārtika Pūrṇimā, lamps are floated on her water; at the Kumbh Melā millions bathe at the confluence.

Every day. The great evening Gaṅgā āratī at Haridvār’s Har kī Pauṛī and at Kāśī’s Daśāśvamedha Ghāṭ: priests wave many-tiered lamps to the river as bells ring.

ZAPPY: A festival for her coming down and another for her birthday?

PROF. BOLT: Mothers get both, Zappy: the day she was born and the day she came home.

Her birthday and her homecoming.

ALONG HER COURSE

From Gaṅgotrī to the sea

Gaṅgotrī. Her temple near the source, where Bhagīratha is said to have done his penance; the glacier mouth beyond is Gomukh. See Gaṅgotrī.

Haridvār. “Door of Hari”, where she leaves the mountains; one of the seven sacred cities and a Kumbh site.

Prayāga. The Triveṇī, where Gaṅgā meets Yamunā and the hidden Sarasvatī; the greatest Kumbh is held here.

Kāśī. On the crescent of ghāṭs at Vārāṇasī, Viśvanātha’s city, people come to die by her banks; ashes of the dead are given to her across India.

Gaṅgāsāgar. Where she meets the Bay of Bengal, with Kapila’s temple, the end of Bhagīratha’s journey; a great bath at Makara Saṅkrānti.

ZAPPY: Why do people want their ashes in the Gaṅgā?

PROF. BOLT: Remember Sagara’s sons, Zappy. Her water freed their ashes; families ask the same for theirs.

From source to sea.

GAṄGĀ IN THE SOUTH

Every river can be her

A verse from the shlokam.org collection names the Netrāvatī, Kāverī, Kapilā and other rivers together with Gaṅgā as “born of Śrī Hari’s lotus feet”.

🌊 Dakṣiṇa Gaṅgā

The Godāvarī and Kāverī are each called the “Gaṅgā of the South”. Talakāvēri in Kodagu, where the Kāverī rises, is visited like Gaṅgotrī; see Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa.

🪷 Every year at Kāverī

A tradition says Gaṅgā herself comes to bathe in the Kāverī in the month of Tulā to wash away the sins people leave in her, which is why the Tulā bath is prized.

🏞️ Tuṅgā

“Tuṅgā pāna, Gaṅgā snāna”: drink the Tuṅgā’s water, bathe in the Gaṅgā’s, as the Kannada saying goes. Śṛṅgeri sits on the Tuṅgā.

🫙 At home

Families keep a sealed pot of Gaṅgā water (gaṅgājala) to sprinkle in purification and to give a few drops to the dying, even far from the river.

ZAPPY: So I can meet Gaṅgā in Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: In the Kāverī, Zappy, or in your bath with the right verse. She goes wherever she is invited.

She goes where she is invited.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Mercy that flows downhill

Grace comes down. Gaṅgā’s whole story is descent: from beyond the universe to heaven, to Śiva, to earth, to the underworld. Many read her as grace that flows to the lowest place it can find. (Interpretation.)

Effort draws grace. She came because Bhagīratha would not give up. “Bhagīratha prayatna” still means a supreme effort.

Power softened. Śiva’s hair tames her force; Jahnu humbles her pride. A gift too strong destroys; it has to be received in a form the world can bear. (Interpretation.)

A river to care for. Devotees and scientists alike now speak of keeping her clean; many teachers say that honouring a river-mother includes not polluting her.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Gaṅgā?

PROF. BOLT: Say the seven-rivers verse at your next bath, Zappy, and don’t throw anything into a river that a mother wouldn’t want.

Grace flows to the lowest place.
