Godāvarī

The Gaṅgā of the South · explained and illustrated

The Gaṅgā of the South

HOW THIS WORKS: The longest river of the peninsula: the sage Gautama and the cow, Tryambakeśvara and Kuśāvarta, Rāma at Pañcavaṭī, Basara, Bhadrācalam and Rāmadāsa, the seven mouths, the Puṣkara and the Gautamī Māhātmya.

WHERE WE ARE

The Godāvarī is the longest river of the peninsula and the one the south calls Dakṣiṇa Gaṅgā, the Gaṅgā of the South. She rises on Brahmagiri near Tryambakeśvara, crosses the Deccan from west to east, and reaches the Bay of Bengal by seven mouths below Rājamahendravaram.

Her story is a sage’s story. The ṛṣi Gautama, tricked into seeming to kill a cow, brought down a stream of the Gaṅgā from Śiva’s hair to cleanse himself, and the river took his name as Gautamī. This part tells that legend in its two great versions, then follows her: to Kuśāvarta, to Rāma’s Pañcavaṭī, past Basara, Kāḷēśvaram and Bhadrācalam, to the seven mouths, the Puṣkara and the hundred-odd tīrthas of the Gautamī Māhātmya.

The previous part was Yamunā; the next is Narmadā. The temple at her source has its own guide: Tryambakeśvara.

PROF. BOLT: The Purāṇas say so, Zappy: the same water, a different door. That is why the south calls her Gaṅgā too.

The Gaṅgā of the South.

HER NAMES

Godāvarī, Gautamī, Dakṣiṇa Gaṅgā

Godāvarī. The name is usually explained as go + dā: go means a cow, but also water, light and the earth, and dā means “to give”. So she is the giver of cows, of water, of plenty. It is a traditional reading; the cow in her founding story makes it a favourite. (The etymology is traditional.)

Gautamī. Because the sage Gautama brought her, she is Gautamī or Gautamī Gaṅgā. The Brahma Purāṇa uses this name throughout its long praise of her, the Gautamī Māhātmya, and the Jyotirliṅga hymn places Tryambaka “on the Gautamī’s bank”. Her largest delta branch still carries the name.

Dakṣiṇa Gaṅgā. In the Purāṇic telling she is literally the Gaṅgā: the part of the heavenly river that stayed in Śiva’s hair when Bhagīratha brought the rest down. So she is the Gaṅgā of the South, a title the Kāverī shares in her own country.

Seven names at the sea. Near the coast she splits into seven branches named after seven sages, the Sapta Godāvarī; one of them is still called Gautamī, as if the river signs her founder’s name at both ends.

ZAPPY: So “Godāvarī” means cow-giver?

PROF. BOLT: That is the usual reading, Zappy. And the whole river begins with a cow, so it fits.

Named for a cow and a sage.

THE COW AND THE GAṄGĀ

The Brahma Purāṇa’s telling

The story

Brahma Purāṇa, Gautamī Māhātmya ch. 4–6 (Bhatt tr.); retellings differ in details.

A mother’s worry. Pārvatī was unhappy that Śiva kept Gaṅgā in his matted hair, so close to him. Persuasion failed, so her son Vināyaka (Gaṇeśa) set out to free his mother of the problem.
A drought and a refuge. A great drought struck the earth for many years. Only the hermitage of the sage Gautama on Brahmagiri stayed green, and sages from everywhere took shelter there. Vināyaka went to live among them.
The cow. Vināyaka asked Jayā, a companion of Pārvatī, to become a cow, graze on Gautama’s grain, and fall down as if dead when he drove her away. Gautama shooed her with a blade of grass; she bellowed and fell.
The price. The sages refused to stay where a cow had been killed. Vināyaka told Gautama that only Gaṅgā’s water from Śiva’s hair, flowing over the cow, would wash away the sin.
The descent. Gautama went to Kailāsa and won Śiva over with penance and hymns. Śiva released the Gaṅgā left in his hair; Gautama led her down to Brahmagiri, and she became the Gautamī Gaṅgā. The Purāṇa says Gautama knew of the trick and did the task gladly for the world’s sake.

ZAPPY: So Gaṇeśa tricked a good man?

PROF. BOLT: For a good cause, Zappy: the south got its Gaṅgā. And the Purāṇa says Gautama saw through it and went anyway.

A trick that brought a river.

THE SAGES’ GRUDGE

The Śiva Purāṇa’s telling and Kuśāvarta

The story

From the Śiva Purāṇa, Koṭirudra Saṃhitā (ch. 24–27), on the origin of the Tryambakeśvara jyotirliṅga.

Envy. During the drought, Varuṇa gave Gautama a pond that never dried. The other sages’ families grew jealous of his plenty, and the sages prayed to Gaṇeśa to have him driven away.
The skeletal cow. Gaṇeśa, unwilling but bound by their worship, took the form of a weak, skeletal cow and wandered into Gautama’s field. Gautama pushed it away with a straw, and it fell dead.
Penance. Accused of cow-killing, Gautama moved away and did a long penance, with circuits of the mountain and worship of a crore of earthen liṅgas, until Śiva appeared.
Tryambaka. Gautama asked for the Gaṅgā; the gods asked Śiva to stay there too. So Śiva remained as Tryambakeśvara, and the Gaṅgā came down as the Gautamī. The gods promised to gather there whenever Jupiter is in Siṃha.
Kuśāvarta. Local tradition adds that the river kept vanishing into the hill; Gautama ringed her with kuśa grass, and she rose in a pool there, Kuśāvarta, “the kuśa ring”. From it the Godāvarī is said to flow on.

ZAPPY: Two stories, two villains?

PROF. BOLT: Two tellings, Zappy. In one Gaṇeśa helps his mother; in the other he is pushed by jealous sages. Traditions differ, and both end with the same river.

Held by a ring of grass.

BRAHMAGIRI AND KUŚĀVARTA

Where she begins

The hill of Brahmā. Brahmagiri, a flat-topped hill of the Western Ghāṭs above the town of Trimbak, is where the Godāvarī begins. Pilgrims climb to the shrine at the spring, called Gaṅgādvāra, the Gaṅgā’s door. Tradition says Śiva, pleased with Brahmā, took the hill’s name for himself.

Kuśāvarta. In the town below, the square tank of Kuśāvarta is where the river is said to reappear after Gautama ringed her with kuśa grass. It is the bathing place of the Siṃhastha Kumbh, which Trimbak shares with Nāsik’s Rāmkuṇḍ; see the Kumbh Melā guide.

Ahalyā’s stream. Just outside the Tryambakeśvara temple a small stream named Ahalyā, after Gautama’s wife, joins the young Godāvarī. The Gautamī Māhātmya counts an Ahalyā-saṅgama among its tīrthas (ch. 16), linked to Indra’s offence against her and his release.

For the ancestors. Trimbak is famous for rites for the dead; the tradition says a śrāddha on the Godāvarī here satisfies the forefathers as few other places can. Families from Karnataka and far beyond come for them; see Tryambakeśvara.

ZAPPY: A river that starts in a temple town?

PROF. BOLT: Most of India’s holy rivers do, Zappy. The town grew because the river began there.

She begins at Gaṅgā’s door.

RĀMA ON THE GODĀVARĪ

Pañcavaṭī and the name Nāsik

Agastya’s advice. In the forest book of the Rāmāyaṇa, Rāma asks the sage Agastya where to spend the rest of his exile. Agastya sends him to Pañcavaṭī, “five banyans”, near the Godāvarī, a place of fruit, water and birds where Sītā will be happy.

Life by the river. Lakṣmaṇa builds a leaf hut, and the three live there through the seasons; Vālmīki describes Rāma walking down to bathe in the Godāvarī in the cold of Hemanta, Lakṣmaṇa talking of Bharata as they go.

The nose. At Pañcavaṭī, Rāvaṇa’s sister Śūrpaṇakhā attacked Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa cut off her nose. Popular tradition derives the name Nāsik from nāsikā, “nose”; a shrine in the town marks the spot.

The loss. From here Rāvaṇa carried Sītā away, and on the way the vulture Jaṭāyu fought him. So the Godāvarī’s bank is where the second half of the epic begins. Nāsik’s Kālārām temple and Rāmkuṇḍ keep the memory; see Rāma.

ZAPPY: So the Godāvarī saw both Rāma’s happiest days and his worst?

PROF. BOLT: In the same few months, Zappy. Vālmīki’s Godāvarī is calm and lovely; then everything changes on her bank.

Where the story turns.

ACROSS THE DECCAN

Paiṭhaṇ, Basara and Kāḷēśvaram

Paiṭhaṇ. On her upper course stands Paiṭhaṇ, ancient Pratiṣṭhāna, capital of the Sātavāhana kings two thousand years ago and home of the Marāṭhī saint Eknāth in the sixteenth century, who is remembered for bathing in the Godāvarī and serving the outcast.

Basara. Near where the Mañjīrā joins her, at Basara in Telangana, stands the temple of Jñāna Sarasvatī. The story says Vyāsa, seeking peace after the war, bathed in the Godāvarī daily and each time brought three handfuls of her sand, which became images of Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī and Kālī. Children are brought here for akṣarābhyāsa, their first letters; the name is read as “Vyāsa’s place”.

Kāḷēśvaram. Further east, where the Praṇahitā comes in, Kāḷēśvaram is counted a triveṇī, with an unseen Sarasvatī as the third stream; its temple holds two liṅgas on one pedestal, of Śiva and of Yama. The Sarasvatī Puṣkara is kept here.

Many rivers in one. The Pravarā, Mañjīrā, Praṇahitā, Indrāvatī and Śabarī all feed her. The Gautamī Māhātmya counts a confluence with each as a tīrtha, so a pilgrim walking her banks is never far from a holy meeting.

ZAPPY: Sarasvatī made of the Godāvarī’s sand?

PROF. BOLT: That is Basara’s story, Zappy. The river gave the goddess of learning her body.

Learning from her sand.

BHADRĀCALAM AND RĀMADĀSA

The tax-collector who built a temple

The story

As told in the Bhadrācalam tradition and in Rāmadāsa’s own songs; history and legend are mixed, and details vary.

The hill. On a hill by the Godāvarī, a devout woman of the area, Pokala Dhammakka, is said to have found images of Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa and built them a simple shelter.
The officer. In the seventeenth century Kañcarla Gopanna was the revenue officer of the region under Abul Hasan Qutb Shāh of Golconda (1672–1686). Moved by the neglected shrine, he spent the revenue he had collected on building a proper temple.
Prison. Charged with misusing the treasury, Gopanna was thrown into the Golconda fort’s prison. There he poured out songs to Rāma, the kīrtanas that made him Bhadrācala Rāmadāsa.
The gold. The story says that one night two young men came to the sultan, paid every coin Gopanna owed in gold stamped with Rāma’s name, and took a receipt. When the sultan woke, the gold was real; he freed Gopanna and endowed the temple.
Dakṣiṇa Ayodhyā. Bhadrācalam is called the Ayodhyā of the South. Its Rāma Navamī wedding of Sītā and Rāma is famous across the Telugu country; see Rāma Navamī.

ZAPPY: Rāma paid a prisoner’s fine?

PROF. BOLT: That is the story the devotees tell, Zappy. Historians see a pious officer and a sultan who was finally persuaded. Both versions love the songs.

A temple paid for twice.

THE SEVEN MOUTHS

Rājamahendravaram and the Sapta Godāvarī

The head of the delta. At Rājamahendravaram (Rājahmundry) the river is at her widest, with long ghāṭs where the Godāvarī Puṣkara draws its greatest crowds. The town is also the cradle of Telugu literature: tradition says Nannaya began the Telugu Mahābhārata here in the eleventh century.

Seven branches. Below the town she divides into the Gautamī and Vasiṣṭha branches and spreads into a delta. Tradition counts seven mouths, each named for a sage: Tulyabhāgā, Ātreyī, Gautamī, Jāmadagnī (now Vṛddha Gautamī), Bhāradvājī, Kauśikī and Vāsiṣṭhī.

The Sapta Sāgara yātrā. A traditional pilgrimage visits all seven meetings of river and sea. The Gautamī Māhātmya’s last chapters describe the Godāvarī entering the sea in seven branches (ch. 102) and the tīrthas at Bhīmeśvara (ch. 103).

Seven in one tank. At Drākṣārāmam the temple tank is called Sapta Godāvarī, said to hold water from all seven branches, brought by the seven sages; see Drākṣārāmam.

ZAPPY: Seven mouths for seven sages?

PROF. BOLT: Each sage claimed a branch, Zappy. It is a lovely way of saying the whole delta is holy.

She meets the sea by seven doors.

THE GODĀVARĪ PUṢKARA

Jupiter in Siṃha

Her twelve days. When Jupiter enters Siṃha (Leo), the Godāvarī has her Puṣkara. The Śiva Purāṇa’s telling of Tryambaka already has the gods promise to gather on her banks when the teacher of the gods is in Siṃha, so this Puṣkara is among the oldest and the most crowded.

Kumbh at the source. The same planetary moment gives the Siṃhastha Kumbh at Nāsik and Trimbak, so the river is honoured at both ends of her course at once, with the Kumbh at the source and the Puṣkara in the delta.

Mahā Puṣkara. Every twelfth Godāvarī Puṣkara, once in about 144 years, is called a Mahā Puṣkara. The last was kept in July 2015, chiefly at Rājamahendravaram and the delta ghāṭs.

What is done. Bath with saṅkalpa, gifts (dāna) of food, cloth and grain, and piṇḍa offerings for the ancestors on the bank, as described in Part 1.

ZAPPY: Does the river know it is her turn?

PROF. BOLT: The people know, Zappy. That is what makes a turn.

Her turn in the lion’s sign.

THE GAUTAMĪ MĀHĀTMYA

A hundred and five chapters on one river

No other river has a Purāṇic praise as long as the Godāvarī’s. A few of its tīrthas, by chapter, as listed in G. P. Bhatt’s translation:

📖 The book

Part four of the Brahma Purāṇa: Brahmā tells Nārada of her fords, one by one.

🐄 Gotīrtha

The gods take back land given as a gift and give a tawny cow in exchange: a cow, the book says, is a better gift than land.

🌍 Kapilā-saṅgama (ch. 71)

Where the earth, as a cow, gave her milk to King Pṛthu, and he milked it into the great rivers, the Godāvarī above all.

🦅 Garuḍa and Govardhana

Chapters 20 and 21 give Garuḍa and Kṛṣṇa’s hill fords on her bank.

💸 Ṛṇamocana (ch. 29)

“Release from debt”: a ford where debts to gods, sages and ancestors are said to be paid off.

🐒 Kiṣkindhā and Vyāsa (ch. 87–88)

The monkey kingdom and Vyāsa both have fords on her banks; the Purāṇa gathers the epics to her.

🌊 Seven mouths (ch. 102)

The river meets the sea in seven branches, each a tīrtha.

👂 The fruit (ch. 105)

Hearing these chapters, the book says, is itself a pilgrimage along the river.

ZAPPY: A hundred and five chapters for one river?

PROF. BOLT: She is long and old, Zappy. Every village on her bank wanted its story in the book.

A river written down ford by ford.

KARNATAKA AND THE GODĀVARĪ

A neighbour’s river

Next door. The Godāvarī herself does not flow through Karnataka, but her longest tributary, the Mañjīrā, crosses the state’s north-east corner in Bidar district before joining her in Telangana. Bidar’s farmers are, in that sense, on the Godāvarī’s land.

The Datta link. The Datta tradition ties the Godāvarī country to Karnataka: Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha, counted the first human form of Dattātreya, was born at Pīṭhāpuram in the Godāvarī delta, and his successor Narasiṃha Sarasvatī made Gāṇagāpura on the Bhīmā his home. See Gāṇagāpura.

Rites at Trimbak. Kannada families, like families from every state, travel to Trimbak for rites for the dead that are done best on the Godāvarī, and many Mādhva and Smārta households keep a pot of Godāvarī water from such a visit.

In the verse. And she is the third river named in every Kannada pūjā’s kalaśa verse, after Gaṅgā and Yamunā, the first of the southern rivers in the list.

ZAPPY: So Karnataka touches her through a tributary?

PROF. BOLT: And through a guru, Zappy, and through every pot of pūjā water.

A neighbour in every pūjā.

WHAT THE GODĀVARĪ TEACHES

A sage’s river

Grace through blame. Gautama did nothing wrong; he was blamed for a cow he did not kill, and his answer was not argument but penance, and a river came of it. Many read this as a lesson that a false accusation borne well can bring good to the world. (Interpretation.)

Every cow matters. The whole river turns on one cow. The founding story makes care for cattle, and for any life, the centre of the Godāvarī’s holiness. (Interpretation.)

Two Gaṅgās. The north had its Gaṅgā by a king’s effort, the south by a sage’s. The Purāṇas let every region have the holiest water without taking it from anyone else.

A river of tīrthas. The Gautamī Māhātmya’s 105 chapters say, in effect, that the whole length of a river can be holy, not just a few famous ghāṭs. For people living on her banks that is a reminder that the river outside their own town is sacred too.

ZAPPY: One thing to remember about her?

PROF. BOLT: That a blade of grass and a cow brought the Gaṅgā to the south, Zappy. Small things, big rivers.

A cow brought the Gaṅgā south.
