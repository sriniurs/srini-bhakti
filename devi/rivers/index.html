WHERE WE ARE

Every Hindu bath can be a pilgrimage. Before pouring the first mug, many people name seven rivers, from the Gaṅgā in the north to the Kāverī in the south, and ask them to be present in that small vessel of water. A child in Mysuru learns those names long before learning where the rivers flow.

This series is about those rivers. This first part looks at the idea behind them all: why a river is called a mother, the Ṛgveda’s hymn to the rivers, the seven of the Veda and the seven of the bath verse, the tīrtha or crossing place, the sacred bath, the twelve-year Puṣkara, the ancestors, the river goddesses at temple doors, and the rivers of Karnataka.

Two rivers have their own guides already: Gaṅgā and Sarasvatī. The parts that follow take the other rivers one by one: Yamunā, Godāvarī, Narmadā, Sindhu, Kāverī and Tuṅgabhadrā.

PROF. BOLT: India has hundreds of holy rivers, Zappy. Seven is the number that fits in one breath before a bath, and these seven stand for the whole land.

THE RIVER AS MOTHER

Why rivers are called mā

She feeds. A river brings water to fields, cattle and towns, as a mother brings milk. In India this is not a figure of speech: almost every old city, temple town and farming belt sits on a river, and a bad monsoon on a river is still felt in every kitchen downstream.

The oldest word. The Ṛgveda calls Sarasvatī ambitame nadītame devitame, “best of mothers, best of rivers, best of goddesses” (2.41.16). The three titles sit side by side, as if being a mother, a river and a goddess were one job.

Rivers as cows and mothers. In the river hymn of the tenth book, the streams run towards the Sindhu like mothers to a child and like milch cows lowing as they come with milk (10.75.4). The picture is of plenty that cannot help flowing.

Every river a she. Almost all Indian rivers are feminine and are addressed as Mā or Ammā: Gaṅgā Maiyā in the north, Kāverī Ammā in the south. A few are counted male, the Brahmaputra and the Sōn among them, and traditions differ on others.

ZAPPY: So when people say “Gaṅgā Mā”, they mean it literally?

PROF. BOLT: They mean she cares for them without being asked, Zappy. That is the most literal thing a child can say about a mother.

A river feeds like a mother.

THE HYMN OF THE RIVERS

Ṛgveda 10.75, the Nadīstuti

Nine verses. The tenth book of the Ṛgveda holds a short hymn of nine verses known as the Nadīstuti, “the praise of rivers”. Tradition names its seer Sindhukṣit Praiyamedha. Its hero is the Sindhu, the Indus, praised as the mightiest of the streams in verses 1, 2, 7, 8 and 9.

Rivers in sevens. The first verse says the waters went forth in three sets of seven, with the Sindhu surpassing them all in force. The second says Varuṇa himself cut the paths for her to run, and that she flows over the heights ahead of all moving things.

A roar and a bull. Her roar rises to the sky like thunder from a rain-cloud, and she comes on bellowing like a bull. A fifth verse calls the rivers of the east by name, and a sixth the rivers of the far north-west: Kubhā (the Kabul river), Gomatī (the Gomal), Krumu (the Kurram) and others.

A rich river. The last verses dress the Sindhu in horses, chariots, fine cloth, gold and wool, and picture her as a young woman on a chariot of joy. Historians value the hymn as a rare map of Vedic geography; devotees value it as the oldest hymn to rivers in Sanskrit.

ZAPPY: A hymn that is also a map?

PROF. BOLT: The seer names the rivers in order from east to west, Zappy. Scholars still use it to work out where the Vedic people lived.

The first hymn to rivers.

THE SEVEN RIVERS OF THE VEDA

Sapta sindhavaḥ

A land of seven rivers. The Ṛgveda speaks again and again of the sapta sindhavaḥ, “the seven rivers”. One verse says the Sun surveyed the eight peaks of the earth, its three dry regions and its seven rivers (1.35.8). The seven are a whole country as much as a list.

Which seven? The hymns never give a fixed list. Many scholars place them in the Punjab: the Sindhu, the five rivers between (Śutudrī, Vipāśā, Paruṣṇī, Asiknī, Vitastā) and the Sarasvatī. One verse makes Sarasvatī the seventh with the Sindhu as her mother (7.36.6); another calls her “she of seven sisters”. Traditions and scholars differ.

The same name in Iran. The Avesta, the old scripture of the Zoroastrians, lists hapta həndu, “seven rivers”, among the lands its god made. Old Iranian turned Sanskrit s into h, so Sindhu became Hindu. Through Persian and Greek this word gave the world “Hindu”, “Indus” and “India”.

Five instead of seven. The later name Punjab is Persian for “five waters”, a translation of the Sanskrit pañcanada. The count changed, but the habit of naming a land by its rivers did not.

ZAPPY: So the name India comes from a river?

PROF. BOLT: From the Sindhu, Zappy. Part 5 tells that story properly.

A land named by its rivers.

A map in two lines. Read as geography, the verse draws India: the northern plains, the north-west, the Vedic past, the middle, the Deccan and the far south. The Vedic seven were all in the north; this later seven reaches the tip of the peninsula, and for a south Indian the list closes at home with the Kāverī.

TĪRTHA

The crossing place

The word. Tīrtha comes from the root tṝ, “to cross over”. Its first meaning is a ford: the shallow place where people could walk or wade across a river. The scholar Diana Eck points out that the crossing is also upward, from this shore of life to the far shore, so a tīrtha is a place where heaven and earth are close.

More than rivers. Over time tīrtha came to mean any holy place, the holy water given in a temple, and even a holy person. Jainism calls its great teachers tīrthaṅkaras, “ford-makers”. Saṃnyāsins of the Śṛṅgeri lineage carry the name Tīrtha, as in Bhāratī Tīrtha.

The great tour. The Mahābhārata’s forest book holds a long Tīrthayātrā Parva. The sage Pulastya tells Bhīṣma which fords to visit and what each one gives, and the sage Nārada later retells it to Yudhiṣṭhira, who sets out with his brothers on the same circuit.

The inner ford. The same epic says the body’s fords matter as much as the river’s: truth, patience, self-control and a clean mind are called tīrthas too, and a bath in water does not cleanse a person whose heart stays dirty. Many teachers read the outer pilgrimage as a support for this inner one. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: So I can go on a tīrtha without leaving the house?

PROF. BOLT: The epic says the inner one goes with you, Zappy. But it still sends the Pāṇḍavas walking to the real rivers.

A tīrtha is a place to cross.

THE SACRED BATH

Snāna in a river

First the intention. A ritual bath begins with a saṅkalpa: the bather says aloud where and when he is bathing and why. Monier-Williams, describing a householder’s day in 1883, noted that the bath should be in a sacred stream, else a tank or pond, and only in need at home, and that the bather first announces the bath he is about to take.

Into the water. The usual pattern is three dips, often facing the sun or the current, with the river verse or the name of the river on the lips. Then water is cupped in both hands and poured towards the sun as arghya, and offered to the gods, the sages and the ancestors as tarpaṇa.

Manners in the river. The dharma texts lay down conduct for river bathing: do not bathe naked, do not throw dirt or waste into the water, and honour the river before using it. Teachers in Karnataka still remind bathers to leave soap and shampoo on the bank during the bathing months.

An elephant’s bath. An old saying calls a careless bath a kuñjara-snāna, an elephant’s bath: the elephant washes in the river and then throws dust over itself on the bank. A holy dip followed by the old habits is the same thing. Kārtika, Māgha and Vaiśākha are the great bathing months; see Kārtika · 7 articles.

ZAPPY: Do I have to say all that in a bathroom?

PROF. BOLT: The texts allow a home bath when a river is far, Zappy. The verse is how the river comes to you.

A bath with intention.

WHY THE RIVERS TAKE TURNS

The story of Puṣkara

The story

A legend told in astrology books and Puṣkara handbooks, mostly in the Telugu and Kannada country; versions differ in who gave the first boon.

A boon. A pious brāhmaṇa (named Tuṇḍila in some tellings) pleased Śiva with long penance and was given the power to live in water and make it pure. He became Puṣkara, lord of all the holy waters.
Brahmā’s pot. Brahmā, needing purifying water for his work of creation, asked for Puṣkara, and Puṣkara went to live in Brahmā’s water pot. Other tellings put Brahmā’s boon first and Śiva’s second.
Jupiter asks. Bṛhaspati, teacher of the gods and the planet Jupiter, asked Brahmā for Puṣkara too. Puṣkara did not want to leave Brahmā.
A fair split. Brahmā settled it: Puṣkara would go with Bṛhaspati for the first twelve days after Jupiter enters a new sign and the last twelve days before he leaves it, and live in the river that belongs to that sign.
The gods come too. Wherever Puṣkara goes, the gods, sages and all the holy fords go with him. So in those days one river becomes the holiest water in the land, and people bathe, give gifts and offer rites for their ancestors there.

ZAPPY: So a river gets its turn once in twelve years?

PROF. BOLT: Jupiter takes about twelve years to go round the zodiac, Zappy. Each river’s turn comes round with him.

Every river gets its turn.

THE TWELVE PUṢKARA RIVERS

One river for each sign of Jupiter

Each Puṣkara is held when Jupiter enters that river’s sign. The first twelve days (Ādi Puṣkara) draw the crowds; the last twelve (Antya Puṣkara) are also kept. Lists agree on most signs and differ on a few, as noted.

♈ Meṣa and Vṛṣabha

Aries: Gaṅgā. Taurus: Narmadā. The cycle is counted from the Gaṅgā.

♊ Mithuna and Karkaṭa

Gemini: Sarasvatī, kept where she is believed to flow unseen, as at Kāḷēśvaram. Cancer: Yamunā.

♌ Siṃha and Kanyā

Leo: Godāvarī; every twelfth round of hers is called a Mahā Puṣkara. Virgo: Kṛṣṇā.

♎ Tulā and Vṛścika

Libra: Kāverī. Scorpio: Bhīmā (Bhīmarathī); in Tamil Nadu the Tāmraparṇī.

♐ Dhanus and Makara

Sagittarius: Puṣkaravāhinī, read as the Brahmaputra or the Tāpī. Capricorn: Tuṅgabhadrā.

♒ Kumbha and Mīna

Aquarius: Sindhu. Pisces: Praṇahitā, a tributary of the Godāvarī.

ZAPPY: Why do the lists disagree for Scorpio and Sagittarius?

PROF. BOLT: Regions kept the feast on the rivers they had, Zappy. Both answers are honest; traditions differ.

Twelve rivers, twelve years.

RIVERS AND THE ANCESTORS

Water for those who have gone

Tarpaṇa. The simplest rite for the dead is water. Standing in a river or holding water with sesame seeds and darbha grass, the son or heir pours handfuls for his father’s line and mother’s line by name. The word means “satisfying”: the ancestors are said to be refreshed by it.

Ashes to the river. After cremation the bones and ashes are gathered and given to a holy river, a rite called asthi-visarjana. Gaṅgā is the first wish; most families use the nearest sacred river or a confluence. The story of Sagara’s sons, whose ashes waited for the Gaṅgā, is told in the Gaṅgā guide.

Near Mysuru. For Mysuru, Bengaluru and Mandya families the place is usually the Kāverī at Paścimavāhinī near Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, where the river turns west, or the confluence at Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa or Tirumakūḍalu. The ashes of the last Mysuru royal scion, Śrīkaṇṭhadatta Narasiṃharāja Woḍeyar, were given to the Kāverī at these places in 2013.

At the river feasts. Puṣkara days and the bathing months are favoured for a tīrtha-śrāddha, rites for all the ancestors at a holy river. The yearly fortnight for the ancestors is in Pitṛ Pakṣa · 5 articles.

ZAPPY: Why a river and not a lake?

PROF. BOLT: A river carries things on, Zappy. People like to think of their dead being carried, not kept.

The river carries the dead on.

AT THE TEMPLE DOOR

Gaṅgā on the makara, Yamunā on the tortoise

Two guardians. From the Gupta age (about the fourth to sixth centuries) temple doorways in northern and central India carry two river goddesses: Gaṅgā standing on a makara, a crocodile-like water beast with a curling tail, and Yamunā on a tortoise. Each usually holds a water pot, often with an attendant holding a parasol.

Why at the door. Before a devotee sees the god, he passes between the two holiest rivers of the north. Museums and art historians read them as purifying everyone who enters, as if each visitor took a dip at the threshold. (Interpretation.)

Early and later. At Udayagiri near Vidiśā, the pair flank the great Varāha panel, carved about 400–425 CE. A fifth-century Yamunā from Ahicchatra, now in the National Museum, Delhi, raises her pot beside a parasol-bearer. In the earliest doorways the goddesses sit at the top corners; later they move down to the foot of the jambs.

Mostly a northern sign. The motif belongs above all to the Nāgara temples of the north, whose lifeline was the Gaṅgā–Yamunā plain, and it appears in Odisha’s temples too. For how to read the rest of a temple door, see How to Read a Hindu Temple · 12 articles.

ZAPPY: How do I tell them apart?

PROF. BOLT: Look down, Zappy. Crocodile tail, Gaṅgā. Tortoise, Yamunā.

Two rivers guard the door.

THE RIVER AT HOME

Kalaśa, conch and ācamana

The kalaśa. Every formal pūjā begins by making ordinary water holy. A pot of water, the kalaśa, is filled, topped with mango leaves and a coconut, and the seven rivers are called into it with the bath verse. From then on that water stands for every tīrtha in the land.

The conch and the sprinkle. In the five-deity worship described by Monier-Williams in 1883, the same invocation is said over the water before the conch is worshipped. Water from the kalaśa or the conch is then sprinkled (prokṣaṇa) on the offerings, the place and the people.

Sips of water. Ācamana, three small sips of water from the palm with names of Viṣṇu, opens most rites. It is the shortest pilgrimage there is: the river enters the body itself.

A pot of Gaṅgā. Many homes keep a sealed copper pot of Gaṅgā water, brought by a pilgrim relative, for purifications and for the last sips given to the dying. In Kodagu, a pot of Kāverī water from Talakāverī serves the same purpose; see Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa. More on home worship in Home Pūjā.

ZAPPY: Is the water in our kalaśa really the Gaṅgā?

PROF. BOLT: It is your well water with an invitation, Zappy. Whether she comes is a matter of faith; the texts say she does.

Every pūjā begins with rivers.

RIVERS OF KARNATAKA

Saṅgamas, ghāṭs and a Mysuru saying

The Kāverī. She rises at Talakāverī in Kodagu, gives the state its oldest temple towns on her islands and banks, and meets the Kapilā at Tirumakūḍalu, the “holy three-way meeting”. See T. Narasipura and the Triraṅga · 4 articles; Kāverī gives her own story.

Tuṅgā and Bhadrā. Two sisters rising together in the Western Ghāṭs, they meet near Shivamogga as the Tuṅgabhadrā and flow past Hampi. Śṛṅgeri stands on the Tuṅgā, where the Jagadguru performs Gaṅgā pūjā on the river bank at the solar turning of Dakṣiṇāyana. The Kannada saying runs Tuṅgā pāna, Gaṅgā snāna: drink the Tuṅgā, bathe in the Gaṅgā.

The Kṛṣṇā’s country. In the north the Kṛṣṇā and its tributaries hold the great saṅgamas: Kūḍala Saṅgama, where the Malaprabhā joins it and where Basavaṇṇa’s life turned (see Basavaṇṇa).

On the coast. The Netrāvatī and Kumāradhārā come down past Dharmasthaḷa and Kukke and meet at Uppinaṅgaḍi, called the Kāśī of the South. Every confluence in the state is a small Prayāga to the people who live there.

ZAPPY: So Mysuru has its own Prayāga?

PROF. BOLT: Several, Zappy. Tirumakūḍalu is the one people mean, with its hidden third stream.

Karnataka is full of meetings.

IN THE VERSE

Yathā nadyaḥ

MUṆḌAKA 3.2.8 · INTO THE OCEAN
yathā nadyaḥ syandamānāḥ samudre’staṃ gacchanti nāmarūpe vihāya |tathā vidvān nāmarūpād vimuktaḥparāt paraṃ puruṣam upaiti divyam ||
As flowing rivers disappear into the ocean, giving up name and form, so the knower, freed from name and form, reaches the divine Person, higher than the highest.
WordMeaning

The end of every river. The bath honours each river as a goddess; the Upaniṣad looks at where they all go. In the sea there is no Gaṅgā, only water, and Śaṅkara names her among the rivers lost there. In the Gītā Kṛṣṇa is the Jāhnavī among streams (10.31), and the steady person is the full ocean that rivers enter without making it overflow (2.70).

WHAT THE RIVERS TEACH

Readings old and new

Purity that moves. Rivers cleanse because they flow. Many teachers read this as a lesson: what keeps moving stays clean, in water and in the mind. (Interpretation.)

Many and one. The bath honours seven separate rivers; the Upaniṣad says all of them end in one sea. Devotion and knowledge are two ways of looking at the same water, and the tradition keeps both. (Interpretation.)

A mother who can be hurt. Calling a river a mother has never stopped people from dirtying her. On 20 March 2017 the Uttarakhand High Court declared the Gaṅgā and Yamunā living legal persons; the Supreme Court stayed that order on 7 July 2017. Whatever the law says, the old manners of the bath, no waste into the water, say the same thing.

A thread across India. A Kannadiga calling the Narmadā, a Bengali calling the Kāverī, a Tamil calling the Sindhu: the bath verse ties each person to rivers they may never see. Few prayers carry a whole country in them so plainly.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for a river?

PROF. BOLT: Treat her like your own mother’s kitchen, Zappy. You would not throw rubbish in there either.

Honour the river by keeping her clean.
