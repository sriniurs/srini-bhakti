Yamunā

Daughter of the Sun, Sister of Death · explained and illustrated

Daughter of the Sun

HOW THIS WORKS: The dark river of the north: her names and birth from the Sun, the Vedic twins, Yama Dvitīyā, Yamunotrī, the Kṛṣṇa stories from the night crossing to Kālindī’s wedding and Balarāma’s plough, and Vallabhācārya’s Yamunāṣṭaka.

WHERE WE ARE

The Yamunā is the dark river of the north. She rises below the Sun’s own mountain, runs past the old Pāṇḍava capital and through Kṛṣṇa’s Mathurā and Vṛndāvana, and gives herself to the Gaṅgā at Prayāga. Her father is the Sun and her twin brother is Yama, lord of death, so her story begins in the sky and touches the end of every life.

This part tells all of it: her names, her birth, the Vedic twins, the day Yama came to dinner, her source at Yamunotrī and her course to the plains, then the Kṛṣṇa stories in order (the night crossing, the poisoned pool, the moonlit banks, the bride in the river and Balarāma’s plough), Vallabhācārya’s hymn to her, her image, and how she reaches Karnataka.

The idea of the sacred river, the bath verse and the Puṣkara are in Part 1. Her elder sister in that verse has her own guide: Gaṅgā.

ZAPPY: A tortoise? Isn’t that slow for a river?

PROF. BOLT: Slow and steady, Zappy, like a river on the plains. The texts don’t say why; that reading is just a nice guess.

The Sun’s dark daughter.

HER NAMES

Twin, Sun’s daughter, dark one

Yamī and Yamunā. In the Veda she is Yamī, the female twin of Yama. A popular reading takes both names from yama or yamala, “twin”, “paired”. A Purāṇic naming story is told on the next page. (The etymology is traditional.)

Kālindī. Her later name means “of Kalinda”. Kalinda is the mountain where she rises, named after her father, since Kalinda is also a name of the Sun. Poets also hear kāla, “dark” or “time”, in it, a fair pun for a dark river and the sister of death.

The Sun’s girl. She is Sūryatanayā, Sūryajā and Ravinandinī: daughter or joy of the Sun. In the hymns of Vallabhācārya she is Sūrasutā, “child of the Sun”, and Kalinda-nandinī.

The dark one. She is also Asitā, “not white”. The Vāmana Purāṇa says her clear water turned black when Śiva, mad with grief for Satī, plunged into it. Another telling blames the poison of Kāliya; devotees simply say she took Kṛṣṇa’s colour.

ZAPPY: So which story explains the colour?

PROF. BOLT: All three are told, Zappy. Traditions differ, and nobody in Mathurā minds.

Twin, daughter, dark one.

DAUGHTER OF THE SUN

Saṃjñā, the Shadow and the twins

The story

Told in the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa (ch. 77), the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (3.2), the Harivaṃśa and others; details differ from book to book.

A bride who could not look. Saṃjñā, daughter of the divine craftsman Viśvakarman, married Sūrya. His blaze was too much for her and she kept closing her eyes. They had a son, Manu; then twins, Yama and Yamī. One story has the Sun naming the boy Yama for her shut eyes and the girl Yamunā for her flickering ones.
The Shadow. Unable to bear the heat any longer, Saṃjñā made a woman exactly like herself, Chāyā, “Shadow”, left her to care for the children and slipped away to her father, and then to the forest as a mare, to do penance.
A different mother. Chāyā bore the Sun children of her own, Śani (Saturn) and Sāvarṇi Manu, and began to favour them over Saṃjñā’s three.
The curse. Hurt, young Yama raised his foot at her. Chāyā cursed the foot. A real mother would not curse her child, Yama told his father, and so the secret came out. The Sun softened the curse and went looking for his wife.
Reunited. Viśvakarman trimmed the Sun’s blaze so that Saṃjñā could bear it; the Sun found her as a horse and from that union came the Aśvins. Yama became lord of the dead, and Yamī, many books say, came down to earth as the river Yamunā.

ZAPPY: So Śani is her half-brother?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, Zappy. Yama, Śani and the Yamunā are all children of the Sun. That is quite a family table.

Born of light she could not bear.

THE VEDIC TWINS

Yama, Yamī and the first night

The first mortals. In the Ṛgveda, Yama is the first man to die and so the first to find the road to the world of the fathers; he becomes their king. Yamī is his twin sister, and in later Vedic thought the pair are almost the first couple of creation.

The dialogue. Ṛgveda 10.10 is a conversation between the twins. Yamī asks Yama to continue the human line with her; Yama refuses, citing the law of the gods and the watchful eyes of Mitra and Varuṇa. Scholars read the hymn as a statement of the rule that brother and sister may not marry. (Interpretation.)

Why night was made. The Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā tells a tender story. When Yama died, Yamī grieved without end; every time the gods asked her to stop, she said, “He died only today.” Since there was only day, there was no “yesterday”. So the gods made night, a tomorrow came, and with time her grief eased.

From goddess to river. By the time of the epics and Purāṇas, Yamī has become the Yamunā on earth. The sister who could not stop grieving for death becomes the river that is said to free people from the fear of it.

ZAPPY: Night was made so she could stop crying?

PROF. BOLT: That is the story, Zappy. Time heals because there is a yesterday. The old seers knew it.

Night was made for her grief.

YAMA COMES TO DINNER

The second day of Kārtika

The story

Skanda and Padma Purāṇas, told from her side; the Dīpāvali setting is in the Dīpāvali guide.

A busy brother. Yamunā loved her twin and asked him again and again to visit her. Yama, with every life in the world to account for, never found the time.
He comes. On the second day of the bright half of Kārtika he came at last. The sister of death bathed him, put a tilaka on his forehead, garlanded him and fed him with her own hands.
The boon. Pleased, Yama asked what she wanted. She asked that on this day every year, a brother who eats from his sister’s hands should not fear death, and that whoever bathes in her water on this day should not be dragged to his city.
The day is named. Yama granted it. So the day is Yama Dvitīyā or Bhaginī-hasta-bhojana, “eating from a sister’s hand”, known in the north as Bhāī Dūj.
At her bank. Brothers and sisters bathe together in the Yamunā at Mathurā’s Viśrām Ghāṭ on this day, and lamps are floated on the water at dusk.

ZAPPY: Do brothers in Karnataka keep it?

PROF. BOLT: Many families do, Zappy, on the last day of Dīpāvali, even far from her water. The Dīpāvali guide has the details.

A sister’s meal beats death.

WHERE SHE RISES

Yamunotrī

Under the Sun’s mountain. The Yamunā rises from the Champasar glacier and a frozen lake on Kalinda Parvata, the mountain named for her father, below the Bandarpunch peaks of Garhwal. The true source is out of reach, so the shrine of Yamunotrī stands lower down, the westernmost of the four Himalayan dhāms.

Hot springs. Beside the temple the mountain pours out hot water. In the hottest spring, the Sūrya Kuṇḍa, pilgrims tie rice or potatoes in a cloth and cook them in the water to offer to the goddess and carry home as prasāda. Another spring, cooler, is for bathing.

The divine rock. Near the Sūrya Kuṇḍa a rock pillar, the Divya Śilā, is worshipped before the goddess herself. Tradition also places the hermitage of the sage Asita here.

The temple. The present temple dates from the nineteenth century, built under the rulers of Tehri Garhwal and rebuilt after snow and floods; some accounts credit a queen of Jaipur. Sources differ. The full temple guide is Yamunotrī.

ZAPPY: Rice cooked in a spring? Really?

PROF. BOLT: Really, Zappy. The Sun’s daughter cooks for her pilgrims with her father’s heat.

She rises by her father’s fire.

DOWN TO THE PLAINS

From the hills to Prayāga

Out of the hills. After Yamunotrī she cuts down through the Garhwal hills and comes out on the plains, where she and the Gaṅgā run side by side, a little apart, for hundreds of kilometres. The land between them is the Doāb, “two waters”, the heart of the old Madhyadeśa.

The Pāṇḍava city. On her bank stood Indraprastha, the Pāṇḍavas’ capital. The Mahābhārata tells how Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna, picnicking on the Yamunā, met Agni and helped him burn the Khāṇḍava forest; from that fire Maya the architect was saved and built the Pāṇḍavas’ famous hall.

Kṛṣṇa’s country. Downstream lie Mathurā, where he was born, and Gokula and Vṛndāvana, where he grew up. This stretch, Vraja, is the Yamunā every devotee pictures: ghāṭs, groves, cows and the sound of a flute.

Into her sister. At Prayāga she meets the Gaṅgā. Her dark green water and the Gaṅgā’s paler water run side by side for a while before mixing, and tradition adds the hidden Sarasvatī as a third. The Kumbh Melā is held at this meeting.

ZAPPY: Why does she give up her name at Prayāga?

PROF. BOLT: The bigger flow keeps the name, Zappy. But pilgrims say Prayāga is holy because of her as much as the Gaṅgā.

From the Sun’s hill to her sister.

THE NIGHT CROSSING

Vasudeva carries the child

The story

From the Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.3, with a detail beloved in popular tellings. The birth itself is told in the Janmāṣṭamī guide.

Midnight in Mathurā. Kṛṣṇa was born in Kaṃsa’s prison. The chains fell open, the guards slept, and his father Vasudeva lifted the baby in a basket to carry him to Gokula, across the river.
A river in flood. It was the rainy season and the Yamunā was in spate, deep and full of whirlpools. The serpent Śeṣa spread his hoods over the child to keep off the rain.
She makes way. The Bhāgavata says the river gave Vasudeva a path, as the ocean once gave way to Rāma. The first time the Yamunā met her Lord, she stepped aside for him.
The touch. Popular tellings add that the water rose higher and higher until it reached the basket, because the river wanted to touch the baby’s feet; when one small foot dipped in, the flood went down.
Safe on the far bank. Vasudeva reached Gokula, laid the boy beside Yaśodā and came back with her new-born daughter. In Karnataka homes, this crossing is acted out with a doll in a basket at Gōkulāṣṭamī.

ZAPPY: So the river wanted to see him first?

PROF. BOLT: That is how grandmothers tell it, Zappy. She was the first in Vraja to touch his feet.

She made way for him.

THE POISONED POOL

Kāliya leaves the Yamunā

The story

From the Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.16–17. The Kṛṣṇa guide tells it from his side; this is the river’s side.

A river poisoned. A great many-hooded serpent, Kāliya, had taken over a deep pool of the Yamunā near Vṛndāvana. His venom boiled the water; birds flying over fell, and cows and cowherd boys who drank there dropped dead.
Why he was there. Kāliya had fled his home at Ramaṇaka island for fear of Garuḍa, the serpent-eater. Garuḍa had been cursed never to enter this one pool, so Kāliya hid in the Yamunā.
Into the water. Kṛṣṇa climbed a kadamba tree on the bank and leapt in. The serpent wrapped him in his coils while the people of Vraja wept on the bank.
The dance. Kṛṣṇa slipped free, sprang onto Kāliya’s hoods and danced on them, crushing the serpent’s pride, until Kāliya vomited blood and his wives begged for his life.
The river cleaned. Kṛṣṇa spared him but sent him back to the sea. The Yamunā’s water became sweet again; some tellings say the dark colour that remains is the stain of the old poison.

ZAPPY: Is that why the river is dark?

PROF. BOLT: It is one of the three stories, Zappy. The poison left, the colour stayed.

The Lord cleans his river.

THE BANKS OF VRAJA

Moonlight, ghāṭs and the rest-ghāṭ

The river of play. In the Bhāgavata, almost everything Kṛṣṇa does as a boy happens near the Yamunā: herding cows on her banks, stealing butter in the village above, playing the flute in her groves. The great rāsa dance with the gopīs on an autumn full-moon night (10.29–33) is set on her sandy bank.

A river of longing. For poets the Yamunā became the river of love in separation. When Kṛṣṇa left for Mathurā, the gopīs remembered him by her water; Vaiṣṇava songs from Bengal to Karnataka make her the witness of that longing. (A theme of devotional poetry.)

The rest-ghāṭ. At Mathurā, Viśrām Ghāṭ marks where, tradition says, Kṛṣṇa rested after killing Kaṃsa. Pilgrims’ circuits of Mathurā begin and end here, and evening ārati is offered to the river with lamps floated on the water. One Purāṇic account links the resting to Vasudeva instead; traditions differ.

Ghāṭs of Vṛndāvana. Upstream, Vṛndāvana’s ghāṭs carry names from the stories, such as Keśī Ghāṭ, where Kṛṣṇa is said to have killed the horse-demon Keśī. Every ghāṭ is a story with steps.

ZAPPY: Why is there always a full moon in these pictures?

PROF. BOLT: Because the rāsa was on a full-moon night, Zappy, and because a river looks best by moonlight.

Every ghāṭ is a story.

THE BRIDE IN THE RIVER

Kālindī finds Kṛṣṇa

The story

From the Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.58.

A hunt by the river. While staying with the Pāṇḍavas at Indraprastha, Kṛṣṇa went hunting with Arjuna. Tired and thirsty, the two bathed in the Yamunā and drank her clear water.
A girl alone. They saw a beautiful young woman walking by the river. At Kṛṣṇa’s request Arjuna asked who she was.
Her vow. She said she was Kālindī, daughter of the Sun, doing hard penance to win Viṣṇu as her husband. “I will have no one else,” she said, “and I live in a house my father built for me under the Yamunā’s water until I see him.”
He takes her. Arjuna told Kṛṣṇa, who already knew. He lifted her onto his chariot and took her to Yudhiṣṭhira, then to Dvārakā.
The wedding. At Dvārakā Kṛṣṇa married her on an auspicious day, and she became one of his eight chief queens, the aṣṭa-bhāryā. The Bhāgavata names ten sons of theirs.

ZAPPY: So the river married Kṛṣṇa?

PROF. BOLT: The river goddess did, Zappy. The river herself kept flowing past Mathurā, so she got to be with him twice.

The river chose her husband.

BALARĀMA’S PLOUGH

The river that would not come

The story

From the Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.65; the Viṣṇu Purāṇa tells it too.

Back in Vraja. Long after Kṛṣṇa left for Mathurā, his elder brother Balarāma came back to Gokula. He stayed two months, the spring months of Caitra and Vaiśākha, comforting Nanda, Yaśodā and the gopīs with news of Kṛṣṇa.
A moonlit grove. One night, merry from a drink of honey-wine (vāruṇī) and wandering in the groves with the gopīs, he called the Yamunā to come to him so they could play in her water.
She refuses. The river, thinking him drunk, paid no attention.
The plough. Angry, Balarāma hooked her with the tip of his plough and dragged her towards him, threatening to split her into a hundred streams.
She bows. Frightened, the Yamunā came in person, fell at his feet and asked forgiveness. He let her go. The Bhāgavata adds that the channels he dragged her into can still be seen (10.65.33).

ZAPPY: Why would a god drag a river?

PROF. BOLT: Some scholars see in it a memory of farmers cutting irrigation channels, Zappy. The plough is Balarāma’s sign, after all. (Interpretation.)

The plough moved the river.

IN THE VERSE

Namāmi yamunām aham

YAMUNĀṢṬAKA 1 · VALLABHĀCĀRYA
namāmi yamunām ahaṃ sakala-siddhi-hetuṃ mudāmurāri-pada-paṅkaja-sphurad-amanda-reṇūtkaṭām |taṭa-stha-nava-kānana-prakaṭa-moda-puṣpāmbunāsurāsura-supūjita-smara-pituḥ śriyaṃ bibhratīm ||1||
With joy I bow to the Yamunā, the cause of every attainment, thick with the bright sand of Murāri’s lotus feet, carrying flowers fragrant from the young groves on her banks, holding the beauty of Kṛṣṇa, worshipped by gods and demons alike.

Sand, not water. The first thing Vallabhācārya praises is not the water but the sand on her banks, because Kṛṣṇa walked on it. She is holy because she held his footprints; to a lover even that dust is precious. “Father of Smara” names Kṛṣṇa, since Kāma was reborn as his son Pradyumna.

Text as in the Yamunāṣṭaka of Vallabhācārya, Ṣoḍaśa Grantha (sanskritdocuments.org “yamunāṣṭakam 3”; bhaktibharat.com), compared word by word; transliterated into IAST.
Holy because he walked here.

IN THE VERSE

Tavāṣṭakam idaṃ mudā

YAMUNĀṢṬAKA 9 · THE FRUIT
tavāṣṭakam idaṃ mudā paṭhati sūrasūte sadāsamasta-durita-kṣayo bhavati vai mukunde ratiḥ |tayā sakala-siddhayo muraripuś ca santuṣyatisvabhāva-vijayo bhaved vadati vallabhaḥ śrī-hareḥ ||9||
O daughter of the Sun, whoever joyfully recites this eightfold praise of yours every day loses all wrongs and gains love for Mukunda; through that love every attainment comes, Murāri is pleased, and one’s own nature is conquered. So says Vallabha, Śrī Hari’s own.
WordMeaning

The real gift. Most hymns promise merit or wealth. This one promises love for Kṛṣṇa, and through it victory over one’s own nature. In Vallabha’s teaching the Yamunā’s special grace is not cleansing alone but the gift of devotion itself.

Same edition as the previous verse. Verse 9 is the closing verse (phalaśruti) of the hymn.
She gives love for Kṛṣṇa.

VALLABHA’S RIVER

The Yamunā in the Puṣṭimārga

The teacher. Vallabhācārya (traditionally 1479–1531), born of a Telugu brāhmaṇa family, founded the Puṣṭimārga, the “path of grace”, centred on Kṛṣṇa as the child and lover of Vraja. Its main seat is Nāthdvārā in Rajasthan, home of Śrī Nāthjī.

First of sixteen. His Yamunāṣṭaka is the first of his sixteen short works, the Ṣoḍaśa Grantha. Members of the sampradāya recite it daily, and the Yamunā is honoured in their shrines as Yamunājī, a goddess in her own right.

Above Gaṅgā? In this tradition the Yamunā’s grace is the higher one: Gaṅgā purifies, but the Yamunā gives love for Kṛṣṇa. A verse of the hymn even says the Gaṅgā became dear to Kṛṣṇa only by meeting the Yamunā. Other traditions rank them differently; this is a devotional reading, not a ruling.

Another hymn. A second Yamunāṣṭakam, opening with the colour of Murāri’s body, is ascribed to Śaṅkara. The two show the river loved by both the devotional and the Advaita traditions.

ZAPPY: Why does a river need a teacher?

PROF. BOLT: She doesn’t, Zappy. People do. Vallabha taught them how to see her.

The river that gives devotion.

HER IMAGE

Dark, on a tortoise, with a pot

What the texts say. The Agni Purāṇa describes her as dark-skinned, standing on a tortoise and holding a water pot. That is the image carved by temple doors: a graceful woman, often with a lotus or a fly-whisk, and a small attendant holding a parasol over her.

At the door. From the Gupta age she stands on one side of the temple door and Gaṅgā on the other, the pair purifying all who enter, as Part 1 explains. A fifth-century Yamunā from Ahicchatra, now in the National Museum, New Delhi, is one of the finest.

Why a tortoise? The texts give no reason. Some writers point to the slow, steady flow of a plains river; others to the tortoise’s place in the water world beside Gaṅgā’s makara. Both are readings, not scripture. (Interpretation.)

In Vraja shrines. In Puṣṭimārga homes and temples she is shown simply as a dark, smiling goddess, sometimes as the river itself painted behind Kṛṣṇa, with lotuses and a strip of blue water.

ZAPPY: Is she always dark?

PROF. BOLT: In sculpture the stone decides, Zappy. In texts and paintings, yes, dark like her river.

Dark, calm, on a tortoise.

THE YAMUNĀ IN KARNATAKA

A northern river in southern homes

In every bath. The Yamunā is far from Karnataka, but she is named second in the bath verse said in homes across the state. Every pot of pūjā water in Mysuru invites her, by name, before the Kāverī.

Gōkulāṣṭamī. On Kṛṣṇa’s birthday Karnataka homes draw small white footprints from the door to the shrine and act out the night crossing with a doll in a basket; see the Janmāṣṭamī guide. At Uḍupi, whose Kṛṣṇa is said to have come by ship from Dvārakā, the Yamunā stories are part of every festival sermon; see Uḍupi Śrīkṛṣṇa.

Songs of the Dāsas. The Kannada Haridāsas, Purandara Dāsa and Kanaka Dāsa among them, sang endlessly of the child Kṛṣṇa of Gokula and Vṛndāvana, his butter, his flute and his gopīs, so that the world on her banks became familiar to Kannada ears.

A Vṛndāvana on the Kāverī. Even the great garden below the Kṛṣṇarājasāgara dam near Mysuru, laid out between 1927 and 1932, carries the name Brindāvan, after Kṛṣṇa’s Vṛndāvana on the Yamunā.

ZAPPY: So I can meet the Yamunā without leaving Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: In your bath verse and your Kṛṣṇa songs, Zappy. Her water stays in the north; her stories travel.

Her stories travel south.

WHAT THE YAMUNĀ TEACHES

Death’s sister, Kṛṣṇa’s river

Life beside death. She is the twin of the lord of death, yet her gift is freedom from the fear of death. Many read this as a lesson that love, the sister’s meal, is stronger than the end. (Interpretation.)

Love over law. The Gaṅgā is often praised for purity; the Yamunā for love. In the Vaiṣṇava reading her water does not only wash; it draws the heart to Kṛṣṇa. (Interpretation.)

Grief that heals. The oldest story about her is the night made for her mourning. Time passing, a “yesterday”, is itself a mercy; the river that keeps moving is a picture of it.

A river under strain. Below Delhi the Yamunā carries much of a great city’s waste, and keeping her clean is a long public struggle. For devotees who call her Yamunājī, that is not only an environmental matter.

ZAPPY: If I could remember one thing about her?

PROF. BOLT: That she asked her brother for one thing, Zappy: that brothers and sisters should not fear death. She asked for others, not for herself.

Love stronger than death.

👑 Aṣṭa-bhāryā

Kṛṣṇa’s eight chief queens; Kālindī is one.

ZAPPY: Which word sums her up?

PROF. BOLT: Kālindī, Zappy: the Sun’s mountain, the dark water and the Kṛṣṇa bride, all in one name.

Ten words for the dark river.

ON ONE PAGE

The whole article in five lines
Who. Daughter of the Sun and Saṃjñā, twin of Yama; Yamī in the Veda, Kālindī later.
Where. From Yamunotrī below Kalinda mountain, past Indraprastha and Vraja, into the Gaṅgā at Prayāga.
Stories. The Shadow-mother, the first night, Yama’s dinner, the crossing, Kāliya, the rāsa, Kālindī’s wedding, Balarāma’s plough.
Worship. Yama Dvitīyā, Viśrām Ghāṭ, the Yamunāṣṭaka of Vallabha, her image on a tortoise.
Meaning. The sister of death who gives freedom from fear; the river that gives love for Kṛṣṇa.

ZAPPY: What’s next?

PROF. BOLT: Part 3 is the Godāvarī: the Gaṅgā of the South, brought down by the sage Gautama on behalf of a cow.
