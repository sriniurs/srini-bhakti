SEVEN MOTHERS · SEVEN POWERS · ONE GODDESS

Sapta Mātṛkās

The Seven Mothers · explained and illustrated

The Seven Mothers on a temple lintel

HOW THIS WORKS: Who the Saptamātṛkās are: each one’s god, mount and weapons, how they rose from the gods to fight Raktabīja in the Devī Māhātmya and Andhaka in the Purāṇas, how they appear on temple walls, their village and Tantric worship, and the Chalukya kings who called themselves their nurslings.

WHERE WE ARE

On the lintels of old temples, in caves at Ellora and in roadside shrines of seven red stones, you meet a row of seven goddesses: the Saptamātṛkās, the Seven Mothers. Each is the power (śakti) of a great god, dressed in his ornaments, carrying his weapons and riding his mount.

This guide introduces each mother, tells their two great battle stories, explains how to recognise them in stone, and follows them from Kushan sculpture and Chalukya inscriptions to village worship and Tantra. Their battle is told in full in our Devī Māhātmya series; Vārāhī has her own Śrīvidyā guide, Vārāhī.

MEET THE MOTHERS (1)

Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī

Iconographic details vary by text and region.

🦢 Brāhmī (Brahmāṇī)

Power of Brahmā: yellow, often four-headed, with rosary and water-pot, on a swan. She is creation’s word.

🐂 Māheśvarī

Power of Śiva: white, three-eyed, with trident and drum and the crescent moon, on Nandi.

🦚 Kaumārī

Power of Kumāra (Skanda): youthful, with spear or bow, on a peacock.

🦅 Vaiṣṇavī

Power of Viṣṇu: dark, with conch, discus, mace and lotus, on Garuḍa.

ZAPPY: They look like their gods!

PROF. BOLT: That’s the idea, Zappy: the god’s power stepping out in a woman’s form.

The gods’ powers step out.

MEET THE MOTHERS (2)

Vārāhī, Indrāṇī, Cāmuṇḍā, Nārasiṃhī

🐗 Vārāhī

Power of Varāha, the boar: a sow’s face, with rod or plough, on a buffalo. In Śrīvidyā she commands the Devī’s armies.

🐘 Indrāṇī (Aindrī)

Power of Indra: thousand-eyed, with the thunderbolt (vajra), on the elephant Airāvata.

💀 Cāmuṇḍā

Power of the Devī herself: gaunt, fierce, with a garland of heads, on a corpse or jackal. She slew Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, hence her name.

🦁 Nārasiṃhī

Power of Narasiṃha: a lion’s face, on a lion. She makes the seven into eight in many lists.

ZAPPY: Cāmuṇḍā like Chāmuṇḍi Hill?

PROF. BOLT: The same name, Zappy. Mysuru’s Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is the slayer of Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa. See Chāmuṇḍeśvarī.

Fierce mothers protect.

THE BATTLE WITH RAKTABĪJA

When every drop became a demon

The story

Devī Māhātmya, chapter 8; told in our Devī Māhātmya series.

The Devī fought the armies of the demons Śumbha and Niśumbha. To help her, the śaktis of the gods came out of their bodies, each in her god’s form, with his weapons and mount.
The demon Raktabīja, “blood-seed”, had a boon: every drop of his blood that touched the ground became another Raktabīja.
The more the Mothers wounded him, the more demons sprang up, until the battlefield filled with copies.
The Devī told Kālī, called Cāmuṇḍā here, to open her mouth wide and drink every drop before it fell.
Bloodless, Raktabīja fell at last. The Mothers danced, drunk with victory, and the gods rejoiced.

ZAPPY: A demon who multiplies when hurt?

PROF. BOLT: Like anger, Zappy: fight it the wrong way and it spreads. Cāmuṇḍā swallows it whole.

Stop it before it spreads.

ŚIVA AND ANDHAKA

The Mothers’ other origin

The story

Matsya Purāṇa; Śaiva Purāṇic tellings differ.

In a Śaiva story, the demon Andhaka had the same power as Raktabīja: his blood created new Andhakas wherever it fell.
Śiva created seven Mothers to drink the demon’s blood, and with their help he won.
Victorious, the Mothers grew hungry and began to devour the world itself.
Śiva could not restrain them; Narasiṃha created thirty-two gentle goddesses who calmed them.
The Mothers were told to protect the world rather than destroy it, and to be worshipped as its guardians.

ZAPPY: Even the Mothers had to be calmed?

PROF. BOLT: Power without restraint becomes danger, Zappy. The story says so even about goddesses.

Power turned to protection.

HOW TO READ THEM IN STONE

The temple panel

The row. The seven usually sit or stand in a fixed order on a single slab, often over a doorway or in a separate shrine facing the temple, each with her mount carved below.

The guardians at the ends. Gaṇeśa closes the row at one end; Vīrabhadra, Śiva’s fierce form, often at the other, sometimes playing the vīṇā.

Mothers with children. Many early images show each Mother holding a child on her lap, a reminder of their origin as goddesses of birth and childhood.

Where to look. The Śaiva caves at Ellora (Caves 14, 16, 21, 22), Elephanta and Udayagiri; in Karnataka, many Chalukya and Hoysaḷa temples carry Mātṛkā panels.

ZAPPY: Why Gaṇeśa at the end?

PROF. BOLT: He guards beginnings and thresholds, Zappy, and he’s a mother’s son.

A row of guardian mothers.

OLDER THAN THE STORIES

Mothers of birth and childhood

Kushan beginnings. Stone images of the Mothers first appear in the Kushan period (first to third centuries CE), especially around Mathurā.

Mothers who could harm. Scholars trace them to goddesses linked with birth and childhood illness, who had to be pleased to protect children. The same ambivalence survives: the Mothers protect and can punish.

Village goddesses. Many scholars think the Mātṛkās began as local village goddesses absorbed into Purāṇic worship. Shrines of seven vermilion-smeared stones, often by water, are still common.

The seven sisters. In South India they are worshipped as the Saptakanyās, seven maidens, and as groups of sister goddesses in villages. Women also worship them on the new-moon day called Piṭhorī.

ZAPPY: Seven red stones by a pond?

PROF. BOLT: Quite possibly the oldest form of the Mothers, Zappy. Before the stories came the stones.

Stones before stories.

NURSED BY THE MOTHERS

The Kadambas and the Chalukyas

Royal preambles. The Kadamba and early Chalukya kings of Karnataka opened their inscriptions by praising the Mātṛkās as givers of strength to defeat enemies.

Nursed by the Seven. The Chalukyas of Bādāmi described themselves as having been nurtured by the Seven Mothers, alongside Kārttikeya’s protection and the boar emblem of Viṣṇu-Varāha.

Why kings cared. Warriors sought fierce protective goddesses; Vārāhī and Kaumārī, powers of the boar and the war-god, suited a martial dynasty. (Interpretation.)

In Karnataka today. Mātṛkā panels on Chalukya and Hoysaḷa temples, and many village goddess shrines with seven sisters, keep the memory.

ZAPPY: Kings said goddesses nursed them?

PROF. BOLT: In their own grand inscriptions, Zappy. Karnataka’s early kings put the Mothers first.

Karnataka’s kings called them mother.

LETTERS AND DIRECTIONS

Tantra and the Aṣṭamātṛkās

Mātṛkā as letters. In Tantra, mātṛkā also means the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, the “little mothers” of all words; mātṛkā-nyāsa places these letter-goddesses on the body.

In the Śrīcakra. Eight Mātṛkās guard a line of the outer square of the Śrīcakra; see Trailokyamohana, the first enclosure.

Eight directions. In Nepal the Aṣṭamātṛkās (with Mahālakṣmī as eighth) are the Ajimā, grandmother goddesses, guarding the eight directions of each Newar city; Bhaktapur adds a ninth for the centre and parades their images each year.

Initiatory worship. Their Tantric mantras belong to initiated practice and are not given here.

ZAPPY: Letters are mothers?

PROF. BOLT: Every word is born from them, Zappy. That’s the Tantric idea.

Mothers of every word.

WORSHIPPING THE MOTHERS

Temples, Navarātri and the village

Temples. Sapta-mātṛkā shrines stand in many Śiva and Devī temple complexes; Jājpur in Odisha, on the Vaitaraṇī, is famous for its seven Mothers.

Navarātri. The Mothers are honoured during Navarātri, when the Devī’s battles are recited; in Nepal’s Patan a royal order of 1667 began their worship in Āśvina Navarātri, still kept.

Village. Seven-sister goddesses receive annual festivals with processions of decorated pots, especially in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.

Protection. Families still pray to them for children’s health, the oldest of their roles.

ZAPPY: Should I be afraid of them?

PROF. BOLT: Respectful, Zappy, not afraid. They’re mothers. Fierce for you, not against you.

Fierce for their children.

WHAT THEY TEACH

The many powers of one

One power, many forms. The Devī Māhātmya says the Mothers came from the gods, but in the end the Devī takes them all back into herself: “I am alone in the world; who else is there?” (Interpretation of chapter 10.)

Power belongs to the feminine. Each god’s strength, his śakti, is shown as a goddess: without her, he cannot act. That is the Śākta reading.

Ambivalence held. Mothers protect and punish; the tradition does not hide the fierce side of care.

Stopping evil at the source. Raktabīja teaches that some harms must be stopped before they multiply.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for the Mothers?

PROF. BOLT: Thank seven women who protected you, Zappy. That’s their oldest worship.

Seven mothers, one care.
