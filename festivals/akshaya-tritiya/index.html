Akṣaya Tṛtīyā

The Day That Never Diminishes · explained and illustrated

Gifts that never run out

HOW THIS WORKS: What akṣaya means, Paraśurāma’s birth, the stories of the day, the temples, Basava Jayantī, and a frank word on gold.

WHERE WE ARE

On the third day of the bright half of Vaiśākha falls Akṣaya Tṛtīyā, “the third day that never diminishes”. Whatever good is begun or given on this day, tradition says, does not decay.

It gathers many stories: Paraśurāma’s birth, the Gaṅgā’s descent, the inexhaustible bowl, Kṛṣṇa and Sudāmā, the opening of Badrīnāth. In Karnataka it is also Basava Jayantī. This guide tells them, and says frankly where modern custom has added to tradition.

THE NAME

What never diminishes

Akṣaya. Kṣaya is decay or loss; akṣaya is what does not decay. The day is held to make good deeds, gifts and prayers “inexhaustible” in their fruit.

A self-auspicious day. It is one of the few days counted auspicious all day long, so no particular hour needs to be chosen for new beginnings.

Giving. The texts that praise it speak above all of giving: water pots, food, umbrellas, sandals and fans in the coming heat.

Tretā Yuga. Some traditions say the Tretā age began on this day.

ZAPPY: So it’s a lucky day?

PROF. BOLT: More a fruitful day: what you sow, especially in giving, keeps growing.

Akṣaya: good that never runs out.

PARAŚURĀMA JAYANTĪ

Viṣṇu’s sixth avatāra

The story

Many celebrate Akṣaya Tṛtīyā as the birthday of Paraśurāma.

Paraśurāma, “Rāma with the axe”, was born to the sage Jamadagni and Reṇukā.
He pleased Śiva by penance and received the divine axe (paraśu).
When the arrogant king Kārtavīrya’s sons killed his father, he fought the corrupt warrior kings again and again.
He later gave away the earth he had won and retired to the Western Ghats; tradition says he reclaimed the coast of Kerala and Karnataka from the sea.
He is a cirañjīvin, said to live on; he teaches Bhīṣma, Droṇa and Karṇa in the Mahābhārata. Our Daśāvatāra series · 10 avatāras + overview tells his story fully.

ZAPPY: He made the west coast?

PROF. BOLT: So the coastal tradition says: he threw his axe into the sea and the land rose. Gokarṇa and Udupi remember him.

Akṣaya Tṛtīyā: Paraśurāma’s birthday.

OTHER STORIES OF THE DAY

Gaṅgā, the bowl, Sudāmā and Kubera

🌊 The Gaṅgā descends

Some traditions say the Gaṅgā came down to earth on this day, brought by Bhagīratha’s penance.

🍚 The akṣaya pātra

In the Mahābhārata the exiled Pāṇḍavas received an inexhaustible vessel from the Sun, which fed all their guests each day until Draupadī had eaten. Popular tradition places the gift on this day.

🤝 Sudāmā

Kṛṣṇa’s poor childhood friend Sudāmā brought him a handful of flattened rice; Kṛṣṇa received it with love and Sudāmā returned home to find his hut a palace. Many tell this story today.

💰 Kubera and Lakṣmī

Some traditions say Kubera received his wealth by worshipping Śiva and Lakṣmī on this day, which is part of how the day became tied to wealth.

ZAPPY: So many stories, all about giving?

PROF. BOLT: Look closely: the inexhaustible bowl, a handful of rice, the river given to earth. Every one is about a gift that never runs out.

A river, a bowl, a handful of rice: gifts that never run out.

THE DAY IN TEMPLES

Badrīnāth and Siṃhācalam

Badrīnāth. The great Viṣṇu shrine in the Himālaya, closed through winter, traditionally reopens around this time; the dates are announced each year. See our Char Dhām series · 8 articles.

Siṃhācalam. At the Narasiṃha temple of Siṃhācalam in Andhra, the deity is covered in sandalwood paste all year; on Akṣaya Tṛtīyā the paste is removed for the nija-rūpa darśan, the true form, then reapplied.

Purī. At Purī, construction of the chariots for the Ratha Yātrā begins around this day.

Everywhere. Temples hold special worship, and many begin new festivals.

ZAPPY: The deity is covered in sandalwood all year?

PROF. BOLT: At Siṃhācalam, yes, to cool the fierce Narasiṃha. Seeing him uncovered is the great event of the day.

Doors open, sandalwood removed: temples on Akṣaya Tṛtīyā.

BASAVA JAYANTĪ

Karnataka’s own

Basavaṇṇa. In Karnataka the day is also Basava Jayantī, birthday of Basavaṇṇa (12th century), the poet-saint and statesman of Kalyāṇa who founded the Vīraśaiva-Liṅgāyata movement.

His teaching. Through his short Kannada poems (vacanas) he taught devotion to Śiva, honest work (kāyaka) and the equality of all, rejecting caste and empty ritual.

Anubhava Maṇṭapa. His hall of experience gathered saints of every background, men and women.

The day. Liṅgāyat communities celebrate with processions, readings of vacanas and community meals.

ZAPPY: A saint who wrote short poems?

PROF. BOLT: Hundreds of vacanas, still sung. “Work is worship” (kāyakavē kailāsa) is his most famous line.

In Karnataka: Basavaṇṇa’s birthday.

THE GOLD QUESTION

Old tradition and new custom

Frankly. Buying gold on Akṣaya Tṛtīyā is now very popular and heavily advertised. It is a recent custom; the older texts praise giving, not buying.

The logic. If what is begun today never diminishes, people reasoned, so will wealth bought today. Kubera’s story helped.

Tradition’s own emphasis. Gifts of water, food, clothes and shelter in the summer heat, worship, and good beginnings.

A balance. There is nothing wrong with marking the day with a purchase; but by the tradition’s own logic, a gift to someone in need is the truer Akṣaya act.

ZAPPY: So should I buy gold or not?

PROF. BOLT: That’s your call. The tradition’s answer is: give something. That is what never diminishes.

The tradition says give; the market says buy.

WHAT TO GIVE ON AKṢAYA TṚTĪYĀ

The traditional gifts

Summer gifts. Coming in the heat of Vaiśākha, the day’s traditional gifts are things that relieve heat: water pots, umbrellas, sandals, fans, and cool drinks.

Food and grain. Rice, wheat, sugar and fruit are given.

Water stalls. Setting up a water stall (prapā) for travellers is praised.

Today. Water for birds and animals in summer is a fine modern form.

ZAPPY: An umbrella is a holy gift?

PROF. BOLT: In May? The most practical holy gift there is.

Gifts against the heat: water, shade, cool air.

NEW BEGINNINGS ON THE DAY

Why people start things

All day auspicious. As a svayaṃsiddha muhūrta, “self-auspicious time”, the whole day is good for beginnings without consulting the almanac for an hour.

Weddings. In some regions many weddings are held on Akṣaya Tṛtīyā.

Farming. Farmers in some regions begin the season’s first ploughing.

Learning. Some begin a new study or practice.

ZAPPY: No need for an astrologer that day?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the point of a self-auspicious day. The whole day is a good hour.

A whole day good for beginning.
