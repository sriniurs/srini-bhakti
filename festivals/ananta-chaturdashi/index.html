Ananta Vrata

Ananta Caturdaśī: Fourteen Knots · explained and illustrated

Fourteen knots of the infinite

HOW THIS WORKS: Ananta Caturdaśī, the fourteenth day of Bhādrapada: worship of Viṣṇu as Ananta, the Endless, resting on the serpent Śeṣa, the fourteen-knotted thread, the story of Suśīlā and Kauṇḍinya, the day of Gaṇeśa’s farewell immersion, and the Jain day of forgiveness that follows.

WHERE WE ARE

Ten days after Gaṇeśa Caturthī, crowds carry Gaṇeśa to the water for his farewell. That same day, in many homes, a quieter worship takes place: of Viṣṇu as Ananta, “the Endless”, with a red thread of fourteen knots tied on the arm.

This is Ananta Caturdaśī. This guide explains the vow, its story, and why one day carries both Viṣṇu’s infinity and Gaṇeśa’s goodbye.

WHO IS ANANTA?

The endless one

The word. Ananta means “without end”, infinite. It is a name of Viṣṇu (in the Sahasranāma) and of the serpent Śeṣa, on whom he rests.

Śeṣa. The thousand-headed serpent floats on the cosmic ocean; Viṣṇu sleeps on his coils between creations. Śeṣa means “what remains”: what is left when everything else dissolves.

Avatāras of Śeṣa. Lakṣmaṇa and Balarāma are held to be Śeṣa incarnate; Rāmānuja and Patañjali are also linked with him by tradition.

On this day. Viṣṇu is worshipped in this reclining form, as at Padmanābhasvāmi and Śrīraṅgam.

SUŚĪLĀ AND KAUṆḌINYA

The thread that was burned

The story

Told by Kṛṣṇa to Yudhiṣṭhira in the vrata texts (and in tellings linked to the Mahābhārata).

Suśīlā, a good-natured young wife, saw women by a river worshipping Ananta and joined them. She tied the fourteen-knotted thread on her arm.
Her household prospered. Her husband, the sage Kauṇḍinya, saw the thread and grew angry: “Our wealth is my doing, not some thread!” He tore it off and threw it into the fire.
Poverty followed. Realising his pride, Kauṇḍinya set off into the forest, asking trees, cows and lakes where Ananta was.
At last, near collapse, he was met by an old brahmin who led him to a palace and revealed himself as Viṣṇu, Ananta.
Kauṇḍinya was told to keep the vow for fourteen years. He did, and his fortunes returned.

ZAPPY: He thought he did it all himself.

PROF. BOLT: A very human mistake, Zappy. The thread was a reminder of grace.

THE ANANTA VRATA

How it is kept

🐍 Image

Ananta is invoked in a kalaśa, or in a serpent of darbha grass, or a picture of Viṣṇu on Śeṣa.

🧵 The thread

A new thread of red or yellow cotton, dyed with turmeric, with 14 knots, is worshipped, then tied: men on the right arm, women on the left in many regions.

🍬 Fourteen

Offerings in fourteens are common, as are 14 types of leaf or flower, and sweets. A partial fast is kept.

🔁 Renewing

Many families keep the vow for 14 years; the old thread is removed after a set time, often 14 days, or replaced the next year.

ZAPPY: So many fourteens!

PROF. BOLT: Fourteen worlds, Zappy: seven above and seven below. Ananta fills them all.

WHY FOURTEEN?

Worlds and knots

The day. It is the 14th tithi, caturdaśī.

The worlds. Purāṇic cosmology counts fourteen worlds (lokas): seven upper, from Bhūr to Satya, and seven lower, from Atala to Pātāla, where Śeṣa supports them all.

Knots. Each knot is often said to stand for one world, or for one of Ananta’s names. (Explanations vary.)

Fourteen years. The vow’s period matches Kauṇḍinya’s penance in the story.

ZAPPY: Śeṣa holds up the worlds?

PROF. BOLT: In the Purāṇic picture, Zappy, on his hoods.

Fourteen worlds, one Ananta.

GAṆEŚA’S FAREWELL

Visarjana day

End of the festival. Public Gaṇeśa Caturthī images, especially in Maharashtra, are kept for ten days and immersed on Ananta Caturdaśī.

The great processions. Mumbai and Pune see processions lasting through the night, with the cry “Gaṇapati bappā moryā, pudhcyā varṣī lavkar yā”: come back soon next year.

Two traditions meet. Home Gaṇeśas are often immersed earlier (1½, 3, 5 or 7 days); the Ananta vrata to Viṣṇu is a separate, older observance.

Clay and water. Many now prefer clay images and tank immersion to protect lakes and seas.

THE JAIN DAY

Forgiveness

Daśalakṣaṇa. For Digambara Jains, Ananta Caturdaśī is the last of the ten days of Daśalakṣaṇa parva, a festival of ten virtues such as forgiveness, humility and honesty.

Kṣamāvaṇī. The next day is the day of asking forgiveness of everyone, with the words “micchāmi dukkaḍaṃ”.

Ananta. In Jain usage, the fourteenth Tīrthaṅkara is Anantanātha.

A shared lesson. Kauṇḍinya’s pride and the Jain day of forgiveness point the same way: humility. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: Asking forgiveness from everyone at once!

PROF. BOLT: A good habit for all of us, Zappy.

Ask forgiveness.

WHAT ANANTA TEACHES

Endless grace

Grace, not just effort. Kauṇḍinya’s mistake was to think he alone made his fortune. The thread reminds us that much is received.

The infinite in the small. A cotton thread on the arm stands for the endless; the tradition often works this way.

Steadiness. A fourteen-year vow is a long commitment; keeping a promise over years is itself the lesson.

Letting go. Gaṇeśa’s immersion on the same day teaches non-attachment: love the image, then let it return to the water. (Interpretation.)
