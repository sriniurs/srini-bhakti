MĀRGAŚĪRṢA PŪRṆIMĀ · THE GURU OF GURUS

Datta Jayantī

The Birthday of Dattātreya · explained and illustrated

Three heads, four dogs, one teacher

HOW THIS WORKS: The day, the birth story of Atri and Anasūyā, the form and its symbols, the twenty-four teachers, the verses, the Datta saints and the places from Gāṇagāpura to Mysuru.

WHERE WE ARE

On the full moon of Mārgaśīrṣa, Maharashtra, north Karnataka, Andhra and Telangana celebrate the birth of Dattātreya: Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva in one form, born as the son of the sage Atri and the devoted Anasūyā, and honoured as the Ādi Guru, the first teacher.

This article covers the date, the story of his birth, how to read his image, the famous account of his twenty-four teachers, two verses from his stotra, the line of Datta saints and the Guru Caritra, and the places where the day is kept. His temple at Gāṇagāpura has its own guide; as a teacher of Śrīvidyā he appears in Dattātreya and Paraśurāma.

THE DAY AND THE NAME

“Given”, son of Atri

The date. Datta Jayantī falls on Mārgaśīrṣa Pūrṇimā, the full moon of the month whose bright Ekādaśī is Gītā Jayantī. Many keep it at pradoṣa, dusk, because tradition says he was born then.

The name. Datta means “given”: the gods gave themselves as a son. Ātreya means “son of Atri”. Devotees often say simply Datta, Dattaguru or Gurudeva Datta.

A week of preparation. Many devotees read the Guru Caritra in seven days ending on the Jayantī, and temples hold festivals from Ekādaśī or earlier up to the full moon.

Where it is biggest. Maharashtra, the Kalyāṇa-Karnāṭaka region (Kalaburagi, Bīdar), Goa, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Gujarat; in Mysuru the Avadhūta Datta Pīṭha keeps a three-day festival.

ZAPPY: Why Thursday for Datta?

PROF. BOLT: Thursday is guruvāra, the teacher’s day, Zappy. Datta is the teacher of teachers.

The full moon of the teacher.

ANASŪYĀ AND THE THREE GUESTS

How the Trimūrti became her children

The story

A popular Purāṇic telling; texts differ in detail (see the next panel).

Anasūyā, wife of the sage Atri, was famed across the worlds for her devotion and virtue. In the popular telling, the goddesses Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī and Pārvatī asked their husbands to test her.
Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva came to the hermitage disguised as ascetics while Atri was away, and asked for alms on an impossible condition that would have broken her vow.
Anasūyā sprinkled water sanctified by her devotion over them, and the three became infants. She fed and cradled them as their mother, and the condition no longer mattered.
Atri returned and, seeing what had happened, embraced the three, who became one child with three heads and six arms. The goddesses came to ask for their husbands back; Anasūyā gladly agreed.
The gods resumed their forms and granted Atri and Anasūyā their wish: a son who would be all three. That son was Dattātreya.

ZAPPY: She turned the gods into babies?

PROF. BOLT: Her purity did, Zappy. The story says no trick can overcome a heart that is truly steady.

Devotion disarms even gods.

OTHER TELLINGS OF HIS BIRTH

What the Purāṇas say

Atri’s tapas. In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (book 4), Atri performs austerity on Ṛkṣa mountain praying for a son like the Lord of the world; Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva appear together and promise him sons: Soma (the Moon) from Brahmā, Datta from Viṣṇu, and Durvāsā from Śiva.

One of the avatāras. The same Bhāgavata lists Datta as the sixth of Viṣṇu’s incarnations, who taught self-knowledge to Alarka and Prahlāda. Many traditions treat him as Viṣṇu’s; others as all three equally.

The Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa tells how Datta, appearing as a wanderer careless of convention, taught yoga to King Alarka and blessed Kārtavīrya Arjuna.

The Anasūyā test of the previous page is the version most told at Datta Jayantī; it appears in later Purāṇic and Marathi devotional tradition rather than in the Bhāgavata. Traditions differ; the meaning they share is that the three gods chose to be given to a devoted household.

ZAPPY: So which story is right?

PROF. BOLT: Each holds a piece, Zappy. All agree on Atri, on Anasūyā and on three gods becoming one teacher.

Many tellings, one son.

READING HIS IMAGE

Three heads, six arms, a cow and four dogs

Three heads. Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva; also read as the three qualities (sattva, rajas, tamas) mastered, or as creation, preservation and dissolution held in one awareness. (Interpretations.)

Six hands. Usually the rosary and water-pot of Brahmā, the conch and discus of Viṣṇu, and the trident and ḍamaru drum of Śiva.

The cow and the dogs. The cow behind him is read as the earth, or as dharma; the four dogs as the four Vedas following him faithfully. Dogs are outsiders in orthodox ritual, which makes their place here pointed: the teacher is beyond convention. (Interpretation.)

The audumbara tree. He is often shown under the audumbara (cluster fig, Ficus racemosa), held sacred to him; Datta temples and āśramas, including Mysuru’s, keep one. Older images, as at Mahur, show him with one head and two arms.

ZAPPY: Dogs in a temple picture!

PROF. BOLT: Exactly, Zappy. The Vedas follow him like faithful dogs, and the outsider is welcomed. That is Datta.

The Vedas follow the teacher.

THE TWENTY-FOUR TEACHERS

What the Avadhūta learned from the world

In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (book 11, chapters 7–9), Kṛṣṇa tells Uddhava how an avadhūta, identified by tradition with Dattātreya, explained his contentment to King Yadu by naming twenty-four teachers. The full list: earth, air, sky, water, fire, moon, sun, pigeon, python, sea, moth, bee, elephant, honey-gatherer, deer, fish, the courtesan Piṅgalā, osprey, child, maiden, arrow-maker, snake, spider and wasp.

🌍 Earth, water, fire, sky

The earth taught patience under every burden; water, to cleanse without pride; fire, to burn what is offered and stay pure; the sky, to touch all things and cling to none.

🌙 Moon and sun

The moon waxes and wanes, but the moon itself does not change: so the self through birth and age. The sun draws water and gives it back as rain.

🐍 Python, bee, elephant, fish

The python accepts what comes; the bee takes a little nectar from many flowers; the elephant and fish are trapped by touch and taste: a warning about the senses.

🧒 Child, maiden, arrow-maker, spider

The carefree child; the maiden whose bangles clashed until she wore just one (live alone to think clearly); the arrow-maker who did not notice a royal procession pass; the spider who spins its web out of itself and draws it back in, like the Lord with the world.

ZAPPY: Twenty-four teachers and no school!

PROF. BOLT: The whole world was his school, Zappy. The Bhāgavata tells it as his reply to King Yadu, who asked why he was so happy.

The world is a teacher.

IN THE VERSE

Jaṭādharaṃ pāṇḍuraṅgam

The dhyāna verse of the Dattātreya Stotra
jaṭādharaṃ pāṇḍuraṅgaṃ śūlahastaṃ kṛpānidhimsarvarogaharaṃ devaṃ dattātreyam ahaṃ bhaje
I worship the god Dattātreya, who wears matted locks, pale-white in colour, trident in hand, a treasury of compassion, who removes every illness.
WordMeaning
jaṭādharamwearing matted hair
pāṇḍuraṅgampale white
śūlahastamtrident in hand
kṛpānidhimocean of compassion
sarvarogaharamremover of all illness

A healer’s portrait. The verse paints Datta as an ascetic, ash-pale with matted hair like Śiva, yet called a treasury of compassion: the stern renunciant is also the gentlest of teachers. “Remover of all illness” is read by devotees both literally and as the illness of rebirth.

Dattātreya Stotra, attributed to the Nārada Purāṇa; text as in sanskritdocuments.org (“dattAtreyastotram (nAradapurANa)”), dhyāna verse.
Stern in form, gentle in heart.

IN THE VERSE

Ādau brahmā

Verse 6 of the Dattātreya Stotra
ādau brahmā madhye viṣṇur ante devaḥ sadāśivaḥmūrtitrayasvarūpāya dattātreya namo ’stu te
In the beginning Brahmā, in the middle Viṣṇu, at the end the god Sadāśiva: to you whose very form is these three, O Dattātreya, salutations.
WordMeaning
ādauin the beginning
madhyein the middle
anteat the end
mūrtitrayathe three forms
svarūpāyato the one whose nature is

Time as a teacher. The verse reads the Trimūrti as phases of time: what begins, what lasts, what ends. Datta is all three at once, so to bow to him is to bow to the whole course of a life. Many devotees chant this single line as a complete prayer.

Same edition, verse 6; some texts read madhya viṣṇur.

ZAPPY: Even endings are part of him?

PROF. BOLT: Especially endings, Zappy. A teacher who could only begin things would leave you halfway.

Beginning, middle, end: one teacher.

THE DATTA SAINTS

Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha and Narasiṃha Sarasvatī

The story

The Guru Caritra tradition; Wikipedia, “Dattatreya”.

Devotees hold that Datta keeps returning as human teachers. The first is Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha, born, tradition says, at Pīṭhāpuram in Andhra Pradesh in the fourteenth century, who lived as a young renunciant and worked wonders for the poor.
The second is Narasiṃha Sarasvatī (fifteenth century), who after years of wandering settled at Gāṇagāpura on the Bhīmā and Amarajā rivers in Kalaburagi district; Narasobāvāḍi and Audumbar on the Kṛṣṇā keep his memory too.
A devotee in his line, Sarasvatī Gaṅgādhara, wrote the Guru Caritra in Marathi: 51 chapters in three parts, on knowledge (1–24), ritual action (25–37) and devotion (38–51).
The book became the scripture of the Datta movement. Reading it in a week (saptāha) ending on Datta Jayantī is a common vow.
Later teachers honoured as Datta’s forms include Māṇik Prabhu of Māṇiknagar (Bīdar), Svāmī Samartha of Akkalkoṭ and Sāī Bābā of Śirḍī for many devotees.

ZAPPY: The same teacher, again and again?

PROF. BOLT: That is how his devotees see it, Zappy: the teacher keeps arriving whenever a student is ready.

The teacher keeps arriving.

WHERE THE DAY IS KEPT

From Gāṇagāpura to Mysuru

Gāṇagāpura (Kalaburagi). Narasiṃha Sarasvatī’s nirguṇa pādukā, his sandals, are worshipped here; Datta Jayantī brings a great fair. See our Gāṇagāpura Dattātreya guide.

Pīṭhāpuram, Narasobāvāḍi, Mahur. Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha’s birthplace in Andhra; the Kṛṣṇā–Pañcagaṅgā confluence in Maharashtra; and Mahur in Nāṇḍeḍ district, a Datta seat with a single-headed image and one of the places named as his birthplace. Māṇiknagar near Humnābād holds a festival from Ekādaśī to Pūrṇimā.

Mysuru. The Avadhūta Datta Pīṭha on the Nañjanagūḍu road, founded by Gaṇapati Saccidānanda Svāmī in 1966, keeps a three-day Datta Jayantī in December with Śrīcakra pūjā and abhiṣeka; once a year, on the Jayantī, devotees may offer oil abhiṣeka to the main image. In 2016 the second day coincided with Hanumān Jayantī.

Chikkamagaḷūru. The hill shrine called Dattātreya Pīṭha on Bābābuḍangiri is honoured by Hindus as a Datta seat and by Sufis as the shrine of Bābā Buḍan; its management has been the subject of long dispute. Traditions differ.

ZAPPY: A Datta Pīṭha in Mysuru!

PROF. BOLT: On the road to Nañjanagūḍu, Zappy, with its own audumbara tree. The city’s Datta Jayantī is three days long.

His day, across the Deccan.

KEEPING DATTA JAYANTĪ

At home and in the temple

The week before. The Guru Caritra read in seven days, or one chapter a day; many keep simple food and visit a Datta temple each Thursday.

The day. A bath, worship of Datta’s image or pādukā with flowers, tulasī and bilva, bhajans such as the Marathi refrain “Digambarā Digambarā Śrīpāda Vallabha Digambarā”, and, at dusk, a cradle ceremony in many temples with songs for the newborn.

Offerings. Sweets and fruit; feeding cows and dogs, honouring Datta’s companions; giving food to wandering ascetics, since Datta is said to come to the door in disguise.

The Avadhūta Gītā. Some read from this Advaita text of eight chapters attributed to him, a song of the free who see one Self in all.

ZAPPY: Feed the dogs on Datta’s day?

PROF. BOLT: The Vedas walk with him as dogs, Zappy. Kindness to them is a fine way to honour him.

Feed the guest; the guest may be Datta.

WHAT THE DAY TEACHES

The teacher within and without

Everything can teach. The twenty-four teachers turn the whole world into a classroom. The festival asks: what did the earth, a child, a mistake teach me this year? (Interpretation.)

Unity. Datta joins Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva, Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva, the Sanskrit texts and the Marathi saints, the Nātha yogīs and the householder. His devotees are among the least sectarian in India.

Freedom. The avadhūta, “one who has shaken off” convention, shows that holiness is not a costume. Teachers warn this is a goal, not an excuse to drop discipline. (Interpretation.)

Gratitude to the guru. As Ādi Guru, Datta is honoured on behalf of every teacher, as on Guru Pūrṇimā.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing on Datta Jayantī?

PROF. BOLT: Think of one teacher who wasn’t a person, Zappy, and say thank you to them in your heart.

Learn from everything.
