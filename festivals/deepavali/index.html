FIVE DAYS · NARAKA, LAKṢMĪ, BALI · AND YAMA

Dīpāvali

The Five Days of Light · explained and illustrated

A row of lamps on the darkest night

HOW THIS WORKS: The festival day by day, from the water pots of Trayodaśī to Yama’s dinner with his sister, with every story told in full, the verses of each day, and how Karnataka keeps it.

WHERE WE ARE

Dīpāvali is the one festival the whole of India keeps, and no two regions keep it alike. Karnataka fills water pots, bathes before dawn and welcomes King Bali home; Tamil Nadu asks “have you had your Gaṅgā bath?”; the north lights Ayodhyā for Rāma; Bengal worships Kālī; Gujarat begins its new year. Underneath all of it is one picture: a lamp lit on the darkest night of the year.

This article takes the festival day by day, from the thirteenth of the dark fortnight to the second of the bright one. It tells every story attached to it in full (Dhanvantari, the prince saved from Yama, Naraka, Lakṣmī, Bali and Vāmana, Yama and Yamunā, Rāma’s return), gives the verses said on each day from the Purāṇas, and explains how Karnataka keeps it.

Our main source is the Skanda Purāṇa’s Kārttika-māhātmya, whose ninth chapter is a complete Dīpāvali manual, with the Padma Purāṇa and the Bhāgavata for the stories.

With the blessings of the Lord,

THE NAME AND THE NIGHT

Why lamps on the new moon

The word. Dīpa is a lamp and āvali a row or line: Dīpāvali is a row of lamps. Old texts also call it Dīpamālikā (a garland of lamps) and Dīpotsava (the lamp festival); Kannada says Dīpāvaḷi, and many Karnataka villages simply call it doḍḍa habba, the big festival.

The darkest night. The centre of the festival is Amāvāsyā, the new moon of the month that ends the rains. There is no moonlight at all; the lamps carry the whole night. Many teachers read this as the festival’s lesson: light matters most where it is darkest. (Interpretation.)

Which month? In the amānta calendar used in Karnataka, the new moon ends Āśvina and the next day begins Kārtika; in the pūrṇimānta calendar of the north the same night falls in Kārtika. It is one night with two month-names, and the reason sources seem to disagree.

The start of Kārtika. In Karnataka the festival opens the month of Kārtika, the month of lamps, and in many homes the lamps simply continue every evening until Kārtika Pūrṇimā.

ZAPPY: So the new moon is the main day?

PROF. BOLT: For Lakṣmī, yes. But in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu the dawn bath the day before often feels like the real Dīpāvali. That’s why the five days matter.

A row of lamps on the moonless night.

A FESTIVAL OVER A THOUSAND YEARS OLD

What the old records say

The plays and poets. Harṣa’s seventh-century drama Nāgānanda names a Dīpapratipadutsava, a lamp festival on the first day of the bright fortnight, when newly-weds received gifts. In the ninth century Rājaśekhara’s Kāvyamīmāṃsā calls it Dīpamālikā, when houses were whitewashed and lit with lamps.

Karnataka’s inscriptions. Some of the oldest dated mentions come from Kannada country: a copper plate of the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Kṛṣṇa III (tenth century) mentions a Dīpotsava, a tenth-century Jain inscription at Saundatti and a twelfth-century inscription at Dharwad record lamp festivals. (Details as summarised in secondary sources; we have not read the inscriptions themselves.)

The travellers. The Persian scholar al-Bīrūnī (eleventh century) described the festival on the new moon of Kārtika; the Italian Niccolò de’ Conti (fifteenth century) saw lamps burning in temples; the Portuguese Domingo Paes described it at Vijayanagara in the sixteenth.

The Purāṇas. The Padma and Skanda Purāṇas give the fullest ritual accounts, day by day. Their dates are uncertain; scholars place much of this material between the seventh and tenth centuries.

ZAPPY: So it was already old when Vijayanagara was new?

PROF. BOLT: Older than most of the temples you know, Zappy. And Karnataka’s stones are among its earliest witnesses.

Recorded for over a thousand years.

DAY 1 · DHANVANTARI

Dhana Trayodaśī: the healer rises from the ocean

The story

The first day honours the physician of the gods. The story is in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 8.

Gods and demons churned the ocean of milk with Mount Mandara and the serpent Vāsuki, seeking amṛta, the nectar of immortality. (The whole churning is told in our Nīlakaṇṭha article.)
First the poison rose, which Śiva drank; then came treasures: the wish-cow, the white horse, the elephant Airāvata, the Kaustubha gem, and Lakṣmī herself.
Last of all rose a dark, handsome youth with a pot full of nectar in his hands: Dhanvantari, master of Āyurveda, a portion of Viṣṇu.
The demons snatched the pot from his hands, and Viṣṇu had to take the form of Mohinī to win it back for the gods.
Because the healer appeared with the nectar of life, Trayodaśī is kept by many as Dhanvantari Jayantī, and India now marks it as National Ayurveda Day.

ZAPPY: Is “Dhanteras” about money or medicine?

PROF. BOLT: Both, and the words are mixed up. Dhana is wealth; Dhanvantari is the healer. Health is the first wealth, so many families honour him before Lakṣmī.

The healer comes with the nectar of life.

THE PRINCE SAVED FROM YAMA

The story of the Yama-dīpa

The story

The Skanda Purāṇa only names “the story of Hemanaka’s son”; this is the telling heard in most homes.

King Hima (Hemanaka) had one son. Astrologers foretold that the prince would die of a snake-bite on the fourth night after his wedding.
The prince married, and his young bride heard the prophecy. On the fourth night she would not let him sleep.
She heaped all her gold and jewels at the door of the room, lit lamps all round them, and kept him awake telling stories and singing.
At midnight Yama came as a serpent. The blaze of lamps and gold dazzled him; he coiled on the heap, listened to her songs till dawn, and went away without his prize.
So on Trayodaśī evening a lamp is set out at the door, facing south, the direction of Yama: the Yama-dīpa, offered against untimely death.

ZAPPY: She beat Yama with lamps and songs?

PROF. BOLT: With wakefulness, Zappy. The story says: stay awake, keep the light burning, and even death waits. That’s the Yama-dīpa.

A lamp at the door against untimely death.

A lamp for the god of death. The Skanda Purāṇa (Kārttika-māhātmya 9.20–26) says the lamp is set out early in the night, outside the door, facing south, and that whoever does so need not fear untimely death. The Padma Purāṇa gives the same rite with a slightly different wording of this verse. Yama is not treated as an enemy here: he is invited to be pleased.

Yama is honoured, not feared.

NĪRU TUMBUVA HABBA

Karnataka’s water-filling day

The water festival. In much of Karnataka, Trayodaśī is nīru tumbuva habba, the festival of filling water. The big copper bath-boiler (hande) and the water pots are scrubbed, painted with lime and kuṅkuma, garlanded with flower strings and filled with fresh water for the next dawn’s oil bath. Many worship the filled vessel as Gaṅgā.

Why the night before. The water stands overnight in the decorated vessels, and the fire under the boiler is kept going so the bath is hot before sunrise. The Skanda Purāṇa says that at the Caturdaśī bath “Lakṣmī is in the oil and Gaṅgā in the water”: the vessel is being prepared for Gaṅgā.

In the Malnāḍ and Uttara Kannaḍa. Hill villages fetch the water from a river or well with song; some set up a new earthen pot as a household goddess of herbs and health, and fill it with leaves and medicinal plants. Customs vary from village to village.

Across India. The same evening the north buys gold, silver and new utensils for Dhanteras, farmers decorate their cattle, and Tamil homes prepare marundu, a medicinal paste eaten before the festival sweets.

ZAPPY: So Karnataka worships the bath water?

PROF. BOLT: The water that will wash you on the holiest morning of the year, yes. Fill it with care, and the bath becomes a Gaṅgā-snāna at home.

Fill the pots for tomorrow’s Gaṅgā.

DAY 2 · THE DEMON OF PRĀGJYOTIṢA

Naraka Caturdaśī, part one

The story

The fourteenth belongs to Kṛṣṇa. The story is told in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 10, chapter 59.

Naraka, also called Bhauma (“son of the Earth”), ruled Prāgjyotiṣa in the east. Later Purāṇas make him the son of Bhūmi, the Earth goddess, by Varāha.
Grown proud, he stole the earrings of Aditi, mother of the gods, and the royal umbrella of Varuṇa, seized a peak of Mount Mandara, and shut away sixteen thousand princesses in his palace.
Indra came to Dvārakā and told Kṛṣṇa. Kṛṣṇa mounted Garuḍa and, the Bhāgavata says, took his queen Satyabhāmā with him.
Prāgjyotiṣa was ringed by hills, by weapons that fired themselves, by water, fire and wind, and by the snare-wires of the demon Mura.
Kṛṣṇa broke the rock with his mace, the weapons with arrows, the fire, water and wind with his discus, the wires with his sword, and blew his conch till the city’s magic seals shattered.

ZAPPY: Why take Satyabhāmā to a war?

PROF. BOLT: Traditions differ. The Bhāgavata simply says she came. Some tellings say she asked to; in others she is Bhūmi herself, and that matters in the next part.

Kṛṣṇa rides against the thief of heaven.

THE FALL OF NARAKA

Naraka Caturdaśī, part two

The story

Bhāgavata 10.59, verses 6–37.

The five-headed Mura rose from the moat and hurled his trident at Garuḍa. Kṛṣṇa split it with arrows and cut off his heads with the discus; that is why he is called Murāri, enemy of Mura. Mura’s seven sons fell after him.
Naraka came out on an elephant. His weapons failed against Garuḍa, and as he raised his trident, Kṛṣṇa’s discus severed his head.
Bhūmi, the Earth, came forward with Aditi’s earrings, Varuṇa’s umbrella and the Vaijayantī garland, praised Kṛṣṇa, and placed Naraka’s frightened son Bhagadatta at his feet. Kṛṣṇa gave the boy fearlessness.
In the palace Kṛṣṇa found the sixteen thousand captive princesses and sent them to Dvārakā with honour; he returned the earrings to Aditi in heaven.
Popular tradition adds two things the Bhāgavata does not: that Naraka could die only at his mother’s hand, so Satyabhāmā, an incarnation of Bhūmi, struck the blow; and that the dying Naraka asked that his day be kept with lamps and a bath.

ZAPPY: A demon asked for a festival?

PROF. BOLT: So the story goes, Zappy. He saw his mistake at the end and asked to be remembered not with mourning but with light. That’s the generosity of the tradition.

Pride falls; the captives go free.

THE DAWN OIL BATH

Abhyaṅga snāna and the Yama-tarpaṇa

Before sunrise. The Skanda Purāṇa tells everyone to bathe on Caturdaśī before dawn, while the stars are still out, after rubbing the body with sesame oil: “Lakṣmī is in the oil and Gaṅgā in the water,” and whoever bathes so “does not see Yama’s world.” Hence the Tamil greeting “Gaṅgā snānam āccā?”, have you had your Gaṅgā bath?

At home in Karnataka. The eldest woman applies warm oil to everyone’s head, the children first; a paste of gram flour or herbs follows, then the hot water from the pots filled the night before. Then new clothes, the first sweets, the first crackers, and the elders’ blessings.

The apāmārga. During the bath a sprig of the thorny apāmārga (prickly chaff-flower), with a clod of earth from a furrow, is turned around the head three times while a verse is said (next page): the sin is “turned away”.

Yama-tarpaṇa and lamps. The same text has bathers offer water with sesame to Yama under fourteen names (Yama, Dharmarāja, Mṛtyu, Antaka, Vaivasvata, Kāla…) and, in the evening, a four-wicked lamp to Naraka himself; many also light a lamp for Yama’s two dogs. In parts of the north the day is Choṭī Dīvālī or Rūp Caudas.

ZAPPY: Oil before a bath? Doesn’t that defeat the point?

PROF. BOLT: Ask any grandmother, Zappy. The oil soaks in, the hot water opens the skin, and you come out softer than ever. The scripture calls it Lakṣmī and Gaṅgā; your skin calls it winter care.

Oil is Lakṣmī, water is Gaṅgā.

What is turned away is the sin.

DAY 3 · THE NIGHT OF LAKṢMĪ

Amāvāsyā in the Skanda Purāṇa

The day. The Skanda Purāṇa’s programme for the new moon is precise. In the morning, a bath and a pārvaṇa śrāddha for the ancestors: Dīpāvali begins with the dead. In the evening, Lakṣmī is worshipped in a decorated pavilion, a bed is made for her, and sweets (laḍḍukas) are offered.

Torches for the ancestors. In the early night lamps and firebrands (ulkā) are lifted to show the ancestors their way back after the fortnight of Pitṛ Pakṣa. Much later, firecrackers took over this place; the text speaks only of lamps and torches.

Driving out Alakṣmī. At midnight, the text says, the town goes round with music, and before dawn the women of the house drive out Alakṣmī, misfortune, Lakṣmī’s sister, with the noise of winnowing fans and drums. Then Lakṣmī is woken and the house is hers.

The night of waking. The night is called Sukharātri, the happy night, and the text asks people to stay awake, with the Purāṇa, songs, dice or the Gītā. Lakṣmī, says the old belief, goes looking for homes that are awake, clean and lit.

ZAPPY: Ancestors and Lakṣmī on the same day?

PROF. BOLT: Gratitude to those who came before, then welcome to what comes next. The text does both, morning and night.

Clean, lit, and awake.

LAKṢMĪ CHOOSES

Why she comes on this night

The story

Two stories explain the Lakṣmī Pūjā.

From the churned ocean rose Śrī, Lakṣmī, on a lotus, so beautiful that gods, demons and sages all wanted her (Bhāgavata 8.8).
She looked over them all, the proud, the angry, the ascetic, the unkind, and found every one wanting. At last she placed her garland on Viṣṇu, who wanted nothing, and made his chest her home.
Popular tradition holds that this choosing, or her marriage to Viṣṇu, fell on the Dīpāvali new moon.
The Skanda Purāṇa adds another story: when Vāmana granted Bali a boon, Bali asked that Lakṣmī stay permanently in every house that lights lamps on these days of his rule.
So Lakṣmī is worshipped on the darkest night in homes made clean and bright, with the ledgers, the coins, the jewellery, and the lamp itself.

ZAPPY: She chose the one who didn’t want her?

PROF. BOLT: Wealth stays with those who don’t grab, Zappy. That’s the oldest lesson of Lakṣmī.

Śrī stays where there is light.

THE SAME NIGHT ELSEWHERE

Kālī, Kedāra Gaurī and the new ledgers

🌑 Bengal and Assam

The night is Kālī Pūjā. Records show Lakṣmī was worshipped here earlier; the great Kālī celebrations grew mainly from the twentieth century. Related reading: the Mahāvidyās.

📒 Traders

Shops worship their account books with “śubha lābha” written in them; in Gujarat new ledgers open (cōpḍā pūjan), and the next day is the new year.

🏺 Karnataka

Many families keep the Kedāra Gaurī vrata on Amāvāsyā, worshipping Pārvatī as Kedāra Gaurī with a kalaśa and a twenty-one-knot thread; farming homes add Gaurī and cow worship.

🪔 Jains and Sikhs

Jains keep the night as Mahāvīra’s nirvāṇa: when his light went out, the story says, eighteen kings lit lamps. Sikhs keep Bandī Choṛ Divas for Guru Hargobind’s release from Gwalior fort. Newar Buddhists keep the five days as Swanti.

ZAPPY: So the same lamps mean different things?

PROF. BOLT: The same lamp, many prayers. That’s why Dīpāvali belongs to everyone.

One night, many prayers.

DAY 4 · THREE STEPS OF LAND

Bali and Vāmana

The story

The story is in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 8, chapters 15–23. Read also our Vāmana article.

Bali, grandson of Prahlāda, was a just and generous asura king who had conquered the three worlds and driven Indra from heaven. Viṣṇu would not kill a good king and a devotee.
Instead he was born as Vāmana, a dwarf brahmin boy with an umbrella, and came to Bali’s great sacrifice, where the king had promised to give anything asked.
Vāmana asked only for three steps of land. Bali’s guru Śukra warned him it was Viṣṇu himself; Bali replied that he would not break his word, and poured the water of the gift.
The boy grew into Trivikrama: one step covered the earth, a second the heavens. Nothing was left for the third. Bali bowed and offered his own head.
Pleased, Viṣṇu sent him to rule Sutala below the earth, promised he would be Indra in a future age, and stood guard at his door himself. Popular tradition adds the boon of Bali Pratipadā: once a year Bali may come home.

ZAPPY: So Bali lost?

PROF. BOLT: He lost a kingdom and kept his word. The Bhāgavata treats that as the greater victory, and so does every Karnataka village that welcomes him.

He gave even his own head.

That is why the Dharmasindhu counts Bali Pratipadā among the three most auspicious days of the year, and why Bali is worshipped, not defeated, on it.

A promise is worth more than a kingdom.

BALI PĀḌYAMI IN KARNATAKA

Balīndra comes home

The welcome. On Kārtika Śukla Pratipadā, Karnataka welcomes Bali Cakravarti, often called Balīndra. In many villages a figure of Bali, often a triangle of cow dung, is shaped on a plank set on a rangoli, crowned with marigolds and worshipped with lamps; Bali and his queen Vindhyāvali are honoured together.

Coastal Karnataka. In Tuḷunāḍu Dīpāvali is above all Bali’s festival: lamps are set on the paddy fields and cattle sheds, and Bali is called back with the cry for “Balīndra” to come and see how well his people live. Read more in our Tuḷunāḍu festivals.

Gō-pūjā. Cows and bullocks are bathed, their horns painted, garlanded and fed sweets, and the cowshed is worshipped. The Skanda Purāṇa’s chapter opens with a cow-and-calf vow (Vatsa Dvādaśī), and the cattle remain at the centre of Day 4 in farming homes.

Elsewhere. In the north the day is Govardhana Pūjā: a hill of cow dung or a mountain of food (Annakūṭa) is offered to Kṛṣṇa, who lifted Govardhana. Maharashtra calls it Pāḍvā; Gujarat keeps its new year; Kerala welcomes the same Mahābali at Ōṇam. Many families play dice, recalling the story that Śiva and Pārvatī played on this day and Pārvatī won.

ZAPPY: Why cow dung for a king?

PROF. BOLT: Because it is what the farm has, Zappy: clean, sacred and made by the cow. Bali was a king of farmers. They make him from the earth they live on.

The good king comes home.

DAY 5 · YAMA DINES WITH HIS SISTER

Yama Dvitīyā

The story

The last day is for brothers and sisters.

Yama, lord of death, and Yamunā, the river, are twins, children of the Sun. Yamunā asked her brother again and again to visit her, and he never found the time.
On the second day of Kārtika’s bright fortnight he came at last. Yamunā bathed him, marked his forehead with tilaka, garlanded him and fed him with her own hands.
Delighted, Yama offered her a boon. She asked that any brother who visits his sister on this day and eats from her hand should be free from the fear of untimely death.
Yama granted it. So the day is Yama Dvitīyā, also Bhaginī-hasta-bhojana, “eating from the sister’s hand”. Many bathe in the Yamunā this day.
Another telling says that after killing Naraka, Kṛṣṇa visited his sister Subhadrā, who welcomed him with sweets, lamps and a tilaka.

ZAPPY: The festival starts with a lamp for Yama and ends with Yama at dinner?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly, Zappy. It begins with keeping death at the door and ends with feeding him at the table. Dīpāvali makes even death family.

Brothers fed, death made gentle.

HOW YAMA DVITĪYĀ IS KEPT

Bhāī Dūj and its cousins

🍛 The meal

Brothers go to their sisters’ homes and eat a meal she cooks; she does ārati, applies a tilaka and prays for his long life; he gives a gift in return.

🗺️ The names

Bhāī Dūj in the north, Bhāū Bīj in Maharashtra and Goa, Bhāī Phõṭā in Bengal, Bhāī Ṭīkā in Nepal (with a seven-coloured mark), Bhaginī Hasta Bhojanamu in Andhra and Telaṅgāṇa, Yama Dvitīyā in Karnataka homes.

🪔 In Karnataka

Kept by many families rather than everywhere; in some areas the sisters’ ārati is done on the water-filling day or on Pāḍyami instead. Practice follows the family.

🌙 No brother?

In Maharashtra a woman without a brother may worship the moon as her brother; in many homes a cousin or a brother-in-spirit is fed.

ZAPPY: Like Rakṣā Bandhan?

PROF. BOLT: Cousins, Zappy. In Rakṣā Bandhan the sister ties a thread; here she feeds him. Both say: I want you to live long.

A sister’s meal for a brother’s long life.

RĀMA COMES HOME

Dīpāvali in the north

The return. Across north India Dīpāvali is above all Rāma’s homecoming: after fourteen years of exile and the defeat of Rāvaṇa, Rāma returned to Ayodhyā with Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa, and the city lit rows of lamps to welcome him. Read the life in our Rāma article.

What the texts say. The Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa describes Ayodhyā decorated with banners and flowers for the return and the coronation. The link between the return and the new-moon lamps is a beloved later tradition, strongest in the north; Karnataka families more often tell the Naraka and Bali stories.

Twenty days apart. Dasarā (Vijayadaśamī) marks Rāma’s victory, and Dīpāvali, about twenty days later, his arrival home; the tradition counts the journey back in between. (Popular tradition.)

Ayodhyā today. The ghāṭs of the Sarayū are lit with lakhs of lamps in a great Dīpotsava. The custom of lighting homes for a returning king, though, is what every household repeats.

ZAPPY: So which story is the real Dīpāvali?

PROF. BOLT: All of them, Zappy. Naraka in the south, Rāma in the north, Bali in Karnataka, Mahāvīra for the Jains. The lamps don’t argue.

A city lit for its king.

A KARNATAKA DĪPĀVALI AT HOME

Lanterns, lamps and the sweets of the season

🏮 Ākāśa buṭṭi

Star-shaped sky lanterns of paper and bamboo hung over the door, often from Naraka Caturdaśī right through Kārtika.

🪔 Haṇate

Clay lamps (haṇate) along walls, steps, windows, the tulasī platform and the well; many homes then light a lamp every evening of Kārtika.

🍬 The kitchen

Hōḷige (obbaṭṭu), karjikāyi, cakkuli, kōḍubaḷe, adhirasa, kajjāya and the festival oṭṭu-śāvige; in the Malnāḍ, special dishes for each day.

🎶 The villages

In north Karnataka the festival is the Haṭṭi habba, the cattle-shed festival; in the Malnāḍ teams go house to house singing antige-pintige songs with a lamp, blessing each home.

ZAPPY: Antige-pintige?

PROF. BOLT: Singers carrying a lamp from house to house, Zappy, who light your lamp from theirs and bless you with a song. The festival walks door to door.

Light passed from house to house.

WHAT DĪPĀVALI MEANS

Tamaso mā jyotir gamaya

Lead me from darkness to light. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad’s prayer, tamaso mā jyotir gamaya (1.3.28), is the line most teachers give as Dīpāvali’s meaning: the outer lamp stands for the inner light of knowledge. (Interpretation.)

Every story is a victory of light. Dhanvantari brings life; the bride’s lamps turn Yama back; Naraka’s captives go free; Lakṣmī chooses the one who does not grab; Bali gives even himself; Yamunā feeds her brother. Read in order, the five days move from fear of death to a feast with death at the table. (Our reading.)

Cleaning as worship. The house is whitewashed, swept and lit because Lakṣmī is said to enter only clean, bright, awake homes. Many read the outer cleaning as a picture of the inner one.

Light without harm. The texts speak of lamps and torches, not bangs. Many families now keep crackers few and outdoors for the sake of the air, the elderly and the animals; the oil lamp remains the heart of the festival.

ZAPPY: If I could do only one thing for Dīpāvali?

PROF. BOLT: Light one lamp on the new-moon night, say the lamp verse, and give someone a sweet. The rest grows from there.

One lamp lights another.
