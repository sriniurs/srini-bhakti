WHERE WE ARE

This article is about Aparā Ekādaśī, the Ekādaśī of the dark fortnight of Jyeṣṭha, the season of the height of summer. Its name means “the unlimited” (its merit is said to be boundless); also Acalā.

Kṛṣṇa tells Yudhiṣṭhira (Brahmāṇḍa and Padma Purāṇa tellings). Here is its story in full, its characters, what it teaches, what its māhātmya promises, and how it is kept. Before it comes Mohinī Ekādaśī; after it, Nirjalā Ekādaśī.

THE NAME APARĀ

“the unlimited” (its merit is said to be boundless); also Acalā

The name. Aparā means unlimited, for the merit said to come from it; it is also called Acalā, the steadfast.

The fortnight. It falls in the dark fortnight, when the moon is waning toward the new moon; dark-fortnight Ekādaśīs are often kept with special thought for release and for the departed.

The month. Jyeṣṭha is the season of the height of summer.

Its place in the year. It follows Mohinī Ekādaśī and comes before Nirjalā Ekādaśī.

ZAPPY: Is the name important?

PROF. BOLT: In every Ekādaśī the name is a summary of the story. Remember the name and you remember the lesson.

Aparā: “the unlimited” (its merit is said to be boundless); also Acalā.

WHO TELLS THE STORY

The frame of the māhātmya

The teller. Kṛṣṇa tells Yudhiṣṭhira (Brahmāṇḍa and Padma Purāṇa tellings).

Stories within stories. Ekādaśī māhātmyas are nested: Kṛṣṇa answers Yudhiṣṭhira’s question by retelling what a sage once told a king, so each story carries the authority of a chain of tellers.

The sources. These māhātmyas are found mainly in the Padma Purāṇa (Uttara Khaṇḍa) and the Bhaviṣyottara Purāṇa, and are printed together in popular Ekādaśī books. Details differ between editions.

Read at the vow. The story is read or heard on the day, usually after worship; hearing it is itself said to bring merit.

ZAPPY: Why so many layers of tellers?

PROF. BOLT: It’s how the Purāṇas work: wisdom passed down hand to hand, like the lamp of the guru-paramparā.

A story told from teller to teller.

THE STORY OF APARĀ

The vrata kathā in order

The story

Aparā’s story is about a ghost set free by a stranger’s kindness.

The good king Mahīdhvaja was murdered by his jealous younger brother Vajradhvaja, who buried him under a pīpal tree.
Killed unjustly, the king became a restless ghost living in the tree and frightening passers-by.
The sage Dhaumya, passing by, saw him with his yogic sight and pitied him.
The sage himself kept the Aparā Ekādaśī and gave its whole merit to the ghost.
Mahīdhvaja was freed and rose to heaven, blessing the sage.

ZAPPY: The sage fasted for someone he didn’t know?

PROF. BOLT: For a stranger, and a ghost at that. That is the highest kind of giving.

A sage’s fast frees a murdered king.

WHAT IT TEACHES

The meaning of the story

Kindness to the dead. Like Indirā Ekādaśī, its story ties the fast to the welfare of departed souls.

In practice. Some observe it in memory of family members who died suddenly or unhappily.

In one line. A sage’s fast frees a murdered king..

The family of stories. Like the other 25, it ends in release and a fresh start; see what the stories share in Part 1.

WHAT THE MĀHĀTMYA PROMISES

Phala-śruti

The promise. Its merit is called boundless (aparā); it is said to free from the sins of false witness, slander and the like.

Phala-śruti. Every māhātmya ends with a “hearing of the fruits” (phala-śruti), listing what the vow is said to give. These lists are generous by design: they are meant to encourage.

Read with sense. Teachers say the real fruit of any Ekādaśī is a mind turned to God; the other promises follow from that or are signs of it.

Hearing counts. Most māhātmyas say that even hearing or reading the story with faith brings part of the merit.

ZAPPY: Is it like a reward list?

PROF. BOLT: A list of encouragements. The best reward is the change in the person who keeps the vow.

The promised fruit, and the real one.

KEEPING THIS EKĀDAŚĪ

What is special about the day

Special to this day. Some keep it in memory of family members who died suddenly or unjustly.

The usual vow. Prepare on Daśamī with a simple meal; on Ekādaśī fast at your level and avoid grains; worship Viṣṇu with tulasī; hear the story; keep a vigil if you can; break the fast on Dvādaśī within the pāraṇa window. Details are in Part 2: Keeping the Vow.

Which date. Check your pañcāṅga; Smārta and Vaiṣṇava calendars sometimes differ by a day.

Care. The old, sick, pregnant and children are traditionally excused from strict fasting.

Keep Aparā with its own special touch.

WHEN IT FALLS

Two calendars

Northern name. In the full-moon-ending (pūrṇimānta) calendar of the north, Aparā falls in Jyeṣṭha.

Karnataka name. In the new-moon-ending (amānta) calendar used in Karnataka, it falls in Vaiśākha; the day itself is the same.

Season. It comes in the season of the height of summer.

Your almanac. The exact day is set by the pañcāṅga for your place.

Jyeṣṭha in the north, Vaiśākha in Karnataka.

TELLING IT TO CHILDREN

The story in a few lines

Short version. The good king Mahīdhvaja was murdered by his jealous younger brother Vajradhvaja, who buried him under a pīpal tree. Mahīdhvaja was freed and rose to heaven, blessing the sage.

The question to ask. The sage fasted for someone he didn’t know?

The answer. For a stranger, and a ghost at that. That is the highest kind of giving.

Why tell it. Ekādaśī stories are easy to remember and carry a clear lesson; children who hear them grow up knowing the day.
