Keeping the Vow

Fasting, the Night, and Breaking the Fast · explained and illustrated

The fast, the night and the morning after

HOW THIS WORKS: The three days of the vow, the levels of fasting, why rice is avoided, the night vigil, pāraṇa, which date to keep, and a word of care.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 1 we met Ekādaśī, the eleventh day, and the goddess born from Viṣṇu. Now the practical side: how the day is kept.

There are many levels, from a complete fast to simply avoiding grains, and the tradition is clear that health and circumstance matter. We describe the common shape; your family and teacher come first.

ZAPPY: Is there one correct way?

PROF. BOLT: No. There are strict ways and gentle ways. Pick what you can keep sincerely.

LEVELS OF FASTING

From strict to simple

💧 Nirjalā

No food and no water, from sunrise to the next sunrise. Traditionally only for Nirjalā Ekādaśī or the very strong.

🍎 Phalāhāra

Fruit, milk, nuts: the most common full fast.

🍲 Ekabhakta

One meal of non-grain food, often sago, potato, millet-free items as per family custom.

🌾 No grains

At the simplest, eat normally but avoid rice, wheat, pulses and beans. Even this is keeping Ekādaśī.

ZAPPY: Which one should I do?

PROF. BOLT: The one you can keep cheerfully. A harsh fast kept angrily misses the point.

Nirjalā, fruit, one meal, or no grains: choose sincerely.

WHY NO RICE

The grain rule

The rule. In nearly all traditions grains (especially rice) and beans are avoided on Ekādaśī, even by those who do not fully fast.

The story. A popular teaching, found in later Vaiṣṇava texts, says that on Ekādaśī sin takes shelter in grains, so eating them takes it in. It is a vivid way of saying: this day, eat lightly and simply.

Rice above all. South Indian households, where rice is the staple, are often strictest about rice.

What is eaten. Fruit, milk, curd, nuts, root vegetables, sago and certain flours, with variations by family.

ZAPPY: Is the story literal?

PROF. BOLT: Some take it so; many take it as teaching. Either way, the practice is to eat simply and keep God in mind.

Grains, above all rice, are set aside.

THE NIGHT VIGIL

Jāgaraṇa

Staying awake. The stories again and again praise jāgaraṇa, keeping awake through the night of Ekādaśī, singing bhajans, chanting names and listening to stories.

Why. Sleep is the most ordinary thing we do; giving up one night of it for God is a small, real offering. Several Ekādaśī heroes are saved precisely because they stayed awake, even by accident.

Communities. Temples and maṭhas often hold all-night bhajans on the great Ekādaśīs, especially Vaikuṇṭha.

If you can’t. Stay up a little later with a reading or a few names. The tradition values sincerity over endurance.

ZAPPY: All night, every Ekādaśī?

PROF. BOLT: Few do that. Most keep the vigil on the great ones. Even an hour counts.

Jāgaraṇa: a night given to the Lord.

BREAKING THE FAST

Pāraṇa on Dvādaśī

Pāraṇa. The fast is properly broken on Dvādaśī, the twelfth day, in the morning, within a window set by the almanac. Breaking it too late or skipping pāraṇa is said to spoil the vow.

First things. Many begin with a tulasī leaf, water or a little of the food offered to Viṣṇu, then a simple meal.

Feeding others. Feeding a guest, a brahmin or someone in need before eating is a traditional part of pāraṇa.

Ambarīṣa. The great story of pāraṇa is King Ambarīṣa and the sage Durvāsas, told in our Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī article.

ZAPPY: Why does the timing matter so much?

PROF. BOLT: Because the vow has a shape: prepare, keep, complete. Pāraṇa is the completion. Ambarīṣa risked a sage’s curse rather than miss it.

Pāraṇa on Dvādaśī: the vow completed.

WHICH DAY? SMĀRTA AND VAIṢṆAVA

Why calendars sometimes differ

The rule of overlap. When Daśamī (the 10th tithi) is still running at dawn of the day Ekādaśī begins, many Vaiṣṇava traditions consider that day “touched” by Daśamī and fast on the next day instead.

So two dates. Almanacs sometimes list a Smārta Ekādaśī and a Vaiṣṇava (or Bhāgavata) Ekādaśī a day apart.

What to do. Follow your family’s and your sampradāya’s calendar: Mādhva, Śrīvaiṣṇava, Smārta and Gauḍīya calendars each have their rules.

Mahā-dvādaśī. Some calendars also mark special Dvādaśīs on which the fast is kept instead. These are details for your almanac.

ZAPPY: That sounds complicated.

PROF. BOLT: It is, so let the almanac do it. Your part is the fast and the prayer.

Follow your own tradition’s calendar.

A WORD OF CARE

Who is excused, and when

The tradition itself excuses. Children, the elderly, the sick, pregnant and nursing women, and those doing heavy labour are traditionally exempt from strict fasting, or keep a light form.

Medical conditions. Anyone with diabetes, low blood pressure, kidney disease or on regular medication should speak to their doctor before fasting, and should never attempt a waterless fast.

A substitute. Those who cannot fast can avoid grains, chant, read, give food to others or keep the night vigil: all are part of Ekādaśī.

The spirit. The stories reward sincerity, not suffering.

ZAPPY: So I don’t have to fast to keep Ekādaśī?

PROF. BOLT: Fasting is the classic form. But remembering Viṣṇu is the heart, and no one is excused from that, or needs to be.

Health first; the heart of the day is remembrance.
