What Ekādaśī Is

The Eleventh Day and the Goddess Who Slew Mura · explained and illustrated

Moon, lamp, tulasī and the Lord

HOW THIS WORKS: First what a tithi is, then Ekādaśī’s birth from Viṣṇu, why eleven, the year of 26 Ekādaśīs, and what their stories share.

WHERE WE ARE

Twice every lunar month, on the eleventh day after the new moon and the full moon, millions of Hindus fast and pray to Viṣṇu. This day is Ekādaśī, “the eleventh”, and it is the most widely kept vow in the Vaiṣṇava tradition.

Our Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī article covered the greatest of them. This 28-part series covers them all: what Ekādaśī is, how the fast is kept, and then the 26 named Ekādaśīs of the year, each in its own article, with its story.

THE ELEVENTH DAY

What a tithi is

The lunar day. The Hindu calendar counts days by the moon. A tithi is the time the moon takes to move 12 degrees ahead of the sun, so there are 30 tithis in a lunar month: 15 in the bright half, waxing to the full moon, and 15 in the dark half, waning to the new moon.

The eleventh. Ekādaśī is the 11th tithi of each half. So there are two each month: śukla (bright) Ekādaśī and kṛṣṇa (dark) Ekādaśī.

Tithis and calendar days. A tithi does not match a calendar day exactly, so almanacs (pañcāṅga) decide on which day it is kept. Sometimes Smārta and Vaiṣṇava calendars differ by a day.

Named days. Each of the 24 Ekādaśīs has its own name and story in the Purāṇas.

ZAPPY: So Ekādaśī can fall on any weekday?

PROF. BOLT: Any. It follows the moon, not the week.

Ekādaśī: the 11th lunar day, twice a month.

THE GODDESS WHO SLEW MURA

The birth of Ekādaśī (Utpannā)

The story

Kṛṣṇa told this story to Yudhiṣṭhira.

The demon Mura drove the gods out of heaven, and Viṣṇu fought him for a long time.
Tired, Viṣṇu went to rest in the cave of Badarikāśrama.
Mura followed to kill him as he slept.
From Viṣṇu’s body a radiant goddess sprang forth, armed, and destroyed Mura.
Viṣṇu named her Ekādaśī, since she was born on the eleventh day, and blessed all who would keep her day.

ZAPPY: So Ekādaśī is a person, not just a date?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, a goddess: Viṣṇu’s own power. Keeping her day is honouring her.

From Viṣṇu’s body Ekādaśī was born, and slew Mura.

WHY ELEVEN, AND WHY FAST

Readings across the tradition

The eleven. A traditional reading counts the five organs of knowledge, the five organs of action and the mind: eleven in all. Ekādaśī is the day to bring all eleven back to God.

Fasting. Fasting quiets the body, so the mind can turn inward. The Purāṇas praise the Ekādaśī fast above other vows.

Viṣṇu’s day. Above all, it is a day for remembering Viṣṇu: chanting his names, reading the Gītā or Bhāgavatam, singing.

Health? Many today speak of health benefits of a fortnightly fast; that is a modern reading, not the tradition’s reason.

ZAPPY: So the fast is a tool, not the goal?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The goal is remembering the Lord. The fast clears the way.

Eleven senses turned to God: the meaning of the day.

THE YEAR OF EKĀDAŚĪS

Twenty-four names, two per month

Every one named. From Kāmadā in spring to Āmalakī before Holī, every Ekādaśī has a name, a story and a promised fruit. The leap month adds two more, Padminī and Paramā.

The great ones. Devaśayanī (Viṣṇu sleeps) and Prabodhinī (he wakes) frame the four months of Cāturmāsya. Nirjalā is the hardest. Vaikuṇṭha (Mokṣadā) is the holiest in the south.

Two ways of naming months. North India ends each month on the full moon; Karnataka and much of the south end it on the new moon. The bright Ekādaśīs fall in the same month either way, but each dark Ekādaśī belongs to the next month in the northern reckoning. This series follows the common northern list and notes the difference.

This series. Each of the 26 has its own article: Pāpamocanī, Kāmadā, Varūthinī, Mohinī, Aparā, Nirjalā, Yoginī, Devaśayanī, Kāmikā, Putradā (Śrāvaṇa), Ajā, Parivartinī, Indirā, Pāpāṅkuśā, Ramā, Prabodhinī, Utpannā, Mokṣadā, Saphalā, Putradā (Pauṣa), Ṣaṭtilā, Jayā, Vijayā, Āmalakī, Padminī, Paramā.

ZAPPY: So in Mysuru the dark ones are a month “earlier”?

PROF. BOLT: By name of month, yes. The day itself is the same; only the month label changes.

26 named Ekādaśīs, two per month, two more in a leap year.

WHAT THE STORIES SHARE

Patterns in the Ekādaśī tales

🔓 Release

Almost every story ends in release: from a curse, a ghost’s state, illness, poverty or sin.

🎁 Merit given away

Again and again the fruit of a fast is given to someone else: a husband, a father, a king, a stranger.

🌙 Accidental vows

Several heroes keep the fast without knowing it, through hunger or cold, and are still blessed.

🌅 A fresh start

Thieves, hunters, wastrels and proud saints all get a new beginning. No one is beyond it.

ZAPPY: The stories are quite kind.

PROF. BOLT: They are. They teach that grace is wider than our failures. That is why people love them.

Release, giving, grace, a fresh start.

WHERE THE STORIES COME FROM

The sources of the māhātmyas

Padma Purāṇa. Its Uttara Khaṇḍa holds most of the Ekādaśī māhātmyas, told by Kṛṣṇa to Yudhiṣṭhira.

Bhaviṣyottara Purāṇa. Another large collection, with many of the same stories in different words.

Other Purāṇas. Skanda, Brahmāṇḍa and Nārada Purāṇas add tellings; the Nārada Purāṇa has Rukmāṅgada’s story.

Vrata books. Later digests such as the Hari-bhakti-vilāsa (Gauḍīya) and regional Ekādaśī books gather the rules and stories. Editions differ in names and details.

Mostly Padma and Bhaviṣyottara.

FAMOUS KEEPERS OF THE VOW

Stories about Ekādaśī itself

👑 Ambarīṣa

The king of the Bhāgavata (Book 9) who kept Ekādaśī for a year; when he sipped water to break the fast while waiting for Durvāsā, the angry sage sent a demon, and Viṣṇu’s discus protected the king.

🧘 Durvāsā

Chased by the discus through the worlds until Viṣṇu sent him back to beg Ambarīṣa’s pardon.

👑 Rukmāṅgada

A king of the Nārada Purāṇa so devoted to Ekādaśī that he would break it for nothing, even when tested by Mohinī.

💪 Bhīma

Who kept just one, the waterless Nirjalā.

ZAPPY: Why did the king sip water?

PROF. BOLT: To honour both rules: break the fast on time, yet not eat before his guest. Water counts as both eating and not eating.

Ambarīṣa: the model Ekādaśī keeper.
