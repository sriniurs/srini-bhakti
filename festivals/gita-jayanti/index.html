MOKṢADĀ EKĀDAŚĪ · KURUKṢETRA · 700 VERSES

Gītā Jayantī

The Birthday of the Bhagavad Gītā · explained and illustrated

The song on the battlefield

HOW THIS WORKS: The day and why it falls in Mārgaśīrṣa, the story of the dialogue, the verses recited before the Gītā, Kurukṣetra and Udupi, the great commentators, and how families keep the day.

WHERE WE ARE

Most festivals mark the birth of a god or a victory. Gītā Jayantī marks the birth of a book: the day, tradition says, when Kṛṣṇa spoke the Bhagavad Gītā to Arjuna on the field of Kurukṣetra. It falls on the bright Ekādaśī of Mārgaśīrṣa, the same day as Mokṣadā Ekādaśī.

This article is about the day itself: its date and reason, the setting of the dialogue, the verses recited before reading, the places that celebrate it, the teachers who explained the Gītā, and simple ways to keep it. The text itself, chapter by chapter, is in our Bhagavad Gītā series.

WHY MĀRGAŚĪRṢA

“Among months, I am Mārgaśīrṣa”

The date. Gītā Jayantī is kept on the eleventh day (Ekādaśī) of the bright fortnight of Mārgaśīrṣa, the month called Mārgaśira in Kannada, which falls in November–December.

The reason in the text. In chapter 10, describing his glories, Kṛṣṇa says that among months he is Mārgaśīrṣa (10.35, next page). Tradition took this as a signature: the teaching was given in the month he names as himself.

An Ekādaśī already sacred. The same day is Mokṣadā Ekādaśī, “the Ekādaśī that gives liberation”, with its own vow and story. Many families keep both: the fast of the Ekādaśī and the reading of the Gītā.

A late name. The festival as a named public celebration is modern in form; old texts sanctify the day as Mokṣadā, and the link with the Gītā is a tradition rather than a statement in the Mahābhārata. (Sources differ.)

ZAPPY: Did the Mahābhārata write down the date?

PROF. BOLT: Not in so many words, Zappy. Tradition read Kṛṣṇa’s “I am Mārgaśīrṣa” and the sacred Ekādaśī together.

The month Kṛṣṇa calls his own.

IN THE VERSE

Gītā 10.35

Kṛṣṇa names his month
bṛhatsāma tathā sāmnāṃ gāyatrī chandasām ahammāsānāṃ mārgaśīrṣo ’ham ṛtūnāṃ kusumākaraḥ
Among the chants of the Sāma Veda I am the Bṛhat Sāman; among metres I am the Gāyatrī; among months I am Mārgaśīrṣa; among seasons, the flower-bringing spring.
WordMeaning
bṛhat-sāmathe “great” Sāma chant
chandasāmamong metres
māsānāmamong months
ṛtūnāmamong seasons
kusumākaraḥflower-maker: spring

The best of each kind. In chapter 10 Kṛṣṇa answers Arjuna’s question “how should I think of you?” with a list: in each class of things, look at its finest member. Mārgaśīrṣa, a month of harvest and clear days, once opened the year in some reckonings. Gītā Jayantī uses this verse as its charter.

Bhagavad Gītā 10.35, text as in the Gītā Press edition used across our Gītā series (checked against holy-bhagavad-gita.org).
Look for the best in each kind.

THE FIELD AND THE NARRATOR

How we hear the Gītā

The story

Mahābhārata, Bhīṣma Parvan (the Gītā forms chapters 23–40 in the critical edition).

The Pāṇḍavas and Kauravas, cousins, have gathered their armies on the plain of Kurukṣetra after every attempt at peace has failed.
In Hastināpura the blind king Dhṛtarāṣṭra, father of the Kauravas, cannot see the battle. The sage Vyāsa grants his charioteer Sañjaya divine sight to see and hear everything on the field.
After ten days of war, Bhīṣma has fallen. Sañjaya returns to the king and the king asks: “What did my sons and the sons of Pāṇḍu do?” That question is the Gītā’s first verse.
So the Gītā reaches us as a report inside a report: Kṛṣṇa speaks to Arjuna; Sañjaya tells it to the king; Vaiśampāyana tells the whole Mahābhārata to King Janamejaya.
Sañjaya closes the Gītā (18.78): wherever Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna are, there are fortune, victory and right conduct.

ZAPPY: The Gītā is told by a narrator?

PROF. BOLT: By a charioteer with borrowed eyes, Zappy. Even the reporter is changed by what he hears; he says so at the end.

We hear it through Sañjaya.

FROM DESPAIR TO “I WILL DO AS YOU SAY”

The arc of the conversation

The story

Bhagavad Gītā, chapters 1, 2, 11 and 18; a summary, not a translation.

Between the armies, Arjuna sees teachers, grandfathers and cousins ready to die. He drops his bow (1.47) and says he will not fight.
Kṛṣṇa answers through seventeen chapters: the self is not killed when the body is (ch. 2); act without clinging to results (ch. 3); know, devote, meditate (chs. 4–12); see the field and its knower (ch. 13); sort the three qualities (chs. 14–17).
In chapter 11 Arjuna asks to see Kṛṣṇa’s universal form, and is shown the viśvarūpa: all worlds and all time in one body. He is terrified and asks for the familiar form back.
Chapter 18 gathers it all and ends with the counsel to take refuge in him (18.66) and the question “has your confusion gone?”
Arjuna answers (18.73): my delusion is gone, my memory restored; I will do as you say. The book ends where the battle begins.

ZAPPY: Seven hundred verses before a fight?

PROF. BOLT: A conversation outside time, Zappy. The tradition says it took far less on the clock than it takes us to read.

Confusion to clarity.

IN THE VERSE

Gītā Dhyānam 1

The verse said before reading
oṃ pārthāya pratibodhitāṃ bhagavatā nārāyaṇena svayaṃvyāsena grathitāṃ purāṇamuninā madhye mahābhāratamadvaitāmṛtavarṣiṇīṃ bhagavatīm aṣṭādaśādhyāyinīmamba tvām anusandadhāmi bhagavadgīte bhavadveṣiṇīm
Om. O Mother, O Bhagavad Gītā, taught to Pārtha (Arjuna) by Lord Nārāyaṇa himself, woven by the ancient sage Vyāsa into the midst of the Mahābhārata, raining the nectar of non-duality, in eighteen chapters: I meditate on you, who free us from the round of birth.
WordMeaning
pārthāyato Pārtha, Arjuna
grathitāmwoven, composed
advaita-amṛtanectar of non-duality
aṣṭādaśa-adhyāyinīhaving eighteen chapters
bhava-dveṣiṇīenemy of rebirth

The Gītā as mother. The Gītā Dhyānam is a set of nine verses chanted before reading; it is printed at the start of many editions and attributed variously to Madhusūdana Sarasvatī or Śrīdhara (traditions differ). Verse 1 addresses the book itself as amba, mother. “Raining the nectar of non-duality” reflects an Advaita reading; Vaiṣṇava schools chant the same verse and read it their own way.

Text as given by shlokam.org (Gita Dhyanam); compare the Gītā Press edition.
The book as mother.

IN THE VERSE

Gītā Dhyānam 4

The Upaniṣads are the cows
sarvopaniṣado gāvo dogdhā gopālanandanaḥpārtho vatsaḥ sudhīr bhoktā dugdhaṃ gītāmṛtaṃ mahat
All the Upaniṣads are the cows; the milker is the cowherd boy, Kṛṣṇa; Arjuna is the calf; the wise one is the drinker; and the milk is the great nectar of the Gītā.
WordMeaning
sarva-upaniṣadaḥall the Upaniṣads
gāvaḥcows
dogdhāthe milker
vatsaḥthe calf
sudhīḥthe wise person
dugdhamthe milk

The best-known image. A cow gives milk when her calf is near; Arjuna’s need drew out the teaching, but the milk is for all who drink. The verse places the Gītā as the essence of the Upaniṣads, which is how the commentators treat it: one of the three foundations (prasthāna-traya) with the Upaniṣads and the Brahma Sūtras.

Text as given in the Gītā Dhyānam (Gītā Māhātmya tradition), via shlokam.org and the samskrita list text.
The calf asks; everyone drinks.

KURUKṢETRA AND JYOTISAR

Where the Gītā is said to have been spoken

The field. Kurukṣetra in Haryana, the “field of the Kurus”, is held to be the battlefield. A circuit of about 134 sacred sites, the 48 Kos Parikramā, surrounds it, with the great tank of Brahma Sarovar at its heart.

Jyotisar. On the Jyotisar lake a banyan on a raised platform is honoured as the offshoot of the tree under which the Gītā was taught. Tradition credits Ādi Śaṅkara with identifying the spot. A marble chariot shows Kṛṣṇa teaching Arjuna, and in 2021 a 40-foot aṣṭadhātu image of the universal form was installed.

The Mahotsav. Haryana holds the International Gītā Mahotsav around Gītā Jayantī, centred on Brahma Sarovar: recitation, Gītā āratī on the water, the 48 Kos pilgrimage and hundreds of craft stalls. The 2016 edition, for example, ran from 6 to 10 December.

Lamps on the water. During the Gītā Dīpotsav, Brahma Sarovar, Sannihit Sarovar and Jyotisar are lined with earthen lamps.

ZAPPY: Is the banyan really the same tree?

PROF. BOLT: Its offshoot, by tradition, Zappy. Saplings from it are planted all over the world, so the tree keeps travelling with the book.

A tree that travels with the book.

UDUPI AND THE GĪTĀ

Writing, reciting and the Gītā Mandir

Kṛṣṇa’s town. Udupi, home of the Kṛṣṇa image installed by Madhvācārya, celebrates Gītā Jayantī with recitation and processions; see our Udupi Śrī Kṛṣṇa guide.

Writing the Gītā. During his fourth Paryāya (January 2024 to January 2026), Sugunendra Tīrtha of the Puttige Maṭha launched the Koti Gītā Lekhana Yajña, inviting a crore people to copy out the 700 verses by hand and offer the books to Kṛṣṇa.

Recitation. On Gītā Jayantī, 11 December 2024, the maṭha held a Gītotsava with a Sahasrakaṇṭha (“thousand-voiced”) Gītā Pārāyaṇa in the Rājāṅgaṇa, preceded by a procession on the Car Street; a later Lakṣa Kaṇṭha (“hundred-thousand-voiced”) recitation at the maṭha drew national attention.

The Gītā Mandir. The Puttige Maṭha’s Gītā Mandir in Udupi holds day-long akhaṇḍa recitations.

ZAPPY: Why copy it by hand?

PROF. BOLT: Your hand is slower than your eyes, Zappy. Each verse has to pass through you on its way to the page.

Copy it, and it copies you.

THE GREAT COMMENTATORS

How the schools read the Gītā

The Gītā is one of the three foundation texts (prasthāna-traya) every Vedānta school had to explain. Traditions differ on its meaning; we give each in its own terms.

🕉️ Śaṅkara (Advaita)

The earliest surviving commentary. The self and Brahman are one; action purifies the mind but knowledge alone liberates. Kṛṣṇa’s “I” points to the one Self.

🪷 Rāmānuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita)

The self is real and distinct yet inseparable from God, like body and soul. Bhakti, loving surrender, is the culmination; 18.66 is the charama śloka, the “final verse”.

🐚 Madhva (Dvaita)

Two works, the Gītā Bhāṣya and the Gītā Tātparya Nirṇaya: God and the self are eternally distinct; devotion with right knowledge leads to Viṣṇu’s grace. Madhva’s home was Udupi.

📜 Jñāneśvar and others

The Marathi Jñāneśvarī (1290 CE) retold the Gītā for ordinary people; later teachers such as Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, Śrīdhara, Tilak and Gandhi wrote their own readings.

ZAPPY: Same verses, different meanings?

PROF. BOLT: Same verses, different keys, Zappy. Each school opens the whole house with its key. Our job is to know there are several.

One text, many keys.

KEEPING GĪTĀ JAYANTĪ

A simple plan for the day

The fast. As it is Mokṣadā Ekādaśī, many keep the Ekādaśī fast, with grains avoided and the fast broken on Dvādaśī; see Keeping the Vow.

Worship. A picture of Kṛṣṇa as Pārthasārathi (“charioteer of Pārtha”), a copy of the Gītā placed beside him and offered flowers, tulasī and a lamp; the Gītā Dhyānam before reading and the Gītā āratī after.

Reading. The full text in one sitting (pārāyaṇa, about three hours aloud), or one chapter with a family: chapter 12 on devotion (20 verses) and chapter 15 on the cosmic tree (20 verses) are the two most often chosen.

Giving. Copies of the Gītā are given to students and libraries; many write out a chapter by hand, following the Udupi example.

ZAPPY: Which chapter should I start with?

PROF. BOLT: Twelve, Zappy. Twenty verses, and they describe the kind of person Kṛṣṇa loves. Then go back to chapter two.

One chapter, read slowly.

THE GĪTĀ IN THE WORLD

From Kurukṣetra to every language

First into English. Charles Wilkins’s translation, published in London in 1785, was the first direct translation of a Sanskrit work into English; versions in French, German and other languages followed.

Everyday companion. In India it has been carried by soldiers, read in prisons, sworn on in courts and recited at funerals; schools hold Gītā recitation contests, and many families give it to a child at the thread ceremony.

A conversation for anyone. Its setting, a person who must act and does not know how, makes it readable outside any one school or country. Teachers stress that it is part of the Mahābhārata and should be read with the story around it. (Interpretation.)

On this site. Every chapter, verse by verse, with a deep-dive page: start at the overview or at chapter 2.

ZAPPY: Is it only for Hindus?

PROF. BOLT: It was spoken to a man who didn’t know what to do, Zappy. That covers most of us.

A book for anyone who must act.

WHAT THE DAY ASKS

Read, reflect, act

Read. The day exists so that the book is opened. Even one verse, understood, is the festival kept.

Reflect. The Gītā repeatedly asks for buddhi, a steady discerning mind (2.41, 2.65). Its teaching is meant to be tested in the reader’s own life, not merely admired.

Act. Its last word is Arjuna’s: “I will do as you say.” The aim is not to leave the field but to act on it rightly, without being ruled by the result (2.47).

Gratitude to teachers. As a festival of a teaching, it is also a day to remember one’s own teachers, the way the Gītā Dhyānam bows to Vyāsa. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing on Gītā Jayantī?

PROF. BOLT: Read 2.47 tonight, Zappy, and do one task tomorrow without checking what you’ll get for it.

The book ends where action begins.
