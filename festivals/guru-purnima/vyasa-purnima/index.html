Vyāsa Pūrṇimā

The Sage Who Arranged the Veda · explained and illustrated

The sage who arranged the Veda

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Vyāsa was, how Gaṇeśa wrote down his epic, and how the day is kept.

WHERE WE ARE

On the full moon of Āṣāḍha, Hindus, Buddhists and Jains honour their teachers. For Hindus it is Guru Pūrṇimā, also called Vyāsa Pūrṇimā, the birthday of the sage Vyāsa, who arranged the Veda and composed the Mahābhārata.

This 3-part series covers Vyāsa, the idea of the guru with its most famous verses, and the start of Cāturmāsya, the rainy-season retreat of the sannyāsins.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why a day for teachers?

PROF. BOLT: Because nothing in the tradition is learned alone. Every chant, every verse came through a teacher.

WHO WAS VYĀSA

Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana

His name. He was born Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana: Kṛṣṇa because he was dark, Dvaipāyana because he was born on an island (dvīpa) in the Yamunā, to the sage Parāśara and Satyavatī.

The arranger. He is called Vyāsa, “the arranger”, for dividing the one Veda into four (Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva) and teaching each to a disciple.

The author. Tradition credits him with the Mahābhārata (including the Gītā), the eighteen Purāṇas and the Brahma Sūtra. He also appears in the Mahābhārata as grandfather of the Kauravas and Pāṇḍavas.

Cirañjīvin. He is counted among the cirañjīvins, those said to live through the ages.

ZAPPY: One man wrote all that?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says so; scholars see many hands across centuries under his name. Either way, “Vyāsa” stands for the tradition of passing knowledge on.

Vyāsa: the arranger of the Veda, the author of the epic.

VYĀSA AND GAṆEŚA

How the Mahābhārata was written down

The story

A well-loved story, found in some versions of the Mahābhārata.

Vyāsa had the whole Mahābhārata in his mind and needed a scribe fast enough to write it.
He asked Gaṇeśa. Gaṇeśa agreed on one condition: Vyāsa must never pause in his dictation.
Vyāsa added his own condition: Gaṇeśa must understand every verse before writing it.
Whenever Vyāsa needed time, he composed a very difficult verse; while Gaṇeśa worked it out, Vyāsa composed the next.
When his pen broke, Gaṇeśa broke off his own tusk and kept writing. Our Gaṇeśa: The Broken Tusk tells it fully.

ZAPPY: So the hard verses were a trick to buy time?

PROF. BOLT: So the story says. A teacher and a student, each testing the other. Very Guru Pūrṇimā.

Vyāsa dictates, Gaṇeśa writes with his tusk.

HOW THE DAY IS KEPT

Honouring teachers

Guru pūjā. Disciples worship their teacher, offering flowers, fruit, clothes and a gift (dakṣiṇā), and touch the teacher’s feet.

Vyāsa pūjā. In monasteries (maṭhas) the head of the order performs worship of Vyāsa and the line of teachers; see Part 3.

Students and music. Schools of music and dance, and students of every kind, honour their teachers on this day.

Other traditions. Buddhists remember the Buddha’s first sermon at Sārnāth on this full moon; Jains remember Mahāvīra’s first disciple.

ZAPPY: Can I honour a school teacher too?

PROF. BOLT: Of course. Anyone who has taught you something true is your guru for that.

Flowers at the teacher’s feet: Guru Pūrṇimā.

DIVIDING THE VEDA

Why Vyāsa is “the arranger”

One into four. Tradition says that at the start of this age the Veda was one great body; seeing that people of the coming age could not hold it all, Vyāsa divided it into four.

Four disciples. He taught the Ṛg Veda to Paila, the Yajur to Vaiśampāyana, the Sāma to Jaimini and the Atharva to Sumantu.

A title. “Vyāsa” is also a title: the Purāṇas name a Vyāsa for each age; Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana is the Vyāsa of ours.

Teachers’ day. Because the whole Vedic tradition flows through his disciples, his birthday became the day of all teachers.

ZAPPY: So every Vedic school traces back to Vyāsa?

PROF. BOLT: Through those four disciples, by tradition. That is why priests honour him first.

One Veda, four disciples: Vyāsa the arranger.

THE MAHĀBHĀRATA

The fifth Veda

The longest poem. The Mahābhārata, attributed to Vyāsa, runs to about a hundred thousand verses, the longest epic poem in the world.

Fifth Veda. It calls itself the fifth Veda: knowledge for everyone, including those not taught the Vedas.

The Gītā inside. The Bhagavad Gītā is part of its Bhīṣma Parva; see our Gītā series · 18 chapters + overview.

His own story. Vyāsa appears in it as grandfather of the Kauravas and Pāṇḍavas, and as a guide at key moments.

ZAPPY: He wrote about his own grandchildren?

PROF. BOLT: Composed it, by tradition. He was both author and character. Very unusual.

The Mahābhārata: the fifth Veda.

THE PURĀṆAS AND THE BRAHMA SŪTRA

The rest of Vyāsa’s work

Eighteen Purāṇas. Tradition credits Vyāsa with the eighteen great Purāṇas, including the Bhāgavata, Viṣṇu, Śiva and Skanda Purāṇas, many quoted on this site.

The Bhāgavata. The Bhāgavata says Vyāsa, still unhappy after all his work, composed it at Nārada’s urging, to sing of Kṛṣṇa’s glory.

Brahma Sūtra. The Brahma Sūtra, the foundation text of Vedānta, is attributed to Bādarāyaṇa, often identified with Vyāsa.

Scholarship. Historians see these works as built by many hands over centuries; the tradition gathers them under Vyāsa’s name.

ZAPPY: Even Vyāsa was unhappy until he wrote about Kṛṣṇa?

PROF. BOLT: So the Bhāgavata says: knowledge alone didn’t satisfy him; devotion did.

Purāṇas and Sūtras: the library of Vyāsa.

THE SAME FULL MOON IN OTHER TRADITIONS

Buddhists and Jains

Buddhists. Buddhists remember this full moon (Āsāḷha Pūjā) as the day the Buddha gave his first sermon at Sārnāth to five disciples.

Vassa. It also begins the Buddhist monks’ rainy-season retreat, like Cāturmāsya.

Jains. Jains remember the day Mahāvīra accepted Gautama Indrabhūti as his first disciple.

One idea. Across traditions, the day honours the meeting of teacher and student.

ZAPPY: The Buddha’s first sermon too?

PROF. BOLT: The same full moon. Different traditions, same honour for the teacher.

One full moon, three traditions of teachers.

HONOURING YOUR OWN TEACHERS

Simple ways

Visit. Visit a teacher, offer flowers or fruit, and touch their feet if that is your custom.

Write. Write to a teacher who shaped you, telling them so.

Learn. Begin or renew a study; many start a new text on Guru Pūrṇimā.

Remember. Remember teachers who have passed; some offer a lamp in their name.

ZAPPY: Can I write to an old school teacher?

PROF. BOLT: Few gifts would mean more to them. That is Guru Pūrṇimā done well.

Visit, write, learn, remember: honour your teachers.
