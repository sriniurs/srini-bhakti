HANUMAD VRATA · THE WIND’S SON · AÑJANĀDRI

Hanumān Jayantī

The Birth of Rāma’s Messenger · explained and illustrated

The leap across the sea

HOW THIS WORKS: Why Hanumān has several birthdays, his birth and the leap at the sun, his great deeds, three verses, and Karnataka’s Hanumad vrata and Añjanādri.

WHERE WE ARE

Hanumān is everybody’s god: the strongest, the humblest, the most learned, the most devoted. Almost every Karnataka village has an Āñjaneya temple, often the oldest shrine in the place, at the boundary or under a great tree. His birthday is kept on different days in different regions, and in Karnataka on the bright thirteenth of Mārgaśīrṣa as the Hanumad vrata.

This article explains why there are several birthdays, tells his birth and childhood as Vālmīki and the Purāṇas tell them (the curse on Añjanā, the wind god, the leap at the sun), his great deeds for Rāma, gives three verses sung to him, and goes to Añjanādri near Hampi, where lakhs of devotees end their Hanumān-mālā vow.

SEVERAL BIRTHDAYS

Why the date differs

No single date in the epic. Vālmīki tells of Hanumān’s birth but gives no calendar date for it. Later Purāṇas, almanacs and regional traditions each settled on their own, so Hanumān has several “birthdays”.

Karnataka. Kannada country keeps the Hanumad vrata on the thirteenth of the bright fortnight of Mārgaśīrṣa, in November or December, with a vow, a fast and a sacred thread. Some families keep Caitra Pūrṇimā too.

Elsewhere. Most of the north keeps Caitra Pūrṇimā, a week after Rāma Navamī; Telugu devotees keep a 41-day dīkṣā ending on Vaiśākha Daśamī; Tamil Nadu and Kerala the new moon of Mārgaḻi when the Mūla star prevails; Odisha the solar new year, Pāṇa Saṅkrānti.

One devotion. Whatever the day, the worship is the same: the Cālīsā or the Sundara Kāṇḍa read, sindūra and oil on the image, garlands, and food for everyone.

ZAPPY: Which one should I keep?

PROF. BOLT: Your family’s, Zappy. In Karnataka, that’s usually the Hanumad vrata in Mārgaśīrṣa.

Many dates, one devotion.

AÑJANĀ’S SON

The birth of Hanumān

The story

Vālmīki, Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa 66, where Jāmbavān reminds Hanumān who he is, and later retellings.

Puñjikasthalā, an apsaras of heaven, was cursed to be born on earth as a vānara; she became Añjanā, wife of the vānara chief Kesarī.
Añjanā longed for a son and prayed, by many tellings to Śiva, for years.
One day, as she stood on a mountain top in human form, the wind god Vāyu embraced her unseen and promised her a son as strong as himself, swift and wise.
In one well-loved telling, a kite snatched a portion of the divine pāyasa from Daśaratha’s sacrifice and dropped it, and Vāyu carried it to Añjanā’s hands: so Hanumān was born of the same gift as Rāma.
She gave birth in a cave on the mountain, and was freed of her curse. The child was Āñjaneya (Añjanā’s son), Māruti and Pavanaputra (the wind’s son).

ZAPPY: So he has two fathers?

PROF. BOLT: Kesarī raised him, Vāyu gave him his power, Zappy. Many tellings also call him a portion of Śiva.

The wind’s son.

THE LEAP AT THE SUN

How Hanumān got his name

The story

Vālmīki, Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa 66; the Purāṇas add the boons.

As a baby, hungry, Hanumān saw the rising sun and took it for a ripe red fruit.
He leapt into the sky after it, flying higher and higher, toward the sun.
Indra, alarmed, hurled his thunderbolt (vajra). It struck the child on the jaw (hanu) and he fell to the mountain: hence Hanumān, “the one with the (marked) jaw”.
Furious, Vāyu withdrew into a cave with his son, and all breath stopped in the world. The gods hurried to make peace.
Brahmā revived the child, and every god gave him a boon: no weapon could kill him, he could change his size at will, he would live long and be wise. A sage’s curse made him forget his powers until someone reminded him, which Jāmbavān does before the leap to Laṅkā.

ZAPPY: He forgot his own powers?

PROF. BOLT: Until he needed them, Zappy. Then one friend reminded him, and he crossed the ocean. A lesson for all of us.

Strength remembered when needed.

IN THE VERSE

Manojavaṃ

The verse of refuge in Rāma’s messenger
manojavaṃ mārutatulyavegaṃjitendriyaṃ buddhimatāṃ variṣṭhamvātātmajaṃ vānarayūthamukhyaṃśrīrāmadūtaṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadye
Swift as the mind, fast as the wind, master of the senses, foremost among the wise, son of the wind, chief of the vānara bands, messenger of Śrī Rāma: in him I take refuge.
WordMeaning
mano-javamswift as the mind
māruta-tulya-vegamfast as the wind
jita-indriyammaster of the senses
buddhimatāṃ variṣṭhambest of the wise
vānara-yūtha-mukhyamleader of the vānara bands
śaraṇaṃ prapadyeI take refuge

Strength and wisdom together. This is the verse most Karnataka children learn first for Hanumān. It praises not only his speed and strength but his self-mastery and intelligence: in Vālmīki, Rāma is struck by how well Hanumān speaks at their first meeting.

Swift as the mind, master of himself.

THE LEAP TO LAṄKĀ

Sundara Kāṇḍa in five

The story

Vālmīki’s Sundara Kāṇḍa, the “beautiful book”, Hanumān’s own.

The search party reached the southern sea. No one could jump a hundred yojanas. Jāmbavān reminded Hanumān of his birth and powers; Hanumān grew to the size of a mountain and leapt from Mahendra hill.
Across the ocean he overcame the serpent-mother Surasā by shrinking, the shadow-catching demoness Siṃhikā by force, and landed in Laṅkā at dusk.
He searched the city by night and found Sītā in the aśoka grove, guarded by rākṣasīs, refusing Rāvaṇa. He showed her Rāma’s ring; she gave him her hair-jewel to take back.
He wrecked the grove, let himself be captured, and spoke to Rāvaṇa’s face. When they set his tail on fire, he leapt from roof to roof and set Laṅkā ablaze.
He flew back with the news: “dṛṣṭā sītā”, Sītā has been seen. Reading the Sundara Kāṇḍa is the commonest way to keep Hanumān Jayantī.

ZAPPY: Why is it called the beautiful book?

PROF. BOLT: Many reasons are given, Zappy. The simplest: it’s the book where hope comes back.

“Sītā has been seen.”

THE MOUNTAIN OF HERBS

Saving Lakṣmaṇa

The story

Yuddha Kāṇḍa; the details vary between the Vālmīki text and later retellings.

In the war, Lakṣmaṇa fell, struck by a terrible weapon, and lay as if dead. Rāma wept beside him.
The physician Suṣeṇa said only the herbs of a Himālayan mountain, the life-giving Sañjīvanī among them, could save him, and they had to arrive before dawn.
Hanumān flew north. At the mountain the herbs hid from him; not knowing which was which, he lifted the whole peak and flew back with it.
The herbs were applied and Lakṣmaṇa rose, and the army rejoiced. In many tellings the mountain was then carried back.
That is why Hanumān is so often shown flying with a mountain in his palm, and why he is the god of healing and protection in Karnataka’s village shrines.

ZAPPY: He didn’t know which herb?

PROF. BOLT: So he brought them all, Zappy. When love is in a hurry, it doesn’t stop to sort.

When in doubt, bring the mountain.

What remembering Hanumān gives
buddhir balaṃ yaśo dhairyaṃnirbhayatvam arogatāajāḍyaṃ vākpaṭutvaṃ cahanūmat smaraṇād bhavet
Intelligence, strength, fame, courage, fearlessness, health, alertness and eloquence come from remembering Hanumān.
WordMeaning
buddhiḥintelligence

A list for students. This verse is a favourite before exams in Karnataka homes: it asks Hanumān for the things a young person needs, and notice that intelligence comes first and strength second. Ajāḍyam, freedom from dullness, is a fine thing to ask for.

Text as on our Hanumān ślokas page.

THE HANUMAD VRATA

Karnataka’s Hanumān day

The day. Mārgaśīrṣa Śukla Trayodaśī, in the cold season. Devotees fast, bathe early, and worship Hanumān at home or in the village Āñjaneya temple.

The thread. The vrata centres on a sacred red thread with thirteen knots, one for the thirteenth tithi, worshipped and then tied on the wrist, to be kept for a year by many.

The worship. The image is anointed with oil and sindūra, decked with betel leaves (vīḷyada ele) and tulasī, and offered vaḍe, pongal, laḍḍu and fruit. The Cālīsā, the Sundara Kāṇḍa or the Hanumān ślokas are recited.

The next day. Mārgaśīrṣa Pūrṇimā follows: Datta Jayantī. Many temples hold a car festival or a procession on the vrata day itself.

ZAPPY: Why oil and sindūra on Hanumān?

PROF. BOLT: A beloved story says he saw Sītā wear sindūra for Rāma’s long life and covered himself in it, Zappy. If a little helps Rāma, a lot must help more.

A thread of thirteen knots.

AÑJANĀDRI AND THE HANUMĀN-MĀLĀ

Lakhs on one hill near Hampi

The hill. Añjanādri, in Gaṅgāvati taluk of Koppaḷa district across the Tuṅgabhadrā from Hampi, is revered in local tradition as Hanumān’s birthplace, in the Kiṣkindhā country of the Rāmāyaṇa. About 575 steps climb to the small white temple on top.

The mālā. Devotees wear a Hanumān-mālā for 11, 21 or 41 days, keeping a simple, disciplined life, then climb the hill around the Hanumad vrata to remove it: the Hanumamālā visarjane. The practice is recent, a few decades old, and now draws lakhs; the district arranges routes and the hill is lit at night.

Other claims. Añjanēri near Nāsik, a hill at Tirumala, Āñjan Dhām in Jharkhand and others also claim his birth. These are devotional traditions; none can be proved, and each is dear to its devotees.

Around. Pampā Sarovara, Ṛṣyamūka and the Hampi temples are all close; see our Kiṣkindhā story.

ZAPPY: Which birthplace is right?

PROF. BOLT: Each has its tradition, Zappy. A god can be born in many hearts.

A hill turned saffron.

HANUMĀN ACROSS THE SOUTH

Vaḍe garlands, butter and giant images

🍩 Vaḍe-mālā

Garlands of hundreds or thousands of vaḍes: famous at Namakkal in Tamil Nadu, where the eighteen-foot Āñjaneya receives lakhs on Hanumat Jayantī, and common in Karnataka temples too.

🧈 Butter

Images are covered in butter (veṇṇe kāppu), which does not melt, devotees say, while Hanumān is pleased.

🌿 Betel leaves

Garlands of betel leaves: one story says Sītā, having no flowers in the aśoka grove, blessed him with a betel-leaf garland.

🗿 Giants

Huge Hanumān images draw pilgrims: Śucīndram in Tamil Nadu, Bengaluru’s Ragigudda and Mahālakṣmī Layout, and the countless hill-top Āñjaneyas of Karnataka.

ZAPPY: Thousands of vaḍes!

PROF. BOLT: And every one goes back to the devotees as prasāda, Zappy. Hanumān shares.

Strength shared as food.

WHAT HANUMĀN MEANS

Strength in service

The servant. Hanumān has every power and uses none for himself. Asked who he is, the tradition has him answer: as a body, I am your servant; as a soul, I am your part; in truth, I am you. Devotion, strength and wisdom in one person. (A well-known verse attributed to Hanumān; textual source uncertain.)

Humility. He forgets his own powers until reminded, and always credits Rāma. Many teachers read this as the secret of his strength. (Interpretation.)

The guardian. At the boundary of the village, Āñjaneya protects; that is why his shrines are so many and so old in Karnataka.

The speaker. Vālmīki has Rāma praise Hanumān’s grammar and diction at their first meeting: the strongest of the vānaras was also the most articulate.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Hanumān Jayantī?

PROF. BOLT: Read one canto of the Sundara Kāṇḍa, Zappy, and do one hard job for someone else without telling anyone.

All strength, no ego.
