HANUMĀN JAYANTĪ

Hanumad vrata · four birthdays

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Hanumān has four birthdays: Karnataka keeps Hanumad vrata in December, Tamil Nadu the new moon of Mārgaḻi, the north the full moon of Caitra, and the Telugu states a day in May after a 41-day vow.

This guide sorts out the dates, tells the birth story, climbs Anjanādri with the Hanumamālā devotees, and visits the vaḍai garlands of Namakkal.

ZAPPY: Why four birthdays?

PROF. BOLT: Different regions read the texts differently, Zappy. Hanumān doesn’t mind more parties.

Hanumān Jayantī celebrates the birth of Hanumān, son of Añjanā and the wind god Vāyu, the great vānara devotee of Rāma. Unusually, regions keep it on different dates: Karnataka on Mārgaśīrṣa śukla trayodaśī as Hanumad vrata (Tuesday 22 December 2026, by calculation); Tamil Nadu and Kerala on the Mārgaḻi new moon with Mūla star (Thursday 7 January 2027); the north on Caitra pūrṇimā (Tuesday 20 April 2027); and the Telugu states on Vaiśākha kṛṣṇa daśamī (Monday 31 May 2027).

In Karnataka the biggest event is on Anjanādri hill near Hampi, where lakhs of devotees end their Hanumamālā vow.

ZAPPY: He tried to eat the sun?

PROF. BOLT: A hungry baby, a red fruit in the sky. Our Hanumān birth guide tells the whole tale.

Añjanā, an apsaras born as a vānara, and her husband Kesarī prayed for a son; Vāyu, the wind, carried to her a portion of divine pāyasa or, in other tellings, of Śiva’s energy, and Hanumān was born. As a baby he leapt at the rising sun, thinking it a ripe fruit; Indra struck him on the jaw (hanu) with his thunderbolt, giving him his name.

When Vāyu withdrew in anger, the gods restored the child and gave him boons of strength, wisdom and long life. Our Hanumān stories tell it in full.

ZAPPY: Thirteen knots?

PROF. BOLT: For the thirteenth tithi, Zappy. The vow is tied, then kept.

In Karnataka, Hanumān’s day is Hanumad vrata, on the thirteenth day of the bright half of Mārgaśīrṣa, in November or December. Devotees keep a vow, fast and worship, and many tie a sacred thread of thirteen knots.

Āñjaneya temples, found in every Karnataka village, are crowded. The next day is Mārgaśīrṣa pūrṇimā, Datta Jayantī.

ZAPPY: Lakhs of people on one hill?

PROF. BOLT: Saffron from top to bottom, Zappy. The district makes route maps for them.

Anjanādri hill, in Gaṅgāvati taluk of Koppal district across the Tuṅgabhadrā from Hampi, is revered in local tradition as Hanumān’s birthplace; about 575 steps lead to the temple at the top.

Devotees wear a Hanumān mālā for 11, 21 or 41 days, then climb the hill around Hanumad vrata to remove it, the Hanumamālā visarjane. The practice began in 2008 with twelve devotees and now draws lakhs; the district publishes route maps and the hill is lit at night.

ZAPPY: Which one is right?

PROF. BOLT: Each has its tradition, Zappy. A god can be born in many hearts.

Several places claim Hanumān’s birth: Anjanādri near Hampi, in the Kiṣkindhā country of the Rāmāyaṇa; Añjanēri near Nashik; a hill at Tirumala, named by the TTD in 2021; Āñjan Dhām in Jharkhand’s Gumla; and others.

These are devotional traditions; none can be proved historically, and devotees of each hold theirs dear.

ZAPPY: Why vaḍai?

PROF. BOLT: Many stories; the best says Rāhu fears Hanumān, and vaḍai is Rāhu-shaped. Mostly, devotees love giving him food.

Tamil Nadu and Kerala keep Hanumat Jayanti on the new moon of Mārgaḻi with Mūla star. Temples garland Hanumān with vaḍai mālai, strings of hundreds of vaḍais, butter (veṇṇai) decorations and betel-leaf garlands. The 18-foot Āñjaneya at Namakkal and the great Hanumān at Śucīndram draw huge crowds.

ZAPPY: Why is Hanumān orange?

PROF. BOLT: Sītā wore sindūr for Rāma’s long life, the story goes. Hanumān covered himself in it, for even longer life!

In the north and west, Hanumān Jayantī falls on Caitra pūrṇimā; many traditions say he was born at sunrise. Devotees offer orange sindūr mixed with oil, recite the Hanumān Cālīsā and the Sundarakāṇḍa, and crowd temples such as Saṅkaṭmocan in Vārāṇasī.

Tuesday and Saturday are his weekly days.

ZAPPY: Forty-one days?

PROF. BOLT: A maṇḍala, Zappy: about six weeks of vows. Same idea as Sabarimala.

In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, devotees take a 41-day dīkṣā starting on Caitra pūrṇimā and ending on Vaiśākha kṛṣṇa daśamī, Hanumān Jayantī, many at the Kondagattu temple in Telangana.

ZAPPY: Do gyms worship Hanumān?

PROF. BOLT: Many akhāḍās keep his picture. Strength in service of good: that’s his kind.

Hanumān is pleased above all by Rāma’s name. Devotees recite the Hanumān Cālīsā, the Saṅkaṭmocan Aṣṭak and the Āñjaneya Daṇḍakam, and offer sindūr, betel leaves, vaḍe, jaggery and gram. Wrestlers and gyms take him as patron.

Our Hanumān section has the Cālīsā, the Aṣṭak and the ślokas verse by verse.

ZAPPY: Is Hanumān still around?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says he lives on, listening wherever the Rāmāyaṇa is recited, Zappy. So keep reciting.

Hanumān is the model of devotion joined to strength: the mightiest of beings, and the humblest servant. He is also a scholar, courageous messenger and protector from fear.

Tradition makes him a ciranjīvī, alive wherever the Rāmāyaṇa is told.
