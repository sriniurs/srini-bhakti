FIRE · COLOUR · PRAHLĀDA, KĀMA AND KṚṢṆA

Holi

Fire, Colour and the Full Moon of Phālguna · explained and illustrated

Fire at night, colour in the morning

HOW THIS WORKS: The full moon of Phālguna, the three stories told in full, the verses behind them, and how Karnataka keeps Kāmana habba, with Braj, Bengal, Goa and beyond.

WHERE WE ARE

On the last full moon of the lunar year, in Phālguna, India first lights a fire and then throws colour. In the north the fire is Holikā’s, the aunt who tried to burn the boy Prahlāda; in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu it is Kāma’s, the god of love whom Śiva burned with his third eye. The next morning belongs to Kṛṣṇa, who coloured Rādhā’s face.

This article tells all three stories in full, gives the verses behind them, and goes region by region: Braj and Barsānā, Bengal’s swing festival, Bagalkoṭ’s Holi Habba and Karnataka’s Kāmaṇṇa, the coast and Goa, with the old history of a spring festival far older than any one of its stories.

PROF. BOLT: To start clean, Zappy. The fire takes the old year’s rubbish; then the colours look brighter.

THE LAST FULL MOON

Phālguna Pūrṇimā and the turn of the year

The end of the year. In the lunar calendar used across Karnataka, Phālguna is the last month; the new year, Ugādi, comes about two weeks after Holi. Holi is the year’s last full-moon feast, a burning of the old before the new begins.

The turn of the season. Winter is over, the rabi harvest is ripening in the north, the palāśa trees are in flame-coloured flower. Many scholars read the fire and the play as an old spring festival, older than any single story now told about it.

The fire and the day after. The bonfire is lit on the Pūrṇimā evening, in a window the almanac chooses so as to avoid bhadrā, a division of the day avoided for beginnings. The colours come the next morning; in parts of Maharashtra and central India they come again on Raṅg Pañcamī, five days later.

Two days, one festival. In Karnataka the evening is the main event (Kāmana huṇṇime, Kāma’s full moon), while the colour play is strongest in the north of the state.

ZAPPY: So Holi says goodbye to the year?

PROF. BOLT: The last full moon, Zappy. Burn the old, laugh, and walk into Ugādi clean.

The year’s last full moon.

A VERY OLD SPRING FESTIVAL

What the old records say

The sūtras. The name appears as Holākā in early ritual literature: the Pūrva Mīmāṃsā sūtras of Jaimini discuss it as a rite kept in the east, and the Kāṭhaka Gṛhya-sūtra mentions a full-moon rite of the same name. What exactly was done then is not clear.

The poets. Kālidāsa’s works know a spring festival of love, and Harṣa’s seventh-century play Ratnāvalī opens with the Madanotsava, the festival of the love-god, where townspeople spray one another with coloured water and powder.

Vijayanagara. Reliefs at Hampi show people squirting coloured water from syringes, the ancestor of today’s pichkārī: Karnataka has carved Holi in stone.

Many stories, one festival. Over time three stories gathered around the same full moon: Prahlāda and Holikā, Śiva and Kāma, Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa. Different regions lead with different ones; all of them fit a festival of fire followed by play.

ZAPPY: The Madanotsava was a festival of the love-god?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, Zappy. And in Karnataka the love-god is still at the centre of Holi. You’ll see why in a few pages.

Older than any one story.

THE BOY WHO WOULD NOT STOP

Prahlāda and his father

The story

The story is told in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 7, chapters 4–8.

Hiraṇyakaśipu, king of the daityas, hated Viṣṇu, who had killed his brother Hiraṇyākṣa. By fierce penance he won a boon that seemed to make him unkillable, and ordered the three worlds to worship him alone.
His little son Prahlāda had heard of Viṣṇu while still in his mother’s womb, from the sage Nārada. At school, sent to learn statecraft, he taught his classmates devotion instead.
Asked by his father what was the best thing he had learned, the boy answered with the nine forms of devotion (next page). The king flew into a rage.
He had the boy attacked with weapons, trampled by elephants, bitten by serpents, poisoned, thrown from a height. Each time Prahlāda remembered Viṣṇu and came to no harm.
At last the king turned to his sister Holikā.

ZAPPY: Why didn’t the boy just pretend?

PROF. BOLT: Because he couldn’t lie about what he loved, Zappy. That’s the whole point of Prahlāda.

Devotion that will not bend.

IN THE VERSE

Bhāgavata 7.5.23

The nine forms of devotion, Prahlāda’s answer
śravaṇaṃ kīrtanaṃ viṣṇoḥ smaraṇaṃ pāda-sevanamarcanaṃ vandanaṃ dāsyaṃ sakhyam ātma-nivedanam
Hearing about Viṣṇu, singing of him, remembering him, serving his feet, worship, salutation, service, friendship, and the offering of one’s whole self.
WordMeaning
śravaṇamhearing
kīrtanamsinging, praising
smaraṇamremembering
pāda-sevanamserving the feet
dāsyamservice
ātma-nivedanamoffering oneself

A child’s syllabus. This is what Prahlāda answers when his father asks for the best of his studies; the next verse adds that whoever practises these nine has learned the highest learning. Many teachers read the bonfire as the test of the ninth, ātma-nivedanam: a boy who has given himself wholly cannot be burned. (Interpretation.)

Text: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.23, BBT edition (prabhupadabooks.com), set in IAST.
Nine ways to love God.

HOLIKĀ AND THE FIRE

Why the bonfire burns

The story

The Bhāgavata itself tells of fire among the king’s attempts but does not name Holikā; her story is told in later Purāṇic retellings and in every north Indian home.

Holikā, the king’s sister, could not be harmed by fire: by a boon, say some tellings, or by a fire-proof cloak, say others.
On the king’s order a great pyre was built. Holikā sat on it with the boy Prahlāda in her lap, and the pyre was lit.
Prahlāda only repeated the name of Viṣṇu. The flames rose.
Her protection failed her, used as it was to kill a child: in the cloak telling, the wind lifted the cloak off her and wrapped it round the boy. Holikā burned; Prahlāda walked out unharmed.
The bonfire on Holi night is Holikā Dahana, “the burning of Holikā”. Soon after, Viṣṇu came out of a pillar as Narasiṃha and ended Hiraṇyakaśipu (read our Narasiṃha article).

ZAPPY: Her boon stopped working?

PROF. BOLT: A gift used for cruelty turns against its owner, Zappy. The tellings agree on that, whatever the details.

The fire knows the difference.

IN THE VERSE

The Holikā verse

Said at the bonfire in north Indian pūjā manuals
asṛkpābhayasantrastaiḥ kṛtā tvaṃ holi bāliśaiḥatas tvāṃ pūjayiṣyāmi bhūte bhūtipradā bhava
O Holī, you were made by foolish people terrified of blood-drinkers; therefore I worship you: O being, be a giver of prosperity.
WordMeaning
asṛkpablood-drinkers, demons
bhaya-santrastaiḥby those struck with fear
bāliśaiḥby the foolish, the childish
pūjayiṣyāmiI shall worship
bhūteO being
bhūti-pradāgiver of prosperity

A surprising prayer. The verse treats the pyre itself as Holī, a being made out of fear, and asks her to turn into a giver of wealth. It hints at the oldest layer of the festival: a fire lit against the dark and the frightening, before any one story was attached. At the fire people circle three or seven times, throw in coconut, new grain and dung-cakes, and take a little ash home.

Text as given in modern Holī pūjā manuals (e.g. dekhopanchang.com). We have not traced it to a single Purāṇa; readings vary.
Fear turned into blessing.

ŚIVA BURNS KĀMA

The story Karnataka tells

The story

Told in the Śiva Purāṇa (Rudra Saṃhitā, Pārvatī Khaṇḍa) and in Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava.

After Satī’s death Śiva sat in deep meditation on the mountain, turned away from the world. Pārvatī, Satī reborn, served him daily, but he did not open his eyes.
The demon Tāraka could be killed only by a son of Śiva. The gods sent Kāma, the god of love, with his sugarcane bow and flower-arrows, and spring itself to help him.
As Pārvatī came near, Kāma loosed his arrow. Śiva stirred, felt desire, and looked for its cause.
He saw Kāma hiding, and from his third eye a flame leapt out and burned the love-god to ash.
Kāma’s wife Rati wept and pleaded. Śiva relented: Kāma would live on, but without a body, as Anaṅga, and be restored to her in a later age (as Pradyumna, son of Kṛṣṇa). Pārvatī won Śiva by her own penance, and their son Skanda killed Tāraka.

ZAPPY: So love was burned?

PROF. BOLT: Lust was, many teachers say: the desire that disturbs. Love comes back, without a body. A fine idea for a spring festival.

Desire burned; love returns.

KĀMANA HABBA IN KARNATAKA

Rati, Kāmaṇṇa and the full moon of Kāma

The names. In Karnataka Holi is Kāmana habba, the festival of Kāma, and the full moon is Kāmana huṇṇime. For days before, children go round collecting wood and dung-cakes for the fire, drumming on the halage and calling out songs, some of them cheeky.

Kāmaṇṇa and Rati. In towns and villages, especially in the north of the state, figures of Kāmaṇṇa (Kāma) and Rati are set up at street corners and worshipped. On the full-moon night Kāmaṇṇa is burned on the bonfire, re-enacting Śiva’s third eye; in some places women sing Rati’s lament.

Bagalkoṭ’s Holi Habba. Bagalkoṭ keeps the most famous Holi in the state: it opens with the Kāma dahana bonfire and then runs for several days of colour, drums and processions through the old town’s lanes.

The coast and the hills. On the coast communities keep the Hōḷi kuṇita, a dance tradition with days of preparation and visits from house to house. Around Mysuru the festival is quieter, often just the fire and a little colour for the children.

ZAPPY: Do we have Holi in Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: Quietly, Zappy. Go north to Dharwad, Bagalkoṭ or Vijayapura and you’ll come home a different colour.

In Karnataka, Kāma burns.

RĀDHĀ’S COLOURED FACE

Kṛṣṇa’s Holi

The story

In Braj, around Mathurā and Vṛndāvana, Holi belongs to Kṛṣṇa. This is the household telling, sung in countless Braj songs rather than told in one scripture.

Little Kṛṣṇa, dark as a rain-cloud, came to his mother Yaśodā with a complaint: why was Rādhā so fair and he so dark?
Yaśodā laughed and said: then go and colour her face any colour you like.
So Kṛṣṇa went with his friends to Rādhā’s village and smeared colour on her face. Rādhā and her friends answered with colour and water of their own.
The whole of Vraja joined in: cowherds and milkmaids, with pichkārīs of coloured water and fistfuls of red powder (gulāl), with songs and drums.
Braj still plays it for a week or more. In Barsānā, Rādhā’s village, the women of the town playfully beat the men of Kṛṣṇa’s village Nandgāon with sticks (Laṭhmār Holi), and Vṛndāvana’s temples hold a Holi of flowers.

ZAPPY: Women beating men with sticks?

PROF. BOLT: Playfully, Zappy, against padded shields. Kṛṣṇa’s friends teased Rādhā’s village; the village answers every year.

Colour as love’s play.

HOLI ACROSS INDIA

Swings, songs and the month-long Śigmo

🎪 Bengal and Odisha

Dol Jātrā: images of Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa (in Odisha, of Jagannātha’s representatives) are carried on decorated swings, with coloured powder. The full moon is also Gaura Pūrṇimā, the birthday of Caitanya.

🥁 Goa and the Konkan

Śigmo (Śimgā): a spring festival of about a month with folk dances, drums and processions of floats.

🌕 Manipur

Holi merges with Yaoshang, five days of sports, songs and the thabal chongba dance under the full moon.

🔥 Tamil Nadu and Andhra

Kāman paṇḍigai and the Kāmudu fire: Kāma dahana, Rati’s lament in song, and pantomimes of the love-god before his effigy is burned.

ZAPPY: So Bengal swings while Karnataka burns?

PROF. BOLT: And Braj beats, Goa dances and Manipur plays football, Zappy. One full moon, many temperaments.

One moon, many festivals.

THE COLOURS

Where they came from, and why

From the forest. The old colours came from plants: the flame-orange flowers of the palāśa (ṭesū) soaked overnight for orange water; turmeric for yellow; kuṅkuma and red sandal for red; henna and leaves for green; indigo for blue.

Why colour at all. The Kṛṣṇa story gives the reason most people know. Many also read the colour as the levelling of the festival: for one morning rich and poor, old and young, are the same colour. (Interpretation.)

A season’s medicine. Traditional writers point out that several of the plant colours, turmeric and neem among them, were valued as remedies at the turn of the season. Treat this as tradition rather than medicine.

Today’s caution. Some cheap commercial colours have been found to contain harmful dyes and metals. Many families now go back to natural colours, keep colour out of eyes and mouths, and ask before colouring anyone who has not come to play.

ZAPPY: Can I make colours at home?

PROF. BOLT: Turmeric with a little flour for yellow, beetroot for pink, soaked palāśa flowers for orange. Grandmother’s chemistry set, Zappy.

Colours from the garden.

WHAT HOLI MEANS

Fire, then play

The fire. Each of the stories burns something: Holikā’s cruelty, Kāma’s disturbing desire, the old year’s rubbish. Many teachers read the bonfire as the burning of what we do not want to carry into the new year. (Interpretation.)

The colour. Then comes play without rank: colour the friend you quarrelled with, the elder, the neighbour. The north’s saying “bura na māno, Holī hai”, don’t take it badly, it’s Holi, is a licence to forgive.

The devotee. Prahlāda gives the festival its spine: devotion that fear cannot bend. Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa give it its joy. Śiva and Rati give it a lesson about desire.

Kindness. Holi’s licence is for play, not for forcing. Colour those who want it, spare the animals, keep water use sensible, and leave the old and the unwilling in peace.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Holi?

PROF. BOLT: Throw one thing you don’t need into the fire, Zappy, then put colour on the face of someone you’ve been cross with. Holi done.

Burn the old, forgive, then play.
