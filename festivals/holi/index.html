HOLI

Kāmana habba · colours, fire and spring

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the full moon of Phālguna, India lights a fire and then throws colour. In the north it is Holi, for Prahlāda and for Kṛṣṇa; in Karnataka it is Kāmana habba, when Śiva burns the god of desire.

This guide covers the date, the three great stories, Karnataka’s Rati and Kāmaṇṇa from Bagalkot to Hampi, Braj, Bengal and Goa, the history, and how to play safely.

ZAPPY: Is Holi a North Indian festival?

PROF. BOLT: Its colours are, mostly. Its fire is everywhere, Zappy, and Karnataka has its own version.

Holi falls on Phālguna pūrṇimā, the last full moon of the lunar year, in late February or March. On the evening of the full moon a bonfire is lit; the next morning people throw coloured powder and water, visit friends and share sweets. In 2027 the bonfire is on Sunday 21 March and the colours on Monday 22 March.

Three stories feed it: Prahlāda surviving the fire that burned Holikā; Kṛṣṇa colouring Rādhā; and, in the south, Śiva burning Kāma, the god of desire. In Karnataka it is Kāmana habba.

ZAPPY: Why does the bonfire wait for a good time?

PROF. BOLT: Bhadrā is a karaṇa avoided for beginnings, Zappy. The almanac gives the window.

Holikā Dahana is lit on the evening of Phālguna pūrṇimā, at a time chosen to avoid bhadrā, an inauspicious karaṇa; almanacs publish the window. The colours follow next morning.

In Braj, around Mathurā and Vrindāvan, Holi runs for a week or more; in parts of Maharashtra and the north, colours are played again on Raṅg Pañcamī, five days later.

STORY 1: PRAHLĀDA AND HOLIKĀ

The fire that would not burn the boy
FatherHiraṇyakaśipu, the demon king who demanded worship
SonPrahlāda, devoted to Viṣṇu
AuntHolikā, who could not be burned (by a boon or a cloak)
PlotShe sits in a fire with Prahlāda in her lap
OutcomeHolikā burns; Prahlāda walks out unharmed
AfterNarasiṃha slays Hiraṇyakaśipu (our Daśāvatāra guide)

ZAPPY: Why did her boon fail?

PROF. BOLT: Because she used it for evil, many tellings say. The cloak flew from her to the boy.

The demon king Hiraṇyakaśipu demanded to be worshipped, but his son Prahlāda worshipped only Viṣṇu. After many failed attempts to kill the boy, the king’s sister Holikā, who could not be burned, sat in a fire with Prahlāda in her lap.

Holikā burned and Prahlāda walked out unharmed; in some tellings, her protective cloak flew from her to him. The bonfire re-enacts her burning. Narasiṃha later slew Hiraṇyakaśipu, as our Daśāvatāra guide tells.

STORY 2: RĀDHĀ AND KṚṢṆA

The blue boy and the colours

AdviceYaśodā laughs: colour her face any colour you like
PlayHe smears colour on Rādhā and the gopīs

Phūlōṅ kīHoli of flowers in Vrindāvan temples

ZAPPY: Women beating men with sticks?

PROF. BOLT: Playfully, Zappy, with padded shields. It re-enacts Kṛṣṇa’s friends teasing Rādhā’s village.

In Braj, Holi is Kṛṣṇa’s festival. Dark-skinned Kṛṣṇa, the story goes, complained to his mother that Rādhā was fair; Yaśodā laughed and told him to colour her face as he liked. So he did, and colour-play began.

At Barsānā, Rādhā’s village, women playfully beat the men of Nandgāon, Kṛṣṇa’s village, with sticks in Lathmār Holi; Vrindāvan’s temples hold a Holi of flowers.

STORY 3: ŚIVA BURNS KĀMA

Kāma Dahana
SettingŚiva in deep meditation after Satī’s death
NeedThe gods need a son of Śiva and Pārvatī
KāmaThe god of love shoots his flower-arrow at Śiva
Third eyeŚiva opens it; Kāma is burned to ash
RatiKāma’s wife grieves and prays
BoonKāma lives on, bodiless (Anaṅga), visible to Rati alone

ZAPPY: So love was burned?

PROF. BOLT: Lust was, many say: desire that disturbs. Love comes back, without a body. A fine idea for a festival.

In the Purāṇas, Śiva sat in deep meditation after the death of Satī. The gods needed a son of Śiva and Pārvatī to defeat the demon Tāraka, so Kāma, the god of love, shot his flower-arrow at Śiva. Śiva opened his third eye and burned Kāma to ashes.

Kāma’s wife Rati grieved and prayed; Śiva promised that Kāma would live on, bodiless (Anaṅga), visible to Rati alone. Many read the burning as the burning of lust, not of love.

ZAPPY: Do we have Holi in Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: Quietly, Zappy. North Karnataka, Dharwad, Bagalkot, Haveri, does it loudest.

In Karnataka the full moon is Kāmana huṇṇime and the festival Kāmana habba. Images of Kāmaṇṇa and Rati are set up in streets and squares; children beat the halage drum and dance before them; at night Kāmaṇṇa’s effigy is burned. Colours follow, especially in north Karnataka.

In the Mādhva tradition the day is explained as Rudra, ruler of the mind, burning Manmatha.

NORTH KARNATAKA

DharwadKāmaṇṇa and Rati in every locality
HampiColours before Virūpākṣa, where Śiva is said to have burned Manmatha
TouristsHundreds of foreign visitors join at Hampi

ZAPPY: Make them laugh for a prize?

PROF. BOLT: Lakhs of rupees, Zappy! Holding a straight face as Kāma is a serious skill.

North Karnataka keeps it loudest. Bagalkot’s Holi habba lasts four days and is the town’s biggest event; Dharwad installs Kāmaṇṇa and Rati in nearly every locality; Navalgund comes alive.

Ranebennur in Haveri has a unique tradition, in its 67th year in 2026: two men sit for hours as living Rati and Manmatha while the crowd tries to make them laugh, for a large prize. At Hampi, where Śiva is said to have burned Manmatha, thousands, including many foreign tourists, play colours before the Virūpākṣa temple.

ZAPPY: Why does the south focus on Kāma?

PROF. BOLT: Older layers, perhaps. Medieval spring festivals worshipped Kāma; Holikā and Kṛṣṇa came to dominate in the north later.

In Tamil Nadu, Kāman Paṇḍigai worships painted images of Kāma and Rati; songs tell Rati’s grief, and sandal paste is offered to soothe Kāma’s burns. The Telugu states keep bonfires, and colour-play is spreading in southern cities.

Historians note that medieval spring festivals across India were festivals of Kāma, with swings and worship under flowering trees; in the north, the Holikā and Kṛṣṇa stories later took the lead.

ZAPPY: Swings for gods?

PROF. BOLT: Dol means swing. Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa are rocked in flower-decked swings, then everyone plays with colour.

Bengal and Odisha keep Dol Jātrā, rocking Rādhā and Kṛṣṇa on flower-decked swings and playing with abir. Manipur merges Holi with Yaosang; Goa keeps Śigmo, a longer spring festival; Maharashtra lights the Holi fire and plays colours on Raṅg Pañcamī.

Sikhs celebrate Hola Mohalla, a festival of martial displays begun by Guru Gobind Singh, the day after Holi.

ZAPPY: Is it older than the Holikā story?

PROF. BOLT: The spring festival is old; which story leads has changed with time and place.

Spring festivities appear in the 7th-century play Ratnāvalī by King Harṣa. Mughal courts sometimes celebrated Holi; Aurangzeb banned it in 1665.

The name is usually linked to Holikā; some connect it with holā, roasted green grain, which is still roasted in the bonfire.

ZAPPY: Can I throw colour at anyone?

PROF. BOLT: Only at those who want it, Zappy. “Don’t mind, it’s Holi” isn’t a licence to be unkind.

Some commercial colours contain industrial dyes and metals that harm the skin and eyes; malachite green has been linked to eye irritation. Plant-based colours, from turmeric, marigold, beetroot and flowers, are safer.

Play only with people who want to play, go easy on water in dry years, and leave animals out of it.

THE MEANING

Burn, then colour
FireBurn the old: grudges, lust, ego
ColourThen play: differences of rank and caste blur
SpringThe world puts on colour; so do we
ForgiveA day to mend quarrels
DevotionPrahlāda: faith survives fire
LoveKṛṣṇa and Rādhā: love sees past colour

ZAPPY: Burn first, then play?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the order, Zappy. Let go of the old, then celebrate.

Holi’s order is its lesson: first burn, then play. The fire takes the old year’s rubbish, grudges and desires; the colours erase, for a day, the lines of rank, age and caste.

Prahlāda shows faith surviving fire; Kṛṣṇa shows love seeing past colour; Kāma’s burning shows desire mastered and love made subtle.
