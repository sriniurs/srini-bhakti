Kārtika Pūrṇimā

Tripurāri and the River of Lamps · explained and illustrated

One arrow, a river of lamps

HOW THIS WORKS: Śiva burns the three cities, Vārāṇasī’s Dev Dīpāvalī, Matsya and the river lamps, and closing the month.

WHERE WE ARE

The month ends in a full moon of light. Kārtika Pūrṇimā celebrates Śiva as Tripurāri, destroyer of the three demon cities, and in Vārāṇasī it is Dev Dīpāvalī, “the gods’ Dīpāvalī”, when the ghāṭs of the Gaṅgā blaze with lamps.

It is also remembered as the day of Viṣṇu’s first avatāra, Matsya, and in many places as the climax of the month’s lamp-offering.

ZAPPY: Another Dīpāvalī?

TRIPURĀRI

Śiva and the three cities

The story

This victory is told in the Mahābhārata and the Śiva Purāṇa.

Three demon brothers, sons of Tāraka, built three flying cities of gold, silver and iron, which could be destroyed only by a single arrow when all three lined up.
From their cities they tormented the worlds.
The gods built Śiva a chariot of the whole cosmos: the earth its body, sun and moon its wheels, Mount Meru his bow, Viṣṇu his arrow.
When the three cities came into line, Śiva loosed the one arrow and burnt them all.
He became Tripurāri, “enemy of the three cities”; the gods lit lamps in joy on the full moon of Kārtika.

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu was the arrow?

PROF. BOLT: In the telling, yes: all the gods became parts of Śiva’s weapon. A whole universe working together.

One arrow, three cities: Śiva as Tripurāri.

DEV DĪPĀVALĪ AT VĀRĀṆASĪ

The gods’ festival of lamps

Lamps on every step. On Kārtika Pūrṇimā the ghāṭs of Vārāṇasī are lined with clay lamps from end to end, a sight that draws visitors from across the world.

The story. Tradition says the gods descend to Kāśī to bathe in the Gaṅgā and celebrate Śiva’s victory over Tripura with lamps.

Gaṅgā ārati. The evening ārati at the ghāṭs is especially grand.

Kāśī Viśvanātha. Our Kāśī Viśvanāthāṣṭakam sings of Kāśī’s Lord.

ZAPPY: The gods come to Kāśī?

PROF. BOLT: That is the tradition. Seeing the ghāṭs that night, it is easy to believe.

Vārāṇasī’s ghāṭs ablaze: Dev Dīpāvalī.

MATSYA AND MORE

Other memories of the full moon

Matsya. Some traditions mark Kārtika Pūrṇimā as the day Viṣṇu took his first avatāra, Matsya, the fish who saved Manu and the Vedas from the flood. Our Daśāvatāra series · 10 avatāras + overview tells it.

Floating lamps. In Odisha and elsewhere, little boats with lamps are floated on rivers and seas (Boita Bandāṇa), remembering ancient sea voyages.

Bathing. A dip in a holy river on this full moon is especially praised: at Puṣkar a great fair is held.

Guru Nānak. The Sikh community celebrates Guru Nānak’s birth (Gurpurab) on this full moon.

ZAPPY: So many things on one day!

PROF. BOLT: Full moons gather festivals. This one gathers Śiva, Viṣṇu, rivers and lamps.

Matsya, river lamps, holy dips: a full moon of many meanings.

CLOSING THE MONTH

From Kārtika to Mārgaśīrṣa

The last lamps. Those who kept a lamp vow all month offer their final lamps and complete the vow with charity.

Kārtika snāna ends. The dawn bathing ends with a final bath on the full moon.

Into winter. Mārgaśīrṣa follows, the month Kṛṣṇa calls the best (Gītā 10.35), and with it Dhanurmāsa; see our Dhanurmāsa series · 4 articles.

ZAPPY: What do I do with my Kārtika vow now?

PROF. BOLT: Finish it with a gift of food or a lamp to someone. Then carry one small habit forward.

The lamps of Kārtika lead into winter’s light.

THE PUṢKAR FAIR

Brahmā’s lake

Brahmā’s lake. Puṣkar in Rajasthan has one of the very few temples of Brahmā and a sacred lake.

Bathing. Bathing in the lake on Kārtika Pūrṇimā is held especially holy.

The fair. The Puṣkar fair around the full moon is famous for its camel and livestock trading.

Pilgrims and traders. Devotion and commerce together, as at many old Indian fairs.

ZAPPY: A camel fair on a holy day?

PROF. BOLT: Old India mixed pilgrimage and trade naturally. The lake is the reason; the fair grew around it.

Puṣkar: Brahmā’s lake and a great fair.

BOITA BANDĀṆA

Odisha’s paper boats

Ancient voyages. In Odisha, Kārtika Pūrṇimā remembers the sādhabas, merchants who sailed to Bali and Java in the old days.

Boats at dawn. Before dawn, people float little boats of banana bark or paper with lamps on rivers and ponds.

Bali Yātrā. A great fair at Cuttack, Bali Yātrā, celebrates this maritime past.

A link to the sea. Lamps on water: Kārtika’s light carried outward.

ZAPPY: Merchants sailing to Bali?

PROF. BOLT: Odisha’s ancient seafarers did. The festival remembers them with tiny boats.

Tiny boats, ancient voyages.

COMPLETING THE MONTH’S VOWS

Udyāpana

Udyāpana. A vow kept for the month is closed with an udyāpana: a final worship, often a homa, and feeding guests or brahmins.

Gifts. Lamps, cloth, food and dakṣiṇā are given.

For lamp vows. Those who lit lamps all month may give lamps to a temple.

Gratitude. The closing is thanksgiving for having kept the month.

ZAPPY: Every vow needs a closing?

PROF. BOLT: A good vow has a beginning, middle and end. The udyāpana is the end.

Every vow completed with thanks.

THE MONTH IN ONE THOUGHT

Light in the dark season

The dark half of the year. Kārtika comes as nights lengthen and winter approaches.

Answer with light. Its whole practice is light: lamps at dusk, the dawn bath, the flame on Aruṇācala, the ghāṭs of Kāśī.

Both gods. Viṣṇu as Dāmodara, Śiva as the column of fire: the same light.

Carry it on. The tradition hopes one small habit (an evening lamp) continues through the year.

ZAPPY: So Kārtika is about light?

PROF. BOLT: In one word, yes. The rest is detail.

Kārtika in one word: light.
