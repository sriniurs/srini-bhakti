Kārtika Māsa

The Month of Lamps · explained and illustrated

The month of lamps

HOW THIS WORKS: Why Kārtika is the holiest month, the dawn bath, giving light and the sky lamp, and the vows of the month.

WHERE WE ARE

After Dīpāvalī comes a whole month of lamps. Kārtika, the eighth lunar month, is counted by many traditions as the holiest month of the year, dear to both Viṣṇu and Śiva. People rise before dawn to bathe, light lamps at temples and riverbanks, and keep vows through the month.

This 7-part series covers Kārtika: the month itself, its great hymn the Dāmodarāṣṭakam, Śiva’s Mondays and Karnataka’s lamp festivals, the Kārttikai Dīpam of Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, Tulasī and her wedding, and Kārtika Pūrṇimā.

ZAPPY: A whole month of festival?

PROF. BOLT: A whole month of light. Every evening is a small Dīpāvalī.

THE HOLIEST MONTH

Why Kārtika is special

Praised in the Purāṇas. The Skanda and Padma Purāṇas praise Kārtika above all months; a popular verse says there is no month like Kārtika, no age like Satya, no scripture like the Veda.

For Viṣṇu. Vaiṣṇavas worship Kṛṣṇa as Dāmodara, “the one with a rope round his belly”, through the month (Part 2).

For Śiva. Śaivas keep its Mondays (Somavāras) and light lamps in Śiva temples (Part 3); Kārtika Pūrṇimā celebrates his victory over the three cities (Part 7).

For both. The month joins the two traditions in one practice: light offered in the dark of the year.

ZAPPY: So both Śaivas and Vaiṣṇavas love it?

PROF. BOLT: It is one of the few months both claim with equal joy.

Kārtika: the month both Viṣṇu and Śiva share.

THE DAWN BATH

Kārtika snāna

Before sunrise. Throughout Kārtika many devotees bathe before dawn, ideally in a river or tank, reciting the names of God.

Why dawn. The hour before sunrise (brāhma muhūrta) is counted as the purest; in the cold of early winter the bath is also a discipline.

At home. Those who cannot reach a river bathe at home, with a prayer remembering the sacred rivers.

Then the lamp. After the bath, a lamp is lit at the tulasī or the home shrine.

ZAPPY: In the cold?

PROF. BOLT: That is part of it. A small hardship, cheerfully offered.

Up before dawn, bathe, pray, light a lamp.

GIVING LIGHT

Dīpa-dāna and the sky lamp

Dīpa-dāna. The central practice of the month is the gift of light: lamps offered to Viṣṇu, Śiva, the tulasī, temples, riverbanks and crossroads.

The sky lamp. Many homes hang an ākāśa dīpa, a lamp raised high on a pole, every evening of the month, said to light the way for departed ancestors.

Temples. Temples across Karnataka hold dīpotsavas, festivals of thousands of lamps, through the month (Part 3).

Why light. Light in the dark half of the year is the simplest prayer: may there be light, outside and within.

ZAPPY: Lamps for the ancestors?

PROF. BOLT: So the tradition says: the high lamp shows them the way. It is also a lovely way to remember them.

Light given every evening: dīpa-dāna.

VOWS OF THE MONTH

Kārtika vrata

Simple living. Many keep a vow for the month: eating once a day, avoiding certain foods, sleeping on the floor, or reading a scripture daily.

Reading. The Bhāgavatam, the Gītā, the Kārtika Māhātmya or the Dāmodarāṣṭakam are read daily.

Charity. Giving lamps, food and warm clothing in the coming cold is praised.

Cāturmāsya ends. The four-month vows taken on Devaśayanī are completed on Prabodhinī Ekādaśī in this month (Part 6; see also our Ekādaśī series · 28 articles).

ZAPPY: Which vow should I take?

PROF. BOLT: One you can keep for thirty days. A lamp every evening is a perfect start.

A small vow kept every day of the month.

THE KĀRTIKA MĀHĀTMYA

What the texts say

A whole praise-text. The Skanda Purāṇa (Vaiṣṇava Khaṇḍa) and the Padma Purāṇa (Uttara Khaṇḍa) each contain a Kārtika Māhātmya, chapters praising the month, its bath, its lamps and its vows, with many stories of devotees blessed.

Viṣṇu’s month. These texts call Kārtika most dear to Viṣṇu, and say that even small acts (a lamp, a tulasī leaf, a dawn bath) carry great merit in it.

Read in the month. Many families read a chapter a day through Kārtika, as with the Devī Māhātmya in Navarātri.

Stories inside. They include the tales of Satyabhāmā, of Dharmadatta and the demoness Kalahā, and many others told to encourage the month’s practices.

ZAPPY: A whole book praising one month?

PROF. BOLT: Two of them, in two Purāṇas. That tells you how highly the month was held.

The Kārtika Māhātmya: a book in praise of the month.

DHARMADATTA AND KALAHĀ

A Kārtika story

The meeting. In a story of the Padma Purāṇa’s Kārtika Māhātmya, the pious brahmin Dharmadatta, on his way to a Kārtika lamp worship, met a terrifying spirit: Kalahā, a woman who had lived her life in quarrels and cruelty.

Water with tulasī. Frightened, he sprinkled her with water that held tulasī leaves, chanting Viṣṇu’s names.

Memory returns. At once her sins began to fall away, she remembered her past life, and she begged for release.

Merit shared. Dharmadatta gave her half the merit of his Kārtika vows, and she was freed. The story shows how far the month’s small practices reach.

ZAPPY: Half his merit to a ghost?

PROF. BOLT: Like the Ekādaśī stories: the good done in Kārtika is meant to be shared.

A Kārtika vow shared with a lost soul.

FOOD IN KĀRTIKA

What many families avoid

Simpler eating. Many keep Kārtika vegetarian and simple; some give up onion, garlic, aubergine, or certain pulses for the month.

One meal. Some eat once a day, or avoid eating after sunset.

Damodara’s offerings. Vaiṣṇava households prepare sweets for Dāmodara, especially on Ekādaśīs and the full moon.

Family rules. These practices vary widely; they are vows chosen, not laws.

ZAPPY: Why give up particular vegetables?

PROF. BOLT: Custom, and the idea of offering something you’d miss. The specific items differ by family.

Simple food for a holy month.

KĀRTIKA IN KARNATAKA

Lamps, temples and fairs

Dīpotsavas. Temples across the state hold lamp festivals in Kārtika, from small village shrines to great kṣetras (Part 3).

Lakṣa Dīpotsava. Dharmasthaḷa’s festival of a hundred thousand lamps draws visitors from across the state; see our Tulu Nadu collection · 28 articles.

Śiva and Viṣṇu. Śiva temples are crowded on Kārtika Mondays; Viṣṇu temples keep Dāmodara worship and Tulasī Vivāha.

At home. The ākāśa dīpa on a pole and the evening lamp at the tulasī kaṭṭe are seen across old Mysuru.

ZAPPY: So Karnataka keeps both sides of Kārtika?

PROF. BOLT: Both, fully: Śiva’s Mondays and Viṣṇu’s lamps.

Karnataka’s Kārtika: lamps for Śiva and Viṣṇu alike.
