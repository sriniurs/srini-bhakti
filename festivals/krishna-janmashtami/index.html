KṚṢṆA JANMĀṢṬAMĪ

Gōkulāṣṭamī · born at midnight

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

At midnight on the eighth night of the waning moon, Kṛṣṇa is born in Kaṃsa’s prison. Karnataka draws his tiny footprints into every home, fills plates with uṇḍe and cakkuli, and offers water to the moon; Udupi breaks pots of curd while tiger dancers fill the streets.

This guide explains why there are often two dates, tells the birth story, and visits Udupi, Mumbai, Mathurā and Guruvāyūr.

Śrī Kṛṣṇāya namaḥ,

ZAPPY: Two dates again!

PROF. BOLT: Two rules, Zappy. One looks for aṣṭamī at midnight, the other also wants the Rohiṇī star.

Kṛṣṇa Janmāṣṭamī celebrates Kṛṣṇa’s birth at midnight on the eighth day of the dark fortnight: Śrāvaṇa by Karnataka’s amānta reckoning, Bhādrapada in the north. In 2027 Smārta households keep the night of Tuesday 24 August, and Vaiṣṇava temples Wednesday 25 August.

Karnataka knows it as Gōkulāṣṭamī: little rice-flour footprints, uṇḍe and cakkuli, water offered to the moon. Udupi celebrates with Viṭṭal Piṇḍi, and Maharashtra with Dahī Haṇḍī.

ZAPPY: Who warned Kaṃsa?

PROF. BOLT: A voice from the sky at Devakī’s wedding, in the Bhāgavata. Kaṃsa spent years trying to beat a prophecy.

In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Kaṃsa, tyrant of Mathurā, hears a voice warning that the eighth son of his cousin Devakī will kill him. He imprisons Devakī and her husband Vasudeva and kills their children. Kṛṣṇa, the eighth, is born at midnight.

The chains fall away and the guards sleep; Vasudeva carries the baby across the flooded Yamunā, sheltered by the serpent Śeṣa, to Gokula, where Nanda and Yaśodā raise him, and brings back Yaśodā’s newborn daughter.

THE DATE

Tithi and star
TithiAṣṭamī of the dark fortnight
StarRohiṇī, the star of his birth
Smārta ruleAṣṭamī at midnight (niśītha)
Vaiṣṇava ruleAṣṭamī at sunrise; Rohiṇī preferred
SolarKerala and Udupi: Rohiṇī in the solar month Siṃha (Chiṅgam)
2027Udupi: 24–25 August

ZAPPY: Which is right?

PROF. BOLT: Both, Zappy. Families and temples follow their tradition. Our pañcāṅga guide explains the rules.

Kṛṣṇa was born on aṣṭamī under the Rohiṇī star. Smārta tradition keeps the day on which aṣṭamī prevails at midnight; Vaiṣṇava tradition, followed by ISKCON and many temples, gives more weight to aṣṭamī at sunrise and to Rohiṇī, so it is often a day later.

In Kerala and at Udupi, the festival is set by Rohiṇī in the solar month of Siṃha (Chiṅgam), so it can differ from the lunar date, in some years by weeks. In 2027 Udupi keeps 24–25 August.

ZAPPY: Why footprints?

PROF. BOLT: So little Kṛṣṇa walks into the house, Zappy. Every home wants him to visit.

In Karnataka homes, little footprints are drawn in rice flour from the door to the pūjā room, as if baby Kṛṣṇa had walked in. Many fast until midnight, when an image of Kṛṣṇa is rocked in a cradle and worshipped.

At moonrise, many offer arghya, water, to the moon, which tradition links to Kṛṣṇa’s lunar dynasty. Children are dressed as Kṛṣṇa and Rādhā, and Haridāsa songs are sung.

ZAPPY: Why so many snacks?

PROF. BOLT: He was a butter thief who loved treats. The kitchen celebrates for him.

Gōkulāṣṭamī is famous for its snacks: cakkuli, crunchy spirals; kōḍubaḷe, rings; and uṇḍe, laddus of many kinds. Fresh butter, curd and avalakki (beaten rice, recalling Kucēla’s gift) are offered, and plates go round to neighbours.

ZAPPY: Tigers at a Kṛṣṇa festival?

PROF. BOLT: Udupi’s pili-vēṣa dancers, painted as tigers. It’s their festival as much as anyone’s.

At Udupi, whose Kṛṣṇa image was installed by Madhvācārya about 800 years ago, the festival lasts two days. After midnight worship and arghya, the next day brings Viṭṭal Piṇḍi, or Śrī Kṛṣṇa Līlotsava: a clay image of Kṛṣṇa is taken in procession with the utsava images and immersed in Madhva Sarovara.

Teams break pots of curd hung high (mosaru kuḍike), and dancers painted as tigers, hulivēṣa or pili-vēṣa, fill the streets.

ZAPPY: How high do they go?

PROF. BOLT: Some towers have nine tiers, Zappy. Helmets and harnesses are now required in many places.

In Maharashtra, the day after Janmāṣṭamī, Gopāl Kālā, brings Dahī Haṇḍī: teams of Govindās build human pyramids, sometimes nine tiers high, to break a pot of curd hung high above the street, re-enacting Kṛṣṇa’s butter raids. Courts and the state have set safety rules.

ZAPPY: Do they stay awake all night there?

PROF. BOLT: Midnight is the climax. Bells, conches, and the shout “Nanda ke ānanda bhayo!”

In Mathurā, the birthplace temple stands where the prison is said to have been; Vṛndāvan’s temples stage Rāsalīlā plays, and Dvārakā, Purī and ISKCON temples worldwide celebrate at midnight with conches, bells and kīrtan.

ZAPPY: Guruvāyūr is a Kṛṣṇa temple?

PROF. BOLT: One of the greatest, Zappy. Unnikkaṇṇan, the baby Kṛṣṇa.

In Kerala the festival is Aṣṭamī Rōhiṇī, and Guruvāyūr, home of the child Kṛṣṇa Unnikkaṇṇan, is crowded; children dressed as Kṛṣṇa join shobhā yātrās. Tamil homes draw footprints, make sweets and play uriyaḍi, pot-breaking games.

ZAPPY: A god who steals butter?

PROF. BOLT: That’s why everyone loves him, Zappy. He makes holiness playful.

Kṛṣṇa is born at midnight in a prison: light where it is darkest. As the butter-stealing child, the cowherd, Rādhā’s beloved and the teacher of the Gītā, he makes God near, playful and loved.
