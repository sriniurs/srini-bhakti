MAHĀŚIVARĀTRI

The great night of Śiva

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the darkest night before the new moon of Māgha, India stays awake for Śiva. Temples stay open till dawn, the liṅga is bathed and heaped with bilva, and devotees fast and chant through four watches of the night.

This guide covers the date, the vigil, the stories of the pillar of fire and the hunter in the tree, Mysuru’s golden mask, and the great Śivarātris from Gokarṇa to Kashmir.

The great night of Śiva

2027Saturday 6 March (night of 5–6 March in some almanacs)
KindA night festival: vigil, fasting, worship of the liṅga
OfferingBilva leaves, water, milk
StoriesThe pillar of fire; the hunter in the tree; the poison and the wedding
MysuruThe 11-kg golden mask at Trinēśvara in the palace
MonthlyA small śivarātri every month; this is the great one

ZAPPY: Why at night?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva is the lord of the dark and the still, Zappy. The festival keeps awake with him.

Mahāśivarātri, the great night of Śiva, falls on the fourteenth night of the waning moon in Māgha (by Karnataka’s amānta reckoning) or Phālguna (by the northern pūrṇimānta): the same night everywhere, in February or March. In 2027 most almanacs give Saturday 6 March.

It is a festival of night: devotees fast, keep vigil, and worship the liṅga with water, milk and bilva leaves through the four watches of the night. In Mysuru, the palace’s Trinēśvara temple dresses its liṅga in an 11-kg golden mask, seen only on this day.

THE DATE

TithiCaturdaśī of the dark fortnight
MonthMāgha in Karnataka; Phālguna in the north: the same night
RuleCaturdaśī prevailing at midnight (niśītha)
SeasonFebruary or March
Masa śivarātriEvery kṛṣṇa caturdaśī: twelve a year
Next dayAmāvāsyā; the fast is broken in the morning

ZAPPY: Two month names again?

PROF. BOLT: That’s amānta and pūrṇimānta, Zappy. See our pañcāṅga guide.

Every month has a śivarātri on the fourteenth night of the dark fortnight, the night before new moon; there are twelve a year. The one in Māgha/Phālguna is the mahā-, the great one.

The festival is fixed by the caturdaśī prevailing at midnight, so almanacs occasionally differ by a day. The fast ends the following morning, on amāvāsyā.

ZAPPY: What if I fall asleep?

PROF. BOLT: Then wake and carry on, Zappy. The hunter in the story stayed awake out of fear, and still won.

The heart of the festival is jāgaraṇa, staying awake through the night. Temples divide the night into four praharas, watches of about three hours, each with its own bathing and worship of the liṅga, and remain open till dawn.

Devotees chant “oṃ namaḥ śivāya”, sing bhajans, listen to the Śiva Purāṇa, or simply sit with the god. In cities, concerts, dance programmes and meditation gatherings run through the night.

THE FAST

Upavāsa
CommonFruit, milk, no grains
StrictNirjala: no food or water
SaltRock salt in place of ordinary salt
BreakingNext morning, after worship
WhoBy choice; the young, old and unwell need not
SpiritHunger to remind the mind of what it needs most

ZAPPY: Do I have to fast?

PROF. BOLT: No. Fasting is an offering, not a rule. Health comes first, Zappy.

Many fast through the day and night: some take only fruit and milk, avoiding grains and using rock salt; some keep nirjala, without food or water. The fast is broken next morning after worship.

Fasting is a personal offering. Children, the elderly, the unwell and pregnant women are traditionally not expected to fast strictly.

WORSHIP OF THE LIṄGA

What is offered

BilvaThree-leaved; Śiva’s favourite
VibhūtiSacred ash
Datura, ekkaWild flowers the god of the wild loves
LampGhee lamps through the night
Our ruleWe name the offerings, not the mantra procedure

ZAPPY: Why three leaves on one stalk?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees read them as his three eyes, or the three guṇas. Simplest answer: he likes them.

The liṅga is bathed (abhiṣeka) with water, milk, curd, honey and ghee, smeared with sacred ash, and covered with bilva leaves, the three-leaved sprigs of the bael tree, which are Śiva’s favourite offering. Wild flowers such as datura and arka are also offered, and ghee lamps burn through the night.

In keeping with this site’s practice, we name the offerings but do not set out ritual procedure or mantras; temple priests and family custom guide those.

STORY 1: THE PILLAR OF FIRE

Liṅgodbhava
QuarrelBrahmā and Viṣṇu argue who is greater
PillarAn endless column of fire appears
SearchBrahmā as a swan flies up; Viṣṇu as a boar digs down
No endNeither finds an end
LieBrahmā claims he saw the top, with the ketakī flower as witness
ŚivaEmerges from the pillar; this night is kept in his honour
SourceLiṅga Purāṇa and others

ZAPPY: Did Brahmā get in trouble?

PROF. BOLT: He lost his worship in most temples, and the ketakī flower lost its place at Śiva’s feet. That’s the story.

The Liṅga Purāṇa and other texts tell of a quarrel between Brahmā and Viṣṇu over who was greater. A blazing pillar of fire appeared between them, with no visible end. Brahmā, as a swan, flew up to find its top; Viṣṇu, as a boar, dug down to find its base. Neither succeeded.

Brahmā claimed falsely to have seen the top, with the ketakī flower as his witness. Śiva emerged from the pillar, revealing it as his own endless form, and punished the lie: Brahmā lost most of his worship and the ketakī its place in Śiva’s. The night of this appearance, liṅgodbhava, is one reason given for Śivarātri.

STORY 2: THE HUNTER

Lubdhaka in the bilva tree
HunterHunting in the forest, caught by night
TreeClimbs a bilva tree to escape wild animals
AwakePlucks leaves all night to keep from sleeping
BelowThe leaves fall on a liṅga under the tree
FastingHe had eaten nothing all day
ResultUnknowing worship on Śivarātri earns him liberation
SourceŚiva Purāṇa; retold in Java c. 1466–78

ZAPPY: He didn’t even know?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the point, Zappy. Even accidental devotion on this night counts.

The Śiva Purāṇa tells of a hunter, called Gurudruha or Lubdhaka, who was caught in the forest at nightfall and climbed a bilva tree to escape wild animals. To keep awake he plucked leaves and dropped them; they fell on a liṅga below. He had eaten nothing all day.

Without knowing it, he had fasted, kept vigil and worshipped Śiva on Śivarātri, and he won liberation. The story was retold as far away as 15th-century Java, in the Śivarātrikalpa.

OTHER STORIES

Poison and wedding

VigilThe gods kept him awake that night, one tradition says
WeddingThe night of Śiva’s marriage to Pārvatī, by another
TāṇḍavaThe night of his cosmic dance, by a third
ManySeveral reasons for one night, each beloved somewhere
Our pagesŚiva’s family and symbols; Pañcākṣara Stotram; Śrī Rudram

ZAPPY: So which is the real reason?

PROF. BOLT: All of them, for different families. Festivals collect stories like temples collect bells.

Other traditions give other reasons. Some say Śivarātri is the night Śiva drank the halāhala poison that rose from the churning of the ocean, holding it in his throat, which turned blue, and that the gods kept him awake. Others say it is the night of his wedding to Pārvatī, or of his cosmic dance.

For Śiva’s family, the Pañcākṣara Stotram and the Śrī Rudram, see our Śiva pages.

ZAPPY: A mask kept in a treasury?

PROF. BOLT: With the exam papers, once! It comes out under police guard for one night of glory.

In Mysuru, the great attraction is the Trinēśvara (Trinayanēśvara) temple near the Jayamārtāṇḍa gate of the palace. Its liṅga is dressed in an 11-kg golden mask, chinnada kolaga, given by Maharaja Jayachamarajendra Wadiyar in 1954. The mask is kept in the district treasury and brought out under police guard for the festival.

Huge crowds also gather at Nañjanagūḍu’s Śrīkaṇṭheśvara temple and at Male Mahadēśvara Beṭṭa, whose Śivarātri jātre draws lakhs of pilgrims; special buses run from Mysuru. In the city, the Gurukula with its 101 liṅgas opens only on this day.

ZAPPY: Why walk to Dharmasthala?

PROF. BOLT: A vow, Zappy. Thousands walk for days to reach Mañjunātha by Śivarātri.

At Gokarṇa, Mahābaleśvara’s great chariot festival falls at Śivarātri, starting from the Gaṇapati temple, as our Gaṇeśa guide describes. Murudēśvara, Gavipuram in Bengaluru, and countless village temples hold jātres.

Many pilgrims walk for days to Dharmasthala to reach Mañjunātha by Śivarātri.

ZAPPY: Dancers dance for Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: At Cidambaram, the dancing Śiva’s home. Dance is their worship.

At Kāśī Viśvanātha and all twelve jyotirliṅgas, crowds are immense. In Nepal, Paśupatināth draws sādhus from across South Asia, and the day is a national holiday.

Kashmiri Pandits keep Herāth, a festival of about three weeks, with walnuts as prasāda; at Maṇḍī in Himachal, local deities are carried in for a great fair. At Cidambaram, the Naṭyāñjali dance festival offers dance to Naṭarāja, and similar festivals are held at Konark, Khajuraho and Paṭṭadakal.

THE MEANING

The still point
NightDarkness as the ground from which light comes
AwakeStaying aware when the world sleeps
FastLightness of body for clarity of mind
VirtuesNon-injury, truth, charity, forgiveness
LiṅgaThe formless made visible
SimpleWater on a stone, a leaf, a name: enough

ZAPPY: Is a leaf and some water really enough?

PROF. BOLT: The hunter had less, Zappy, and he got the most.

Śivarātri pairs darkness with wakefulness: the world sleeps, the devotee stays aware. The fast lightens the body; the vigil steadies the mind. Many also take it as a night to reflect on virtues: non-injury, truth, charity, forgiveness.

The hunter’s story says the rest: a little water, a few leaves and a waking night are enough.
