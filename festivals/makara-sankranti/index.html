MAKARA SAṄKRĀNTI

The sun turns north

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

In mid-January the sun enters Makara, and India celebrates: ellu-bella and sugarcane in Karnataka, boiling pots of Pongal in Tamil Nadu, kites over Gujarat, bonfires in Punjab and Assam, holy baths at Gaṅgā Sāgar.

This guide explains the astronomy, the meaning of uttarāyaṇa, and the customs region by region, starting at home in Karnataka.

AT A GLANCE

The sun turns north
WhatThe sun enters Makara (Capricorn) in the sidereal zodiac
When14 January, or 15 in some years
2027Friday 15 January
KindA solar festival: the date barely moves
MeaningStart of uttarāyaṇa, the sun’s auspicious northern course; harvest
KarnatakaSuggi; ellu-bella; “ellu bella tindu oḷḷe mātāḍi”
ElsewherePongal, Uttarāyaṇ kites, Lohri, Māgh Bihu, Til-gul

ZAPPY: Why doesn’t it move like the others?

PROF. BOLT: It follows the sun, Zappy, not the moon. One of the very few.

Makara Saṅkrānti marks the sun’s entry into Makara, Capricorn, in the sidereal zodiac. It is one of the few Hindu festivals set by the sun, so it falls on 14 January, or 15 January in some years; in 2027, on Friday 15 January.

It is a harvest festival and the traditional start of uttarāyaṇa, the sun’s northern course. Almost every region keeps it under its own name: Suggi in Karnataka, Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Pedda Paṇḍuga in the Telugu states, Uttarāyaṇ in Gujarat, Lohri in Punjab, Māgh Bihu in Assam.

THE ASTRONOMY

Saṅkrānti and the solstice
SaṅkrāntiThe sun’s entry into a rāśi; twelve in a year
MakaraSun reaches 270° of the sidereal zodiac
SolsticeShortest day, about 21 December (tropical)
GapPrecession moves them apart about 1° in 72 years
Last togetherAround 291 CE
FutureFirst 16 January Saṅkrānti expected in 2102

ZAPPY: So the festival is late for the solstice?

PROF. BOLT: It used to coincide. The festival kept the stars; the seasons slipped. Both calendars are honest about different things.

A saṅkrānti is the sun’s entry into one of the twelve signs. Makara Saṅkrānti is its entry into Capricorn, measured in the sidereal zodiac, which is fixed to the stars.

The winter solstice, the shortest day, around 21 December, is the true astronomical turn of the sun northward. Because of the slow wobble of the earth’s axis, the sidereal and tropical zodiacs drift apart by about a degree every 72 years; Makara Saṅkrānti and the solstice coincided around 291 CE, and now fall some three weeks apart.

Wikipedia notes that the first 16 January Saṅkrānti is expected in 2102.

UTTARĀYAṆA

The bright half of the year
AyanaHalf-year: uttara (northward), dakṣiṇa (southward)
UttarāyaṇaDay of the gods; held auspicious
Gītā 8.24Fire, light, day, the bright fortnight, the six months of the northern course: the path of no return
BhīṣmaIn the Mahābhārata waits on his bed of arrows for uttarāyaṇa to die
SaṅkrāntiTreated as uttarāyaṇa’s start in practice
Puṇya kālaThe hours after the transit are held especially holy

ZAPPY: Bhīṣma waited for the sun?

PROF. BOLT: Fifty-odd days on a bed of arrows, says the epic. That’s how much uttarāyaṇa mattered.

The year is divided into two ayanas: uttarāyaṇa, the northward half, and dakṣiṇāyana, the southward. Uttarāyaṇa is called the day of the gods and is held auspicious.

The Bhagavad Gītā (8.24) names “the six months of the northern course” among the bright marks of the path from which the yogi does not return. In the Mahābhārata, the wounded Bhīṣma, who could choose the time of his death, waits on his bed of arrows for uttarāyaṇa before leaving his body.

In practice, Saṅkrānti is celebrated as the start of uttarāyaṇa, and the hours after the transit, the puṇya kāla, are held especially holy for bathing and giving.

IN KARNATAKA

Suggi, ellu-bella and kind words
NamesMakara Saṅkrānti; Suggi habba, the harvest festival
Ellu-bellaSesame, jaggery, groundnut, dry coconut, roasted gram
Saying“Ellu bella tindu oḷḷe mātāḍi”: eat sesame and jaggery, speak good words
Ellu bīrōduGirls in new clothes take plates to friends and neighbours
Sakkare acchuSugar figures from moulds; sugarcane; banana
FoodPongal sweet and savoury, puḷiyōgare, payasa

ZAPPY: Why sesame?

PROF. BOLT: Warm and oily for the dry winter, the elders say. And the sweet jaggery to sweeten your words.

In Karnataka the festival is Makara Saṅkrānti or Suggi habba, the harvest festival. Its symbol is ellu-bella: white sesame, jaggery, roasted groundnuts, dry coconut and roasted gram, mixed and shared.

Girls dress in new clothes and carry plates of ellu-bella, sakkare acchu (sugar figures pressed from moulds), sugarcane and banana to friends and neighbours, a custom called ellu bīrōdu. Everyone says “ellu bella tindu oḷḷe mātāḍi”: eat sesame and jaggery and speak good words.

Homes have rangoli, and kitchens make sweet and savoury pongal, puḷiyōgare and payasa. In Mysuru, neighbours exchange small packets of ellu-bella.

KICHCHU HĀYISUVUDU

Cattle and fire
WhatDecorated cattle led over burning hay
WhereRural Karnataka: Mandya, Mysuru, Bengaluru outskirts
BeliefWards off evil; brings luck for the farming year
PracticalHeat said to rid the hides of ticks
ConcernsInjuries to animals and people are reported most years
TodayDebated by animal-welfare groups; still kept in villages

ZAPPY: Doesn’t it hurt the cows?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the argument, Zappy. Farmers say no; injuries say sometimes. We report both.

In rural Karnataka, cattle are bathed, decorated with paint, flowers and bells, paraded, and in the evening led or made to jump across burning hay. This is kichchu hāyisuvudu, “making them cross fire”. It is common around Mandya and Mysuru and even near Bengaluru.

Farmers say it wards off evil and brings luck for the year, and that the heat drives off ticks; they say the animals are not hurt. Animal-welfare groups call it cruel, and news reports most years record injuries to animals or people; in one Mandya incident, twelve people were burned.

It remains a living rural custom and a live debate.

THE SUN ON THE LIṄGA

Gavi Gaṅgādhareśvara, Bengaluru
TempleThe cave temple of Gaṅgādhareśvara, Gavipuram, Bengaluru
EventAt Saṅkrānti evening the setting sun shines through a window onto the liṅga
PathLight passes between the horns of the Nandi
CrowdsThousands gather; broadcast live
BuilderThe temple linked with Kempegowda (16th c.)
MeaningThe sun himself worships Śiva as he turns north

ZAPPY: Planned on purpose?

PROF. BOLT: The builders aligned it, devotees say. Whatever the design, the sun keeps the appointment.

At the Gavi Gaṅgādhareśvara cave temple in Gavipuram, Bengaluru, a temple tradition linked with Kempegowda, the setting sun on Saṅkrānti evening shines through a window and between the horns of the Nandi to fall on the liṅga inside the cave for a few minutes.

Thousands gather to see it, and it is broadcast live. Devotees see it as the sun worshipping Śiva at the start of his northern journey.

PONGAL IN TAMIL NADU

Four days of harvest
BhōgiDay 1: old things burned, homes cleaned
Tai PongalDay 2: rice and milk boiled over in new pots
Cry“Pongalō pongal!” as the pot boils over
Māṭṭu PongalDay 3: cattle bathed, painted, garlanded
Kāṇum PongalDay 4: visiting family and outings
MonthStart of the solar month Tai

ZAPPY: What does pongal mean?

PROF. BOLT: “Boiling over”. When the pot overflows, the year’s plenty does too.

In Tamil Nadu, the festival is Pongal, four days at the start of the solar month Tai. Bhōgi clears out the old, with bonfires of rubbish; on Tai Pongal itself, rice and milk are boiled with jaggery in new pots until they boil over, to cries of “Pongalō pongal!”.

Māṭṭu Pongal honours cattle, which are bathed, their horns painted, and garlanded; Kāṇum Pongal is for visits and outings. “Pongal” comes from pongu, to boil over: the image of overflowing plenty.

IN ANDHRA AND TELANGANA

Pedda Paṇḍuga
DaysBhōgi, Saṅkrānti, Kanuma, Mukkanuma
MugguluRice-flour patterns at every door
GobbemmaluCow-dung balls topped with flowers
HaridāsuSingers who go door to door
KanumaDay for cattle
SizeThe biggest festival of the Telugu year, “the big festival”

ZAPPY: Why is it bigger than Ugādi there?

PROF. BOLT: It’s harvest home, Zappy. Families travel back to their villages.

In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, Saṅkrānti is Pedda Paṇḍuga, “the big festival”, kept over Bhōgi, Saṅkrānti, Kanuma and Mukkanuma. Doors are decorated with muggulu, rice-flour patterns, and gobbemmalu, cow-dung balls topped with flowers.

Haridāsus sing from door to door, and Kanuma honours cattle. Families travel home for it more than for any other festival.

IN MAHARASHTRA AND GUJARAT

Til-gul and kites
MaharashtraTil-gul sweets exchanged
Greeting“Til-gul ghyā, goḍ goḍ bolā”: take til-gul, speak sweetly
BlackBlack clothes worn, warm in winter
GujaratUttarāyaṇ: two days of kite-flying, rooftops full
FoodUndhiyu and chikki
NightPaper lanterns (tukkal) in the sky

ZAPPY: Same saying as Karnataka?

PROF. BOLT: Almost word for word. Sesame and sweet speech travel together.

In Maharashtra, people exchange til-gul, sesame and jaggery sweets, saying “til-gul ghyā, goḍ goḍ bolā”, take til-gul and speak sweetly: the same idea as Karnataka’s saying. Black clothes, unusual for a festival, are worn for warmth.

In Gujarat, Uttarāyaṇ is two days of kite-flying, with rooftops crowded from dawn, kite-fights, and undhiyu and chikki to eat; at night, paper lanterns float up.

NORTH AND EAST

Lohri, Bihu, Gaṅgā Sāgar
LohriPunjab: bonfire on the eve
Māgh BihuAssam: a week of feasting, bonfires, Mejī huts
Gaṅgā SāgarWest Bengal: great bath where the Gaṅgā meets the sea
Māgh MelāPrayāgrāj: bathing at the confluence
KumbhEvery 12 years at four sites; tens of millions
KhichḍīUP and Bihar: Saṅkrānti called Khichḍī, rice and dal given

ZAPPY: Why bathe in January?

PROF. BOLT: A holy dip at the sun’s turning washes away sins, by tradition. Cold, but holy.

Punjab lights bonfires for Lohri on the eve; Assam keeps Māgh Bihu, a week of feasting with bonfires and community huts. In Uttar Pradesh and Bihar the day is called Khichḍī, and rice and lentils are given away.

Saṅkrānti is a great day for holy bathing: at Gaṅgā Sāgar in West Bengal, where the Gaṅgā meets the sea, and at the Māgh Melā in Prayāgrāj. When the Kumbh Melā falls, its bathing days draw tens of millions.

MAKARAVILAKKU

Sabarimala, Kerala
WhereAyyappa’s hill shrine, Sabarimala
WhatThe Makara Jyoti light appears on Ponnambalamēḍu
SeasonEnds the Maṇḍala–Makaravilakku pilgrimage
PilgrimsHundreds of thousands, after a 41-day vow
TiruvābharaṇamAyyappa’s ornaments carried from Panthalam
Our noteAyyappa deserves his own guide

ZAPPY: Is the light natural?

PROF. BOLT: The Travancore Devaswom Board has said the flame is lit by people. Devotees still watch it with joy.

In Kerala, Saṅkrānti is Makaravilakku at Sabarimala, the hill shrine of Ayyappa. Pilgrims who have kept a 41-day vow watch for the Makara Jyoti, a light that appears on Ponnambalamēḍu across the valley, as Ayyappa’s ornaments arrive in procession from Panthalam.

The Travancore Devaswom Board has acknowledged that the flame is lit by people; for pilgrims, the moment remains the climax of the season.

STORIES AND MEANING

Sesame, Śani and the sun
Sūrya and ŚaniThe sun visits his son Śani, who offers sesame
DevīThe goddess Saṅkrānti slays the demon Saṅkarāsura
SesameFrom Viṣṇu’s sweat, in some legends
HarvestThe new crop thanks the sun and the cattle
SpeechSweet words begin the new half-year
DaanGifts of sesame, rice, blankets to the poor

ZAPPY: A god visiting his son?

PROF. BOLT: Sūrya and Śani didn’t get on. At Saṅkrānti, the story says, they make peace. A good day for families.

Purāṇic stories attach to the day: the sun god Sūrya visits his son Śani, with whom he had quarrelled, and is welcomed with sesame; the goddess Saṅkrānti slays the demon Saṅkarāsura. Some legends say sesame came from Viṣṇu’s sweat.

Underneath the stories, the festival thanks the sun, the earth and the cattle for the harvest, and asks for sweetness in speech and in relationships as the bright half of the year begins.
