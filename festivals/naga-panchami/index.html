Nāga Pañcamī

The Serpents’ Fifth Day · explained and illustrated

Milk on the serpent stone

HOW THIS WORKS: Why the serpents are worshipped in the rains, the Mahābhārata stories behind the day, the nine-nāga verse, Karnataka’s sisters and brothers, the nāgabanas of the coast, and kindness to real snakes.

WHERE WE ARE

On the fifth day of the bright fortnight of Śrāvaṇa, with the monsoon in full flow, Karnataka’s women go out to the huttu (anthills) and nāgara kallu (serpent stones) under village trees, and pour milk. This is Nāgara Pañcamī, the first of the long run of festivals from Śrāvaṇa to Dīpāvali, and above all a festival of sisters praying for their brothers.

Snakes come out with the rains, and the festival turns fear into worship. This article tells the stories behind it from the Mahābhārata (Kadru’s curse, Takṣaka and Parīkṣit, Janamejaya’s snake sacrifice stopped by the boy Āstīka) and the Bhāgavata (Kṛṣṇa and Kāliya), gives the nine-nāga verse, and goes from Karnataka’s homes to the sacred groves of Tuḷunāḍu and Kukke Subrahmaṇya, with a frank page on being kind to real snakes.

THE MONSOON AND THE SERPENTS

Why this festival, why now

The rains. In Śrāvaṇa the monsoon fills the burrows, and snakes come out into fields, courtyards and homes. Snakebite was a real danger for farming families; the festival asks for protection and peace.

The earth. Nāgas are spirits of the earth and the water beneath it, guardians of springs, wells and treasure. On this day many families do not dig, plough, cut or fry, so as not to hurt anything living in the ground.

The first festival. In Karnataka, Nāgara Pañcamī opens the festival season: Varamahālakṣmī, Janmāṣṭamī, Gaurī-Gaṇeśa, Navarātri and Dīpāvali follow. Old people call it habbagaḷa tavaru, the mother’s home of festivals.

Very old. Serpent worship in India is older than any surviving text; stones with snakes carved on them stand under village trees across the south, often beside a pīpal and a neem planted together.

ZAPPY: So it’s about the rains as much as the snakes?

PROF. BOLT: They come together, Zappy. The festival is the village making peace with both.

Rain brings the serpents out.

KADRU, VINATĀ AND THE CURSE

Where the nāgas come from

The story

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan, Āstīka Parvan (chs. 14–30 in common editions).

The sage Kaśyapa had two wives, Kadru and Vinatā. Kadru bore a thousand serpent sons, the nāgas; Vinatā bore two sons, Aruṇa and later Garuḍa.
The sisters made a bet on the colour of the tail of the white horse Uccaiḥśravas: the loser would be the winner’s slave. Vinatā said white; Kadru said black.
Kadru told her sons to hang on the tail and make it look black. Those who refused she cursed: they would burn in a king’s fire sacrifice.
Vinatā lost by the trick and became Kadru’s slave, until Garuḍa won her freedom by bringing the nectar of immortality from heaven. Ever since, Garuḍa and the serpents are enemies.
The mother’s curse waited for its time: a snake sacrifice of King Janamejaya, many generations later.

ZAPPY: A mother cursed her own children?

PROF. BOLT: For refusing to cheat, Zappy. The Mahābhārata never hides how strange its families are.

A curse that waits for its time.

TAKṢAKA AND PARĪKṢIT

The bite that started it all

The story

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan; the Bhāgavata’s frame story (book 1 and 12).

King Parīkṣit, Arjuna’s grandson, thirsty after a hunt, asked a sage in meditation for water. The sage did not answer. In anger the king hung a dead snake round his neck.
The sage’s son, Śṛṅgī, cursed the king: within seven days Takṣaka, king of serpents, would bite him and he would die.
Parīkṣit accepted the curse. In the Bhāgavata he spends the seven days listening to Śuka tell the whole Bhāgavata Purāṇa.
Takṣaka came disguised, hid as a worm in a fruit offered to the king, and bit him; Parīkṣit died.
His son Janamejaya grew up and swore revenge on all serpents.

ZAPPY: The Bhāgavata was told because of a snake?

PROF. BOLT: In a way, Zappy. A king with seven days to live asked the best question of his life.

Seven days and a fruit.

ĀSTĪKA STOPS THE SACRIFICE

Why the fifth day is the serpents’

The story

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan, Āstīka Parvan.

Janamejaya held the sarpa-satra, the serpent sacrifice. By the priests’ mantras, snakes from every land were dragged through the air and fell into the fire by the thousand. Kadru’s curse was coming true.
Āstīka, a boy-sage whose mother was the nāga princess Jaratkāru, sister of Vāsuki, set out to save his mother’s people.
At the sacrifice he praised the king, the priests and the fire so beautifully that Janamejaya offered him any boon.
Āstīka asked one thing: stop the sacrifice. The king was bound by his word; the fire was put out, and Takṣaka, already falling, was saved in mid-air.
Tradition holds that the sacrifice stopped on Śrāvaṇa Śukla Pañcamī, and the serpents promised to spare anyone who remembers Āstīka. Hence Nāga Pañcamī.

ZAPPY: A boy stopped a king’s sacrifice?

PROF. BOLT: With kind words and a promise, Zappy. Āstīka is the festival’s real hero.

Kind words put out the fire.

IN THE VERSE

Nava Nāga Stotra

The nine great serpents
anantaṃ vāsukiṃ śeṣaṃ padmanābhaṃ ca kambalamśaṅkhapālaṃ dhārtarāṣṭraṃ takṣakaṃ kāliyaṃ tathāetāni nava nāmāni nāgānāṃ ca mahātmanāmsāyaṅkāle paṭhen nityaṃ prātaḥkāle viśeṣataḥtasya viṣabhayaṃ nāsti sarvatra vijayī bhavet
Ananta, Vāsuki, Śeṣa, Padmanābha and Kambala, Śaṅkhapāla, Dhārtarāṣṭra, Takṣaka and Kāliya: these are the nine names of the great serpents. Whoever recites them every evening, and especially in the morning, has no fear of poison and is victorious everywhere.
WordMeaning
anantamAnanta, the endless
vāsukimVāsuki, Śiva’s serpent
śeṣamŚeṣa, Viṣṇu’s couch
takṣakamTakṣaka
viṣa-bhayamfear of poison
vijayīvictorious

A roll-call of serpents. The nine names include the cosmic serpents (Ananta and Śeṣa, on whom Viṣṇu rests; Vāsuki, the churning rope and Śiva’s necklace), the villain of the Mahābhārata (Takṣaka) and the one Kṛṣṇa tamed (Kāliya). It is the verse most often said on Nāga Pañcamī.

Text as on shlokam.org (Anantam Vasukim Sesham) and sanskritdocuments.org (Navanāganāmastotram), normalised to IAST; editions differ slightly.
Nine names against fear.

KṚṢṆA AND KĀLIYA

The dance on the serpent’s hoods

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.16.

The great serpent Kāliya had made a pool of the Yamunā his home, and its water was so poisonous that birds flying over it fell dead and the cowherd boys’ cattle died drinking from it.
Little Kṛṣṇa climbed a kadamba tree on the bank and dived in. Kāliya wrapped him in his coils, and all Vraja wept on the shore.
Kṛṣṇa slipped free, leapt onto the serpent’s many hoods and danced on them, stamping down each hood that rose, until Kāliya vomited blood and gave up.
Kāliya’s wives, the nāga-patnīs, prayed to Kṛṣṇa to spare their husband. Kṛṣṇa forgave him and sent him to the ocean island Ramaṇaka, promising that Garuḍa would not harm him, since he bore Kṛṣṇa’s footprints on his head.
The river was clean again. The story shows the festival’s balance: the dangerous serpent is subdued, not destroyed.

ZAPPY: Kṛṣṇa forgave him?

PROF. BOLT: Because his wives asked, Zappy. Even the serpent who poisoned the river is spared.

Subdued, not destroyed.

NĀGARA PAÑCAMĪ IN KARNATAKA

Sisters, milk and serpent stones

The day before. Many keep Nāgara Cauti on the fourth as a fast; on the fifth morning women and girls in new clothes carry milk, turmeric, kuṅkuma, flowers and cotton garlands (gejje vastra) to the village nāgara kallu or an anthill.

Milk for the brothers. The heart of the Kannada festival is the brother. Sisters pour milk on the stone or anthill and pray for their brothers’ long life; in many places the sister then touches the milk to her brother’s back and eyes, and he gives her a gift. The custom is called tani ereyuvudu or tanihālu in many districts.

Daughters come home. In north Karnataka newly married daughters return to their parents’ home for Pañcamī; swings are hung from trees for the girls and songs of the season are sung. Girls tie turmeric-dipped threads and draw serpent designs in rangoli.

No harm. Many families do not cut vegetables, fry or dig on Pañcamī, so as not to injure any creature; food is steamed or prepared the day before.

ZAPPY: The sister pours milk for the brother?

PROF. BOLT: Rakṣā Bandhan has a thread, Zappy; Karnataka’s Pañcamī has milk. Same love.

Milk for a brother’s life.

THE FARMER’S DAUGHTER

The vrata story told in homes

The story

A household vrata-kathā; versions differ from district to district.

A farmer, ploughing his field in Śrāvaṇa, accidentally killed a nest of young snakes.
The mother serpent, wild with grief, took revenge on the farmer’s family, and in the night bit them one by one.
Only a married daughter, living in another village, was spared. The serpent went there too, and found her worshipping a serpent image with milk, it being Nāga Pañcamī.
Moved by her devotion, the serpent drank the milk and was calmed. The daughter begged for her family’s lives, and the serpent gave her a nectar that restored them.
So, the story ends, on Pañcamī one should not plough or dig, and should honour the serpents with milk; and so sisters keep the day for their families.

ZAPPY: The daughter saved everyone?

PROF. BOLT: By the festival she kept, Zappy. That’s why it’s the daughters’ and sisters’ day.

A daughter’s vow saves the house.

PAÑCAMĪ FOOD

Tambiṭṭu, eḷḷuṇḍe and turmeric-leaf kaḍubu

🟤 Tambiṭṭu

Balls of roasted flour (rice, gram or wheat) with jaggery, cardamom and ghee: made the day before, so nothing need be fried on Pañcamī.

⚪ Eḷḷuṇḍe

Sesame and jaggery balls, with puffed rice (araḷu) and roasted gram; offered at the anthill.

🍃 On the coast

Arisina eleya kaḍubu (Pātoli in Konkani homes): rice batter with coconut and jaggery steamed in fragrant turmeric leaves, the monsoon treat of Tuḷunāḍu.

🥛 Milk

Milk, of course, offered first, and taken home as prasāda for the brothers and the children.

ZAPPY: Why turmeric leaves?

PROF. BOLT: They give the steamed rolls a wonderful smell, Zappy. Turmeric is also sacred to the nāgas on the coast.

Steamed, roasted, never fried.

TUḶUNĀḌU, NĀGABANA AND KUKKE

The coast’s serpent faith

Nāgabana. In Dakshina Kannaḍa and Uḍupi nearly every family and village has a nāgabana, a small grove left uncut for the serpents, with stone nāga images under the trees. On Pañcamī milk, tender coconut water, turmeric and flowers are offered there; in many coastal homes the men perform the worship.

Nāgamaṇḍala. The coast’s great serpent ritual, the all-night Nāgamaṇḍala, is held at other seasons: a vast coloured serpent design is drawn on the ground, and performers in trance dance around it till dawn.

Kukke Subrahmaṇya. In the Western Ghāṭs stands Kukke Subrahmaṇya, where Subrahmaṇya protects the serpent king Vāsuki, who took refuge there from Garuḍa. It is Karnataka’s great shrine for serpent vows and rites. See our Subrahmaṇya pages.

Why groves matter. The nāgabanas are among the last fragments of old forest on the coast; protecting them protects real snakes, frogs, trees and springs.

ZAPPY: Why are the groves left wild?

PROF. BOLT: For the nāgas, Zappy. And as a bonus, they’re little forests the village never cut down.

Little forests for the serpents.

ACROSS INDIA

Nāgula Chavithi to Manasā

🐜 Telugu states

Nāgula Chavithi, in Kārtika, with milk and eggs at anthills; Nāga Pañcamī too in some families.

🖼️ North and Nepal

Serpent images drawn with cow-dung or charcoal on walls; pictures of nāgas pasted above doors in Nepal.

🐍 Bengal and Assam

Manasā, goddess of snakes, worshipped through the monsoon months.

🌳 Kerala

Sarpakkāvu groves in family compounds and the famous Mannārśāla serpent temple; women pray for children and for their brothers.

ZAPPY: Live cobras anywhere?

PROF. BOLT: Not any more, Zappy. Courts and wildlife law stopped the catching of snakes for the festival. Good.

One fear, many reverences.

BE KIND TO REAL SNAKES

Worship without harm

Snakes do not drink milk. They are reptiles; forcing milk into a captured snake can kill it. The milk of the festival belongs on the stone and the anthill, as an offering.

No snake charmers. Snakes caught for the festival are often defanged, starved and kept in baskets. Catching or keeping them is forbidden under India’s Wildlife (Protection) Act.

Leave them be. The traditional way is the kind one: worship the stone, protect the grove, and leave live snakes alone. If one comes into the house, call a trained rescuer.

The spirit. The festival honours the creature; hurting it in the festival’s name turns the day upside down.

ZAPPY: Then why pour milk at all?

PROF. BOLT: On the stone or anthill, as an offering, Zappy. Never into a live snake’s mouth.

Honour the snake by leaving it alone.

WHAT NĀGA PAÑCAMĪ MEANS

Peace with the wild

From fear to respect. The most dangerous creature of the village is worshipped, not killed. Many read the festival as India’s old way of living with the wild. (Interpretation.)

The serpent in the gods. Śeṣa is Viṣṇu’s couch, Vāsuki is Śiva’s ornament and the churning rope, Subrahmaṇya is the serpents’ protector: the nāga is everywhere sacred, never merely an enemy.

Family. In Karnataka the day belongs to sisters, brothers and daughters coming home: the festival of the serpent is also the festival of the family’s safety.

Restraint. No digging, no frying, no harm: one day a year to walk lightly on the earth.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Nāga Pañcamī?

PROF. BOLT: Say the nine names, Zappy, and leave one patch of earth undug for whatever lives there.

Respect what we fear.
