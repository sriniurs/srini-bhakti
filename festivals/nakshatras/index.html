Nakṣatras and Rāśis

The Sky’s Calendar · explained and illustrated

The moon’s twenty-seven houses

HOW THIS WORKS: The twenty-seven nakṣatras the moon passes through, the Moon’s twenty-seven wives, the pādas and naming syllables, the twelve rāśis, why Saṅkrānti falls on 14 January, and how months, festivals and birth-stars take their names from the sky.

WHERE WE ARE

Every Hindu calendar lists, for each day, the tithi (lunar day) and the nakṣatra, the star-group the moon is passing through. Every Hindu knows their janma nakṣatra, the moon’s star at birth, and many festivals are fixed by a star: Kṛṣṇa’s birth on Rohiṇī, Kārttika Dīpam on Kṛttikā.

This guide explains the twenty-seven nakṣatras and the twelve rāśis (signs), their stories, their gods, how they name months, festivals and even children, and how they are used in practice. For the full almanac, with tithi, vāra, yoga and karaṇa, see our Pañcāṅga guide.

THE MOON’S MANSIONS

What a nakṣatra is

The moon’s path. Watched night after night, the moon moves eastward through the stars, about 13° a day, returning to the same stars in about 27⅓ days.

Twenty-seven houses. Indian astronomy divides this path into 27 equal parts of 13°20′, each named after a prominent star or star-group in it: the nakṣatras, “lunar mansions”. The moon “stays” in one each night.

The word. Nakṣatra simply means “star” in the Vedas; later it came to mean these 27 divisions.

Very old. The Atharvaveda (19.7) already lists 27 or 28 asterisms; the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa, the oldest astronomical text, uses them to time rituals. The oldest lists begin with Kṛttikā, the Pleiades.

ZAPPY: So the moon has a different house each night?

PROF. BOLT: Roughly one a night, Zappy. That’s why your calendar changes the nakṣatra almost every day.

A house each night.

THE MOON’S TWENTY-SEVEN WIVES

Why the moon waxes and wanes

The story

Purāṇic tellings, including the Somnāth story; see Somnāth.

Dakṣa, son of Brahmā, gave his twenty-seven daughters, the nakṣatras, in marriage to Candra, the Moon.
The Moon loved only one of them, Rohiṇī, and spent all his time with her. The other sisters complained to their father.
Dakṣa warned the Moon twice. When he did not change, Dakṣa cursed him to waste away.
The Moon began to shrink. In distress he prayed to Śiva at Prabhāsa (Somnāth), who could not undo the curse but softened it: the Moon would wane for a fortnight and wax for a fortnight, for ever.
So each month the Moon visits each wife in turn, one night each, and waxes and wanes. Śiva wears the crescent in his hair.

ZAPPY: A love story to explain the moon’s phases!

PROF. BOLT: And to remind husbands to be fair, Zappy.

A night with each.

THE TWENTY-SEVEN (1)

Aśvinī to Āśleṣā

1 Aśvinī (the Aśvins, divine physicians; in Aries) and 2 Bharaṇī (Yama).

3 Kṛttikā (Agni; the Pleiades, the six mothers who nursed Skanda) and 4 Rohiṇī (Prajāpati; Aldebaran, the Moon’s favourite).

5 Mṛgaśiras (Soma; the “deer’s head” in Orion), 6 Ārdrā (Rudra; Betelgeuse) and 7 Punarvasu (Aditi; Castor and Pollux; Rāma’s birth star).

8 Puṣya (Bṛhaspati, the gods’ teacher; in Cancer; very auspicious) and 9 Āśleṣā (the Nāgas, serpents; in Hydra).

ZAPPY: Rāma was born under Punarvasu?

PROF. BOLT: By tradition, Zappy, and Kṛṣṇa under Rohiṇī. Birth stars matter in the stories.

Nine houses, nine gods.

THE TWENTY-SEVEN (2)

Maghā to Jyeṣṭhā

10 Maghā (the Pitṛs, ancestors; Regulus), 11 Pūrva Phalgunī (Bhaga) and 12 Uttara Phalgunī (Aryaman; Denebola).

13 Hasta (Savitṛ; the “hand” in Corvus) and 14 Citrā (Tvaṣṭṛ, the divine craftsman; Spica, “the bright one”).

15 Svātī (Vāyu; Arcturus) and 16 Viśākhā (Indra and Agni; in Libra).

17 Anurādhā (Mitra) and 18 Jyeṣṭhā (Indra; Antares, “the eldest”).

ZAPPY: Gods from the Vedic guide again!

PROF. BOLT: Indra, Agni, Vāyu, Mitra, Zappy: the nakṣatra deities are the Veda’s gods. See The Vedic Gods.

The Veda’s gods in the stars.

THE TWENTY-SEVEN (3)

Mūla to Revatī

19 Mūla (Nirṛti; the “root”, the scorpion’s tail), 20 Pūrva Āṣāḍhā (the Waters) and 21 Uttara Āṣāḍhā (the Viśvedevas).

22 Śravaṇa (Viṣṇu; Altair, “hearing”; Kerala’s Onam falls on it) and 23 Dhaniṣṭhā (the Vasus; in Delphinus).

24 Śatabhiṣaj (Varuṇa; “a hundred healers”), 25 Pūrva Bhādrapadā (Aja Ekapāda) and 26 Uttara Bhādrapadā (Ahirbudhnya), in Pegasus.

27 Revatī (Pūṣan, guardian of journeys; in Pisces). Abhijit, with Brahmā as deity, was a 28th in older lists and is still used in choosing auspicious times.

ZAPPY: Abhijit got left out?

PROF. BOLT: When the circle was cut into 27 equal parts, Zappy. It still has a role in timing.

Twenty-seven, and one more.

PĀDAS, 108 AND NAMES

Quarters of a house

Pādas. Each nakṣatra has four quarters (pādas) of 3°20′, so the moon’s circle has 27 × 4 = 108 pādas; many connect this with the 108 beads of a japa mālā.

Naming syllables. Each pāda has a syllable. Many families name a child with a name beginning with the syllable of the birth pāda: a child born in Aśvinī’s first pāda might get a name starting with “Cu”, as in Cūḍāmaṇi.

The secret name. In some families this star-name is kept private, used only in rituals, while the child is called by another name. (Practice varies.)

Rāśi links. Since a rāśi (30°) holds 2¼ nakṣatras (nine pādas), the birth pāda also fixes the moon-sign.

ZAPPY: My name could come from a star?

PROF. BOLT: Ask your parents, Zappy. Many Kannada names were chosen that way.

A name from the sky.

THE TWELVE RĀŚIS

Signs of the zodiac

Twelve signs. The same circle is also divided into twelve rāśis of 30°: Meṣa, Vṛṣabha, Mithuna, Karkaṭa, Siṃha, Kanyā, Tulā, Vṛścika, Dhanus, Makara, Kumbha and Mīna. Their names match the familiar Ram, Bull, Twins, Crab and so on.

Two systems meet. Nakṣatras are an old Indian system tied to the moon; the twelve signs came into Indian astronomy later, in common with Greek and Babylonian traditions, and are tied mainly to the sun.

Moon-sign and sun-sign. In India one’s rāśi usually means the moon’s sign at birth, not the sun’s as in Western horoscopes.

Months. Solar months (used in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Bengal, Odisha) are named after the rāśi the sun is in: Tulā māsa, Makara māsa.

ZAPPY: My Indian sign isn’t my Western sign?

PROF. BOLT: Usually not, Zappy: Moon versus Sun, and the next page explains a second reason.

Twelve signs, moon first.

FIXED TO THE STARS

Why Saṅkrānti is on 14 January

Sidereal. Indian astronomy measures the signs against the fixed stars (nirayana, “without movement”). Western astrology measures them from the spring equinox (tropical, sāyana).

The drift. The earth’s axis slowly wobbles, so the equinox moves backward against the stars, about one degree every 72 years. The gap between the two zodiacs, the ayanāṃśa, is now about 24°.

Makara Saṅkrānti. The sun’s entry into sidereal Makara now falls around 14 January, about three weeks after the winter solstice (21–22 December). Centuries ago they nearly coincided; this is why the festival marks the “northward turn” a little late. See Makara Saṅkrānti.

One consequence. Most people’s Indian sign is one sign earlier than their Western sign.

ZAPPY: The sky moves under the calendar?

PROF. BOLT: Very slowly, Zappy: about a day every seventy years. Indian astronomers noticed it long ago.

Fixed to the stars.

MONTHS NAMED BY STARS

The full moon’s nakṣatra

🌕 The rule

A lunar month is named after the nakṣatra in or near which its full moon falls: the month whose full moon is near Citrā is Caitra.

📅 All twelve

Caitra (Citrā), Vaiśākha (Viśākhā), Jyeṣṭha (Jyeṣṭhā), Āṣāḍha (Āṣāḍhā), Śrāvaṇa (Śravaṇa), Bhādrapada (Bhādrapadā), Āśvina (Aśvinī), Kārttika (Kṛttikā), Mārgaśīrṣa (Mṛgaśiras), Pauṣa (Puṣya), Māgha (Maghā), Phālguna (Phalgunī).

🔢 So the stars run the year

Every time you say “Śrāvaṇa” or “Kārttika”, you are naming a star.

📖 More

How these months fit the tithis and the extra month is in our Pañcāṅga guide.

ZAPPY: Kārttika is named after the Pleiades?

PROF. BOLT: Kṛttikā, Zappy, the six sisters of Skanda. That month’s full moon sits near them.

Every month is a star.

FESTIVALS FIXED BY STARS

When the nakṣatra decides

🍼 Rohiṇī

Kṛṣṇa’s birth: many keep Janmāṣṭamī by the aṣṭamī tithi, others (especially in the South) by the Rohiṇī star. Traditions differ.

🪔 Kṛttikā

Kārttika Dīpam at Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, on the Kṛttikā star of Kārttika; see Kārttikai Dīpam.

🌼 Śravaṇa

Kerala’s Onam on Tiruvōṇam (Śravaṇa) in the month of Ciṅṅam; Śrāvaṇa Pūrṇimā’s upākarma; Viṣṇu’s star.

💃 Ārdrā and others

Ārudrā Darśanam of Naṭarāja on Ārdrā in Mārgaḻi; Paṅguni Uttiram on Uttara Phalgunī; many temple festivals start on a fixed star.

ZAPPY: Some festivals use stars, some use tithis?

PROF. BOLT: And a few use the sun, Zappy. That’s why the calendar needs all three.

Star, tithi or sun.

THE BIRTH STAR IN PRACTICE

Janma nakṣatra, and a word on astrology

Birthdays by star. Many families, especially in the South, celebrate a person’s birthday on their janma nakṣatra in the birth month, not on the solar date: the “star birthday”.

In ritual. In every saṅkalpa (statement of intent) the worshipper names their nakṣatra, rāśi and gotra; archanās in temples are offered in the devotee’s nakṣatra.

Astrology. Jyotiṣa also uses nakṣatras and rāśis for horoscopes, matching marriages and choosing times. Many Hindus rely on it; many teachers and scientists do not, and its predictions are not scientifically established. This guide describes the system and does not endorse predictions.

Muhūrta. Choosing an auspicious time for weddings and housewarmings by nakṣatra and tithi is widespread practice, whatever one’s view of astrology.

ZAPPY: Do I have to believe in horoscopes?

PROF. BOLT: No, Zappy. You can love the calendar and its stories, and make up your own mind about predictions.

Know the sky; think for yourself.

WHAT THE STARS TEACH

Time as a living rhythm

Look up. The nakṣatras are real stars you can find: Rohiṇī in Taurus, Kṛttikā’s cluster, Citrā’s bright Spica. The calendar asks us to know the sky.

Time has character. Each night has its own star, god and story; time is not empty but full of meaning. (Interpretation.)

Old astronomy. Indian astronomers tracked the moon and the drift of the equinoxes with remarkable care; the system is a scientific achievement as well as a sacred one.

Belonging. Knowing your star connects you to every festival, every saṅkalpa and every grandparent who knew theirs.
