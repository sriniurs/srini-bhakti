PART 6 OF 16 · NIGHT 3 · THE MOON-BELL

Candraghaṇṭā

Night 3 · The Moon-Bell · explained and illustrated

Ten arms, a tiger and the sound of a bell

HOW THIS WORKS: Her name, the story of Śiva’s wedding party, her ten arms and the bell, her verse word by word, her night and her meaning.

WHERE WE ARE

On the second night Brahmacāriṇī won Śiva by penance. Tonight comes the wedding, and with it a new form: Candraghaṇṭā, the Goddess with a half-moon shaped like a bell (ghaṇṭā) on her brow.

After two calm nights she is the first warrior among the nine: golden, ten-armed, riding a tiger, ready for battle yet gracious to her devotees.

With the blessings of the Devī,

ZAPPY: A bride with ten weapons?

PROF. BOLT: A bride who can protect her whole household. The bell on her brow is said to send demons running.

THE MOON THAT RINGS

Candra-ghaṇṭā: who she is

Her name. Candraghaṇṭā means “she who has a moon (candra) like a bell (ghaṇṭā)”: the half-moon on her forehead is shaped like a temple bell.

The bell. In worship the bell is rung to call the deity and to drive away what is unholy. Her bell is said to ring through the worlds so that demons flee at its sound.

A warrior bride. She is golden in colour and has ten arms holding weapons, yet one hand is raised in blessing: fierce to the enemy, gentle to the devotee.

Night three. After the calm of the first two nights, the third night brings courage.

ZAPPY: Do we ring a bell for her, then?

PROF. BOLT: You ring the bell in every pūjā. Tonight, notice it: that sound is her.

Candraghaṇṭā: a bell-moon on her brow, courage for the third night.

THE WEDDING OF ŚIVA

A popular telling

The story

This story is told in popular Navarātri tradition rather than in the older Purāṇas.

After Pārvatī’s penance, Śiva agreed to marry her, and he set out for Himavat’s palace with his wedding party.
But Śiva’s companions were his gaṇas: ghosts, goblins and strange beings, and Śiva himself came ash-smeared, wearing snakes, riding his bull.
Pārvatī’s mother Menā saw the procession and fainted with fright; the household was in uproar.
Pārvatī then took the form of Candraghaṇṭā, golden and radiant, and went before Śiva. She asked him to come in a form her family could welcome.
Śiva appeared as a handsome prince, Candraśekhara, adorned with jewels, and the wedding went ahead in joy. Tellings vary in the details.

ZAPPY: So she protected her family from being scared?

PROF. BOLT: She protected the wedding. She stands between the wild and the household and makes them meet in peace.

At Śiva’s wild wedding, Candraghaṇṭā keeps the peace.

HER FORM AND SYMBOLS

What the image teaches

🔔 The bell

Her emblem: a sound that calls the good and scatters the evil. It is also the sound of awakening.

🌙 The half-moon

On her brow, like Śiva’s: the calm that lies under her fierceness.

⚔️ Ten arms

Trident, sword, mace, bow and arrows, lotus, rosary, water pot, bell, and a hand of blessing: ready for anything.

🐅 The tiger

The verse calls her mount “the best of beasts”; it is usually drawn as a tiger, sometimes as a lion.

ZAPPY: Why is her face calm with so many weapons?

PROF. BOLT: Courage isn’t anger. She is ready to fight and at peace at the same time.

Bell, moon, ten arms, tiger: courage that is calm.

IN THE VERSE

NAVADURGĀ STOTRA 3

HER MEDITATION VERSE (DHYĀNA)
piṇḍajapravarārūḍhā caṇḍakopāstrakairyutā .prasādaṃ tanute mahyaṃ candraghaṇṭeti viśrutā ..
Riding the best of beasts, armed with weapons of fierce wrath, she who is famed as Candraghaṇṭā spreads her grace over me.
WordMeaning
piṇḍaja-pravara-ārūḍhāmounted on the best of the womb-born (beasts)
caṇḍa-kopa-astrakaiḥ yutāarmed with weapons of fierce wrath
prasādam tanute mahyamextends her grace to me
iti viśrutāfamed by the name

Fierce weapons, grace for me. The verse puts her terror and her kindness in the same breath: the weapons are for the enemy, the grace is for the devotee. That is the whole meaning of a protective goddess.

Text as in the Navadurgā Stotra (shlokam.org transliteration). “Piṇḍaja” (womb-born) is read as an animal; tradition draws it as a tiger.
Fierce to the enemy, grace to the devotee.

WORSHIP ON HER NIGHT

How devotees keep night 3

The bell. Devotees ring the bell with special attention on this night, and some keep a small bell beside her picture.

For fear. She is prayed to by those facing fear: illness, a hard decision, a frightening journey.

Peace at home. Remembering her wedding story, some pray to her for harmony between families.

Offerings and colours. A widely followed North Indian custom offers milk or kheer on this night; lists differ between families and regions. Many cities also publish a “colour of the day” for each night, but that colour is set each year by the weekday, so we do not fix one here.

ZAPPY: Can I pray to her for exam nerves?

PROF. BOLT: Of course. Fear is fear, big or small. Ring the bell and say her verse.

Night three: ring the bell, ask for courage.

THE INNER READING

What many teachers see in her

Courage. After stability and discipline comes courage: the strength to face what frightens us.

The inner sound. Some teachers link her bell with the inner sound (nāda) heard in deep meditation, said to steady the mind.

Gentle fierceness. Her calm face above ten weapons is read as the ideal: strong boundaries held without hatred.

The yogic map. A modern yogic reading links each night to a centre of the subtle body; for Candraghaṇṭā it is maṇipūra, the navel centre. This mapping is popular but recent, and teachers differ; take it as a meditation aid, not scripture.

ZAPPY: So she’s brave but not angry?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Anger shakes; courage stands. She stands.

Candraghaṇṭā gives calm courage.

THE GODS ARM THE GODDESS

Where the bell came from

The story

Devī Māhātmya, chapter 2.

When Mahiṣāsura drove the gods from heaven, their anger blazed out of their faces as light, and the light gathered into one radiant Goddess.
Each god then gave her a copy of his own weapon: Śiva a trident, Viṣṇu a discus, Varuṇa a conch and a noose, Agni a spear, Vāyu a bow and quivers.
Indra gave her his thunderbolt, and took a bell from his elephant Airāvata and gave it to her too (next page).
Himavān gave her the lion; Kubera a cup; Viśvakarman an axe and armour; the ocean garlands of unfading lotuses.
So armed, she laughed aloud, and the worlds shook. Candraghaṇṭā, with the bell-shaped moon and the bell whose sound terrifies demons, is that Goddess ready for battle.

ZAPPY: The bell came from an elephant?

PROF. BOLT: Airāvata’s war-bell, Zappy. When she rings it, the demons’ courage breaks.

Every god’s gift in one pair of hands.

IN THE VERSE

Devī Māhātmya 2.23

The bell from Airāvata
dadau tasyai sahasrākṣo ghaṇṭām airāvatād gajātkāladaṇḍād yamo daṇḍaṃ pāśaṃ cāmbupatir dadau
The thousand-eyed (Indra) gave her a bell from his elephant Airāvata; Yama gave her a staff from his staff of death, and the lord of the waters (Varuṇa) a noose.
WordMeaning
sahasrākṣaḥthe thousand-eyed, Indra
ghaṇṭāma bell
airāvatāt gajātfrom the elephant Airāvata
kāla-daṇḍātfrom the staff of death
pāśama noose
ambupatiḥlord of the waters

Why the bell matters. Every Hindu pūjā rings a bell at the start, to invite the gods and drive away the demons, as the pūjā manuals say. Candraghaṇṭā takes her very name from it: the moon shaped like a bell on her forehead, and the war-bell from Indra in her hand.
