WHERE WE ARE

After the mother Skandamātā, the sixth night brings the warrior who fought the battle of Part 2. Kātyāyanī is the form of the Devī who slew Mahiṣāsura, named after the sage Kātyāyana in whose hermitage she appeared.

She is also the goddess whom the young gopīs of Vraja worshipped for a month, praying for Kṛṣṇa as their husband: a warrior and a giver of good marriages at once.

THE DAUGHTER OF KĀTYA

Kātyāyanī: who she is

Her name. Kātyāyanī means “she of Kātya”, the daughter of the line of the sage Kātya, usually named as the sage Kātyāyana.

The sage’s wish. Tradition says Kātyāyana did long penance hoping the Goddess would be born as his daughter. When the gods’ combined light took form to fight Mahiṣa, it was in his hermitage that she appeared, and he was the first to worship her.

The warrior. She is the Devī of the battle against Mahiṣāsura, the story of Part 2. Some call her Mahiṣāsuramardinī for this reason.

Night six. The sixth night is a night of victory over inner enemies.

ZAPPY: So the sage got his wish?

PROF. BOLT: He got more than a daughter. He got to bow first to the Goddess who saved heaven.

Kātyāyanī: born in a sage’s hermitage, slayer of Mahiṣa.

THE GOPĪS’ VOW

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.22

The story

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa tells how the young girls of Vraja kept a vow to her.

In the month of Mārgaśīrṣa, at the start of winter, the young gopīs of Vraja took a vow (vrata) to Kātyāyanī.
Every morning they bathed in the cold Yamunā before dawn, made an image of the Goddess from sand on the riverbank, and worshipped her with flowers, incense and lamps.
They ate simple food once a day and prayed with one wish: “O Kātyāyanī, great power, make Nanda’s son, Kṛṣṇa, our husband.”
At the end of the month Kṛṣṇa himself came to them, teased them and blessed their devotion, promising that their wish would be fulfilled.
Because of this story, unmarried girls in many regions still worship Kātyāyanī for a good husband.

ZAPPY: The goddess who killed Mahiṣa helps with marriages?

PROF. BOLT: The same power that defeats the demon protects the young and grants their good wishes. Strength and grace are one.

A month-long vow to Kātyāyanī: the gopīs’ prayer was answered.

HER FORM AND SYMBOLS

What the image teaches

🗡️ The shining sword

The verse calls it candrahāsa, “moon-laughing”, a sword bright as moonlight: the blade that cuts through ignorance.

🪷 Lotus

A lotus in the other hand: the victory is for beauty and good, not for destruction’s sake.

🦁 Lion or tiger

Her verse says śārdūla, a tiger; most images give her a lion. Traditions differ; both are kings of beasts.

🔴 Red

She is often shown in red: the colour of power and of marriage.

ZAPPY: A sword that laughs like the moon?

PROF. BOLT: A poet’s way of saying it gleams. Her sword is light itself.

Shining sword, lotus, lion: victory that serves the good.

IN THE VERSE

NAVADURGĀ STOTRA 6

HER MEDITATION VERSE (DHYĀNA)
candrahāsojjvalakarā śārdūlavaravāhanā .kātyāyanī śubhaṃ dadyādevī dānavaghātinī ..
Her hand bright with the moon-gleaming sword, the noble tiger her mount, may Devī Kātyāyanī, slayer of demons, grant us good.
WordMeaning
candrahāsa-ujjvala-karāher hand blazing with the moon-bright sword
śārdūla-vara-vāhanāwhose mount is the noble tiger
śubham dadyātmay she grant good
dānava-ghātinīslayer of the demons

A slayer who grants good. The verse names her the killer of demons and in the same line asks her for good: her killing is the good, because it removes what harms.

Text as in the Navadurgā Stotra (shlokam.org transliteration).
Moon-bright sword, noble tiger: the slayer of demons grants good.

WORSHIP ON HER NIGHT

How devotees keep night 6

For marriage. Unmarried young women in many regions pray to her on this night, remembering the gopīs.

For victory. She is prayed to for victory over obstacles: lawsuits, illness, bad habits.

In Bengal. Ṣaṣṭhī, the sixth day, is when Durgā Pūjā begins in earnest in Bengal: the Goddess is welcomed and her face unveiled.

Offerings and colours. A widely followed North Indian custom offers honey on this night; lists differ between families and regions. Many cities also publish a “colour of the day” for each night, but that colour is set each year by the weekday, so we do not fix one here.

ZAPPY: Can boys pray to her too?

PROF. BOLT: Of course. Victory over your inner demons is for everyone.

Night six: victory, and the gopīs’ prayer.

THE INNER READING

What many teachers see in her

Inner battle. Kātyāyanī is read as the power that wins the inner battle against anger, greed and pride, the Mahiṣa within.

The brow. Some teachers link her with the brow centre: the clear sight that knows what must be cut away.

Devotion as vow. The gopīs’ month of cold dawns shows devotion as a practice kept day after day.

The yogic map. A modern yogic reading links each night to a centre of the subtle body; for Kātyāyanī it is ājñā, the brow centre. This mapping is popular but recent, and teachers differ; take it as a meditation aid, not scripture.

ZAPPY: So the sword is for my own bad habits?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. She cuts, you grow. That is the sixth night.

Kātyāyanī gives victory over the enemy within.
