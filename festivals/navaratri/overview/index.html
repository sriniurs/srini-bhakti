Navarātri: An Overview

Nine Nights and the Victorious Tenth · explained and illustrated

Nine lamps, one kalaśa, three goddesses

HOW THIS WORKS: First what Navarātri is: the name, the four Navarātris and how the nights are counted. Then a map of the sixteen parts, from the story to the nine Durgās to the Mysuru Dasarā.

WHERE WE ARE

Every autumn, for nine nights, homes and temples across India turn to the Goddess. In Mysuru the city lights up, the doll steps come out, and on the tenth day Cāmuṇḍeśvarī rides through the streets on an elephant. This is Navarātri, and in Karnataka its tenth day gives the whole season its name, Dasarā.

This series walks through it in sixteen parts: the story behind the festival, how the nine nights begin, the nine Durgās one night at a time, the great days at the end, and the Mysuru Dasarā. It builds on our Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts series, which tells the scripture verse by verse.

This first part is the map: what the name means, the four Navarātris of the year, how the nights are counted, and how India keeps them.

ZAPPY: So the tenth day isn’t one of the nine nights?

PROF. BOLT: Right. Nine nights of worship, then a tenth day when the victory is celebrated. That is why you will hear both names, Navarātri and Dasarā.

NINE NIGHTS, THREE GODDESSES

Why nine, why these three, and why the tenth day means victory

The name. Navarātri is simply nava “nine” and rātri “night”: nine nights given to the Goddess. A tenth day closes it, Vijayadaśamī, “the victorious tenth”. In Karnataka the whole season is called Dasarā (from Daśaharā, a name tied to that tenth day), and in Mysuru it is the Nāḍa Habba, “the festival of the land”.

Three by three. In South India the nine nights are often counted in threes: the first three for Durgā (often worshipped as Kālī), the next three for Lakṣmī, the last three for Sarasvatī. Many read this as a path for the seeker: first the Mother clears away what harms, then she grows what is good, then she gives knowledge.

The scripture behind it. The count mirrors the three stories of the Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts, presided over by Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī. The middle story, the slaying of Mahiṣāsura, is the one most homes connect with Navarātri; it gets its own part, Part 2.

Traditions differ. Across the north, each night instead belongs to one of the nine Durgās (Navadurgā), from Śailaputrī on the first night to Siddhidātrī on the ninth. Bengal gathers its worship into the last days as Durgā Pūjā. These are not rival answers: they are different ways of counting the faces of one Goddess.

ZAPPY: So which is right: three goddesses, or nine Durgās?

PROF. BOLT: Both. The Devī is one; three and nine are two ways of counting her. A Mysuru home keeps the three-by-three rhythm and still bows to Cāmuṇḍī, who is Durgā herself, every day of the festival.

Nine nights, counted three by three: Durgā clears, Lakṣmī grows, Sarasvatī teaches. The tenth day is victory.

FOUR NAVARĀTRIS IN A YEAR

Spring, monsoon, autumn and winter: four doors into the Goddess

Four seasons, four Navarātris. The tradition speaks of four Navarātris, each beginning on the first day (pratipadā) of a bright fortnight: Vasanta Navarātri in Caitra (spring), Āṣāḍha Navarātri at the start of the monsoon, Śāradā Navarātri in Āśvina (autumn) and Māgha Navarātri in winter.

The two public ones. Vasanta Navarātri, in spring, ends on the ninth day with Rāma Navamī in many regions, and its first day is the new year of the Deccan, Ugādi. Śāradā Navarātri, in autumn, is the great festival that this whole series follows.

The two “hidden” ones. The Āṣāḍha and Māgha Navarātris are called Gupta, “hidden”, because they are kept quietly, mostly by those doing personal sādhana, rather than with public celebration. In Śrīvidyā they are linked with Vārāhī and Rājaśyāmalā, the two great ministers of Lalitā.

Why autumn wins. The autumn festival is called Śāradā from śarad, autumn. It comes after the rains, when the harvest is ripening and the year turns toward the cool season. Texts and teachers differ on which Navarātri is the most important for which age; what is not in doubt is that the autumn one is the one India celebrates together.

ZAPPY: If there are four, why does everyone only talk about one?

PROF. BOLT: Because the autumn one became the public festival: kings, temples and towns gathered around it. The others stayed quieter, kept at home or by people in a vow.

Four Navarātris a year; Śāradā, in autumn, is the great one.

WHY THE GODDESS COMES IN AUTUMN

The harvest, the season and the story

A harvest festival. In many regions Śāradā Navarātri falls just as the monsoon crop is ripening. Grain is sown in a pot on the first day and grows green by the ninth; tools of work are worshipped on the ninth day; the tenth day starts new work. The festival sits in the farming year as naturally as it sits in the calendar of the Goddess.

The Goddess as Mother coming home. In Bengal and much of the east, the Devī is welcomed as Umā, the married daughter visiting her parents’ home with her children, and sent off again on the tenth day. The mood is a family reunion as much as a war story.

Rāma’s autumn worship. A tradition, best known from the Bengali Rāmāyaṇa of Kṛttivāsa, says that Rāma worshipped Durgā out of season, in autumn, before his battle with Rāvaṇa. That is why Bengal calls its Durgā Pūjā akāla bodhana, “the untimely awakening”. Vālmīki’s Rāmāyaṇa does not tell this story; it belongs to later regional tradition.

The victory. Whatever the region, the festival ends in victory: the Devī over Mahiṣa, Rāma over Rāvaṇa, the Pāṇḍavas taking back their weapons. Each story gets its own page later in the series.

ZAPPY: A war story and a homecoming at the same time?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. A mother fights for her children and then comes home to them. India keeps both moods in the same nine nights.

Autumn brings the harvest, the homecoming daughter, and the stories of victory.

HOW A HOME KEEPS THE NINE NIGHTS

Lamp, kalaśa, reading and visitors

The first morning. Many homes install a kalaśa, a water pot crowned with mango leaves and a coconut, as the seat of the Goddess, and light a lamp that some keep burning through all nine nights. This is Ghaṭasthāpana, the subject of Part 3.

Reading and chanting. The Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts is read through the nine days, in full or in parts. Others chant the Lalitā Sahasranāma · 10 articles or the Yā Devī Sarvabhūteṣu verses daily.

The dolls. In Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, the doll steps go up on the first day: gods at the top, the paṭṭada gombe (the royal doll couple), village scenes below. Friends visit in the evenings to see them. This is Part 13, Gombe Habba.

Fasting and food. Some fast through the nine days, some on particular days, some not at all; customs differ by family and region. Sweets and offerings change from night to night in many homes.

ZAPPY: Do we have to do all of this?

PROF. BOLT: No. Each family keeps what it inherited. A lamp, a prayer and a story each night is already Navarātri.

Lamp, kalaśa, a reading, the dolls and visitors: the rhythm of the nine nights at home.

HOW INDIA KEEPS IT

One festival, many regional forms

East. In Bengal, Odisha and Assam the last days become Durgā Pūjā: the Goddess with her children in a clay image in a pandal, worshipped from the sixth to the ninth day and taken to the river on the tenth.

West. In Gujarat the nights are danced: garbā around a lamp in a pierced pot, and dāṇḍiyā with sticks, in praise of the Mother.

North. In much of the north the nights belong to the nine Durgās, and Rāmlīlā, the staged Rāmāyaṇa, ends on the tenth day with Rāvaṇa’s effigy burnt. In Kullu the Dasarā begins on Vijayadaśamī itself and gathers the valley’s village deities.

South. In Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra the dolls come out (Gombe, Golu, Bommala Koluvu); Sarasvatī Pūjā and Āyudha Pūjā mark the end; Kerala begins children’s learning with Vidyārambha. And in Mysuru the whole city keeps the royal Dasarā, Part 16.

ZAPPY: Which one is the real Navarātri?

PROF. BOLT: All of them. The nine nights are the same; the way each region shows its love is different.

Bengal’s pandals, Gujarat’s dances, the north’s Rāmlīlā, the south’s dolls: one Goddess, many festivals.

THE TENTH DAY

Vijayadaśamī, the day of victory

A day of victory. Vijayadaśamī, the tenth day of the bright fortnight of Āśvina, celebrates victory: the Devī’s over Mahiṣa, Rāma’s over Rāvaṇa, and good beginnings over hesitation.

The śamī tree. In Karnataka, Andhra and Maharashtra the śamī tree (banni in Kannada) is worshipped, remembering the Pāṇḍavas, who hid their weapons in a śamī during their year in disguise. Its leaves are exchanged as “gold”.

New beginnings. It is counted among the most auspicious days of the year to begin learning, a business or a journey. Kerala brings small children to write their first letters; Mysuru carries the Goddess to the banni tree on an elephant.

Its own part. Part 15 covers Mahānavamī and Vijayadaśamī in full, and Part 16 the Mysuru procession.

ZAPPY: Why start things on that day?

PROF. BOLT: Because it is the day victory was won. You begin under the sign of success.

Vijayadaśamī: victory remembered, the śamī worshipped, new work begun.
