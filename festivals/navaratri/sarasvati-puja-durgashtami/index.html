Sarasvatī Pūjā and Durgāṣṭamī

Books, Little Girls and the Junction Hour · explained and illustrated

Sarasvatī, the books and the young Goddesses

HOW THIS WORKS: When and why books go to Sarasvatī, who she is, a simple pūjā, then Durgāṣṭamī, Kanyā Pūjā and the Sandhi hour of Cāmuṇḍā.

WHERE WE ARE

The nine nights are nearly over. On the last days two great observances meet: Sarasvatī Pūjā, when books, pens and instruments are placed before the Goddess of learning, and Durgāṣṭamī, the holy eighth day, with Kanyā Pūjā, the worship of young girls, and the intense Sandhi Pūjā.

In Karnataka homes Sarasvatī arrives on the day of the Mūla star; in Bengal the eighth day is the height of Durgā Pūjā. This part explains both.

SARASVATĪ ARRIVES ON MŪLA

When the books go to the Goddess

Āvāhana on Mūla. In South India, Sarasvatī is invited (āvāhana) on the day the moon is in the Mūla nakṣatra during Navarātri, usually around the seventh day. That is when the books go to her.

What is placed. Children’s schoolbooks, holy books, account ledgers, pens, musical instruments, paintbrushes: anything with which one learns or makes art is arranged before her image and decorated with turmeric, kuṅkuma and flowers.

The days that follow. Worship continues daily (on the following nakṣatras, by many almanacs, with the main worship on the day after Mūla), and the books stay with her until Vijayadaśamī, when they are taken back and used, often with a first act of writing or reading.

Elsewhere. Many regions keep Sarasvatī Pūjā on Navamī alone; Bengal’s great Sarasvatī Pūjā is a separate festival in spring (Vasanta Pañcamī). Traditions differ.

ZAPPY: Why does the timing go by a star?

PROF. BOLT: Mūla means “root”. Starting the worship of learning at the root: the almanac and the meaning agree nicely.

On the Mūla star, books and instruments go to Sarasvatī.

WHO IS SARASVATĪ

Her form and symbols

📖 Book

The Veda and all learning; also the written word, which she guards.

🎶 Vīṇā

Music and the arts. She is the patron of every musician and dancer.

📿 Rosary

Meditation and the repetition of mantras: learning turned inward.

🦢 Swan

The haṃsa is said to separate milk from water: the power to tell the true from the false.

ZAPPY: Is she Brahmā’s wife?

PROF. BOLT: In the Purāṇas, yes, and in the Devī Māhātmya the Goddess of the third story is Mahāsarasvatī. On these last nights she is the learning side of the one Devī.

Book, vīṇā, rosary, swan: knowledge, art, meditation, discernment.

PLACING THE BOOKS

A simple Sarasvatī Pūjā at home

The story

Each family has its own way, but a simple Sarasvatī Pūjā often goes like this:

Clean and arrange. Gather books, pens and instruments, clean them, and arrange them before Sarasvatī’s image, often on a raised stand.
Mark them. Dab each with turmeric and kuṅkuma, and lay a flower on it.
Invite her. Light a lamp, say a prayer or verse to Sarasvatī, and ask her to bless all learning in the house.
Offer. Fruit, sweets and flowers; children may write a sacred syllable or their name on a slate or in rice.
Leave them. The books rest with her until Vijayadaśamī, when they are taken back with a prayer and a first piece of study.

ZAPPY: What if I have an exam the next day?

PROF. BOLT: Families are practical; many keep a token book on the stand and study from the rest. Sarasvatī is the last goddess to object to studying.

Books cleaned, marked, offered, and returned blessed.

DURGĀṢṬAMĪ

The great eighth day

Mahāṣṭamī. The eighth day of the bright fortnight, Durgāṣṭamī or Mahāṣṭamī, is among the holiest days of the year for devotees of the Goddess. In the nine-Durgā order it belongs to Mahāgaurī.

In Bengal. Mahāṣṭamī is the high point of Durgā Pūjā: puṣpāñjali, the offering of flowers with chanted verses, draws whole neighbourhoods to the pandal in the morning.

Fasts. Many who do not fast for nine days fast on Aṣṭamī alone, ending after the evening worship.

Weapons and strength. In some regions the Goddess’s weapons are worshipped on this day too (astra pūjā), a cousin of the Āyudha Pūjā of the next day.

ZAPPY: Why is the eighth day so special?

PROF. BOLT: Because of what happens at its end: the Sandhi hour, when Cāmuṇḍā wins her great battle. Everything builds toward it.

Durgāṣṭamī: flowers, fasting and the build-up to the Sandhi hour.

KANYĀ PŪJĀ

Worshipping the Goddess in young girls

The custom. On Aṣṭamī or Navamī many families invite young girls, often nine, sometimes with a small boy as Bhairava or Gaṇeśa, and worship them as forms of the Devī. In Bengal this is Kumārī Pūjā, done on Mahāṣṭamī with one girl worshipped in great ceremony.

How it is done. The girls’ feet are washed, a tilaka applied, red threads tied, and they are seated on a mat and fed a festive meal, often halwa, pūrī and chickpeas in the north. They receive gifts: bangles, clothes, money.

Why. The Devī is worshipped in every woman; in a young girl, before the world has touched her, devotees say she is especially present. The nine Durgās become nine living girls.

A lesson. Many read the ritual as a yearly reminder of respect: a household that bows to its daughters for a day is asked to honour them all year.

ZAPPY: What if there aren’t nine girls nearby?

PROF. BOLT: Families invite whoever they can, even one. The ritual is about seeing the Goddess, not counting.

Kanyā Pūjā: the Goddess seen in young girls.

SANDHI PŪJĀ

The junction hour and Cāmuṇḍā (Devī Māhātmya 7)

The story

At the exact junction (sandhi) of Aṣṭamī and Navamī, the last 24 minutes of the eighth day and the first 24 of the ninth, comes the most intense worship of the festival.

The Devī Māhātmya tells how Śumbha sent the demons Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa to capture the Devī.
Her face darkened with anger, and from her brow sprang Kālī, terrible, gaunt, with a garland of heads.
Kālī tore through the demon army and cut off Caṇḍa’s and Muṇḍa’s heads, bringing them to the Devī.
The Devī named her Cāmuṇḍā, from Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa. Tradition says the battle ended at this junction hour; our Devī Māhātmya Part 12 gives the verses.
So in the Sandhi Pūjā Cāmuṇḍā is worshipped. In Bengal 108 lamps are lit and 108 lotuses offered, while the drums play at full force.

ZAPPY: Cāmuṇḍā! Like Cāmuṇḍī Hill?

PROF. BOLT: The same name. Mysuru’s Cāmuṇḍeśvarī carries this victory and Mahiṣa’s together.

At the junction hour, Cāmuṇḍā’s victory: 108 lamps, 108 lotuses.

THE LAST DAYS’ MEANING

Learning, purity and victory

Sarasvatī last. In the three-by-three count of Part 1, the last nights belong to Sarasvatī: after clearing and growing comes knowledge. Placing the books with her says that learning, too, is a gift of the Goddess.

Aṣṭamī’s purity. Mahāgaurī’s whiteness and the worship of young girls make Aṣṭamī a day of purity.

The junction. Many teachers read the Sandhi hour as the moment between two states, where the decisive inner victory is won. Junctions (dawn, dusk, the meeting of days) are always sacred in Indian worship.

Toward the tenth. After this the festival turns outward again: tools, weapons, the śamī tree and victory (Part 15).

ZAPPY: So the festival ends with books and battles?

PROF. BOLT: With wisdom and with victory. That is the right order: first know, then win.

Learning, purity and the junction hour: the festival’s inner climax.

SARASVATĪ IN KARNATAKA

Śṛṅgeri, Kollūr and the first letters

Śṛṅgeri. On the Tuṅgā river, the Śāradā Pīṭha founded by tradition by Ādi Śaṅkara keeps a grand Navarātri: the Goddess Śāradā is decorated in a different form each day (as Brahmāṇī, Vaiṣṇavī, Gajalakṣmī and others), with the Sarasvatī form on the Mūla day.

Kollūr. At Kollūr, below the Kodachādri hills, the Goddess Mūkāmbikā is visited by thousands for vidyārambha on Vijayadaśamī: children write their first letters in rice on a plate, guided by an elder.

At home. Families place books, pens, musical instruments and today laptops before Sarasvatī on Mūla and leave them till Vijayadaśamī; children are told not to study for those days, then begin again blessed.

Music. Navarātri is also a season of concerts and of musicians worshipping their instruments: Sarasvatī holds the vīṇā.

ZAPPY: Can laptops be worshipped?

PROF. BOLT: Whatever you learn with, Zappy. Sarasvatī isn’t fussy about the tools, only the learning.

Learning, placed before the Goddess.
