PART 8 OF 16 · NIGHT 5 · MOTHER OF SKANDA

Skandamātā

Night 5 · Mother of Skanda · explained and illustrated

The Mother and her son

HOW THIS WORKS: Her name, the story of Skanda’s birth to defeat Tāraka, her form on the lotus, her verse word by word, her night and her meaning.

WHERE WE ARE

Last night Kūṣmāṇḍā made the universe with a smile. Tonight the Goddess is a mother holding her child: Skandamātā, “mother of Skanda”. Skanda is the warrior son of Śiva and Pārvatī, known in the south as Subrahmaṇya, Kārttikeya and Murugan.

Our Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī series tells his story fully; here we look at it through his mother’s eyes.

ZAPPY: So the fifth Durgā is a mom?

PROF. BOLT: The most powerful kind: she raised the general of the gods’ army. And she sits calmly on a lotus while doing it.

ZAPPY: Who is Skanda?

PROF. BOLT: The six-faced war-god, son of Śiva and Pārvatī, who leads the gods’ army: Subrahmaṇya, Kārttikeya, Murugan.

THE MOTHER OF THE WAR-GOD

Skanda-mātā: who she is

Her name. Skandamātā is simply “the mother (mātā) of Skanda”. She is Pārvatī as the mother of the war-god, and the fifth night honours motherhood.

Her son. Skanda is the commander of the gods’ army, riding a peacock and carrying a spear (vel). In Karnataka he is worshipped as Subrahmaṇya at Kukke and Ghāṭi, whom our series visits.

On a lotus. She is often shown seated on a lotus, and so is also called Padmāsanā, “she who sits on a lotus”.

Two for one. Devotees say that worshipping her also honours Skanda on her lap, so the fifth night carries a double blessing.

ZAPPY: Two blessings in one night?

PROF. BOLT: Mother and son, together. In her lap the warrior is a child; in her blessing the child becomes a warrior.

Skandamātā: Pārvatī as mother, Skanda on her lap.

WHY SKANDA WAS BORN

Her story, from the Purāṇas

The story

The gods needed a commander only Śiva’s son could be.

The demon Tāraka won a boon: only a son of Śiva could kill him. Śiva, lost in meditation after Satī, seemed sure never to have a son.
Tāraka ran riot over heaven, sure of his safety.
Pārvatī’s penance (night two) and her marriage to Śiva (night three) changed everything. From Śiva’s fiery energy, carried by Agni and the Gaṅgā and nursed by the six Kṛttikā stars, a child was born: Skanda, Kārttikeya, with six faces.
Pārvatī embraced him and the six children became one. Tellings differ; our Subrahmaṇya series gives the details.
Still a youth, Skanda led the gods’ army and killed Tāraka. His mother is honoured as Skandamātā, the mother who made the victory possible.

ZAPPY: So all of Pārvatī’s story leads here?

PROF. BOLT: In a sense. The penance and the wedding were for this: a son who would save the gods.

A demon only Śiva’s son could kill; a mother who gave that son.

HER FORM AND SYMBOLS

What the image teaches

🪷 Two lotuses

Both upper hands hold lotuses: purity and grace, held up for the devotee to see.

👶 Skanda on her lap

Her son sits with her, small and six-faced: strength that is still a child in his mother’s arms.

✋ Blessing

Her free hand gives abhaya, “do not fear”: a mother’s promise.

🦁 Lion and lotus seat

She rides a lion yet sits on a lotus: power and calm together.

ZAPPY: No weapons at all?

PROF. BOLT: Her son carries the spear. Her power here is her love and her blessing.

Lotuses, a blessing and a child: a mother’s strength.

IN THE VERSE

NAVADURGĀ STOTRA 5

HER MEDITATION VERSE (DHYĀNA)
siṃhāsanagatā nityaṃ padmāśritakaradvayā .śubhadāstu sadā devī skandamātā yaśasvinī ..
Ever seated on the lion, her two hands holding lotuses, may the glorious Devī Skandamātā always be the giver of good.
WordMeaning
siṃha-āsana-gatāseated on the lion
nityamalways
padma-āśrita-kara-dvayāher two hands holding lotuses
śubhadā astu sadāmay she always give good
yaśasvinīthe glorious one

A double meaning. Siṃhāsana means both “a lion seat” and “a throne”: she sits on her lion, and she sits enthroned. The verse lets you see both at once.

Text as in the Navadurgā Stotra (shlokam.org transliteration).
Seated on the lion-throne, lotuses in hand: may she always give good.

WORSHIP ON HER NIGHT

How devotees keep night 5

Mothers and children. She is prayed to by parents for their children, and by those hoping for a child.

With Skanda. Some add a prayer to Subrahmaṇya on this night, honouring both mother and son.

Calm worship. Her verse has no weapons: a quiet night of lotus flowers, a lamp and her name.

Offerings and colours. A widely followed North Indian custom offers bananas on this night; lists differ between families and regions. Many cities also publish a “colour of the day” for each night, but that colour is set each year by the weekday, so we do not fix one here.

ZAPPY: Can children pray to her too?

PROF. BOLT: Especially children. She is a mother first.

Night five: pray for the children, and greet Skanda with his mother.

THE INNER READING

What many teachers see in her

Love that protects. After courage and creative warmth, the fifth night honours the love that protects and raises others.

Purity of speech. Some teachers link her with the throat centre: the mother teaches the child to speak, and pure words come from a pure heart.

Victory through others. She wins the war through her son. Many read this as the power of nurturing others to do great things.

The yogic map. A modern yogic reading links each night to a centre of the subtle body; for Skandamātā it is viśuddha, the throat centre. This mapping is popular but recent, and teachers differ; take it as a meditation aid, not scripture.

ZAPPY: So her power is giving power away?

PROF. BOLT: A mother’s power is to make someone else strong. That is the fifth night’s lesson.

Skandamātā gives the love that makes others strong.

THE SIX MOTHERS

How Skanda got six faces

The story

Mahābhārata (Vana Parvan) and the Śiva and Skanda Purāṇas; tellings differ in detail.

Only a son of Śiva could kill the demon Tāraka. Śiva’s fiery seed was too hot for anyone to hold: Agni, the fire god, carried it and gave it to the Gaṅgā.
The river could not bear it either and left it in a thicket of reeds (śara-vaṇa) by her bank. There a child was born: Śaravaṇabhava.
The six Kṛttikās, the Pleiades, came to bathe, found the child, and each wanted to nurse him; he grew six faces to drink from all six. Hence Kārttikeya and Ṣaṇmukha.
Pārvatī came, took him in her arms, and (in the southern telling) gathered six infants into one child with six faces and twelve arms.
So Skanda has many mothers and one mother: Skandamātā is Pārvatī holding him on her lap. His war is told in our Ṣaṣṭhī article.

ZAPPY: Six mothers?

PROF. BOLT: Six star-mothers and one Goddess, Zappy. Many tellings, one well-loved child.

Six mothers, one lap.

THE MOTHER AS POWER

Why the warrior’s mother is worshipped

The image. Skandamātā sits on a lion, holding lotuses, with the child Skanda on her lap. She is a mother first; the lion says her motherhood has a warrior’s strength.

Mother of the general. Skanda leads the gods’ army; the Goddess who bore and raised him stands behind every victory. Many read the fifth night as the honour due to every mother. (Interpretation.)

In Karnataka. Skanda is Subrahmaṇya, worshipped at Kukke and Ghāṭi; on this night families who keep Subrahmaṇya vows often remember him with his mother.

Children. Couples praying for children, and parents praying for them, often keep this night with special care. (Popular practice.)

ZAPPY: The general’s mother gets her own night?

PROF. BOLT: Of course, Zappy. Where do you think he learned courage?

Behind the hero, the mother.
