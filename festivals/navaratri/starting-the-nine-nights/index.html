Starting the Nine Nights

Ghaṭasthāpana, the Kalaśa and the Lamp · explained and illustrated

The pot, the shoots and the flame

HOW THIS WORKS: We walk through the first morning piece by piece: the kalaśa, the grain, the unbroken lamp, a simple daily worship, fasting, the reading, and how a Karnataka home does it.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 2 we heard the story. Now we come home. Before the nine Durgās arrive one by one, the house has to be made ready to receive the Goddess. On the first morning of Navarātri, families install a kalaśa, sow grain, and light a lamp. This is Ghaṭasthāpana, “establishing the pot”.

This part explains each piece: what the kalaśa stands for, why barley is sown, what the unbroken lamp means, how a simple daily worship runs, and how fasting and reading fit in. Customs differ by family and region; we describe the common shape and say where traditions part.

THE FIRST MORNING

Pratipadā: the day of invitation

Cleaning first. The house is cleaned, and the place of worship washed and decorated. Many homes hang a toraṇa, a string of mango leaves, over the door and draw a rangoli (Kannada rangōli) at the threshold, as for any honoured guest.

The vow. Before anything else, the head of the household takes a saṅkalpa, a short spoken resolve naming the day, the place and the purpose: to worship the Goddess for these nine nights. A vow gives shape to the days that follow.

The right hour. Families who follow the almanac choose an auspicious time in the morning of pratipadā for setting up the kalaśa. Almanacs differ by place, so we leave the hour to your local pañcāṅga.

An invitation. The whole morning has one mood: the Goddess is being invited to stay. Everything that follows (pot, grain, lamp) is a way of making her welcome.

ZAPPY: Why mango leaves?

PROF. BOLT: They are the classic sign of an auspicious occasion in South Indian homes: weddings, house-warmings, festivals. Green leaves at the door say “something good is happening here”.

Clean, vow, decorate: the house is made ready for a guest.

THE KALAŚA EXPLAINED

A pot that holds the whole world

🏺 The pot

The kalaśa is filled with water, often with a coin, rice grains, turmeric or other small offerings dropped in. It is treated as a seat (pīṭha) into which the deity is invited.

🌿 Leaves and coconut

Five (or more) mango leaves are set round its mouth and a coconut placed on top, often wrapped in red cloth. Many read the pot as the body, the leaves as life and the coconut as the head.

🌊 What it stands for

Ritual verses recited over the kalaśa invite the sacred rivers and the gods into its water. A full pot (pūrṇa-kumbha) is an ancient Indian sign of plenty and welcome.

🙏 The Goddess in the pot

With the invitation (āvāhana), the Devī is worshipped in the kalaśa for nine days. At the end she is bid farewell, and the water is sprinkled in the house or poured at the foot of a tree.

ZAPPY: So the pot is the Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: The pot is where she is asked to sit. The tradition says the divine can be invited into a lamp, an image, a pot of water or a heart.

A full pot, leaves and a coconut: a seat for the Goddess.

SOWING THE GRAIN

The green shoots of the nine nights

The custom. In much of North and East India, a shallow bed of soil is laid near the kalaśa and barley (jau) or other grain is sown in it on the first day. It is watered daily, and by the ninth day the shoots stand tall and green. In Punjab and around it they are called khetrī or jaware.

What it means. The shoots are read as the first harvest offered to the Mother, and as a sign of the household’s fortune for the year: tall, healthy shoots are a good omen. The festival’s roots in the farming year show clearly here.

At the end. On Vijayadaśamī the shoots are cut and offered; in many homes they are tucked behind the ear or into the turban, or shared with family and friends as a blessing.

In the south. Karnataka homes more often skip the grain bed. There the doll steps (Part 13) and the daily lamp carry the festival, though some families do sow navadhānya, nine grains.

ZAPPY: Does it matter if the shoots come up crooked?

PROF. BOLT: Only to the anxious! It is a hopeful sign, not an exam. Water them, enjoy them, give them away.

Grain sown on day one, green by day nine: the harvest offered to the Mother.

THE LAMP THAT DOES NOT GO OUT

Akhaṇḍa jyoti

An unbroken flame. Many households light an akhaṇḍa jyoti, an “unbroken lamp”, at Ghaṭasthāpana and keep it burning day and night through all nine nights, topping up oil or ghee and trimming the wick.

Why unbroken. The flame stands for the Goddess’s presence in the home. Keeping it alive is a quiet nine-day vigil; someone in the family is always, in a sense, awake for her.

Care and safety. It is usually set in a glass chimney or a protected corner, away from curtains and draughts. Families that cannot watch a flame all night simply light the lamp morning and evening; no tradition asks you to risk your house.

Light as worship. Whatever form it takes, the lamp is the simplest offering: in Indian worship, light (dīpa) is offered to every deity, and Navarātri makes the offering last.

ZAPPY: What if it goes out by accident?

PROF. BOLT: Relight it with a prayer. Devotion matters more than a perfect record.

One lamp, nine nights: a vigil of light.

A SIMPLE DAILY WORSHIP

How each of the nine days can run

The story

Every family has its own order, but a simple daily worship often runs like this:

Light. Wash, sit before the kalaśa and image, and light the lamp and incense.
Invoke. Remember the Devī of the day: in many homes, one of the nine Durgās (Parts 4–12), with her verse.
Offer. Flowers, kuṅkuma, a fruit or a sweet, and water, as one would serve an honoured guest.
Read or chant. A chapter of the Devī Māhātmya, the Lalitā Sahasranāma, or simply her name.
Ārati. Wave the camphor or ghee lamp, ring the bell, bow, and share the offered food (prasāda).

ZAPPY: How long does that take?

PROF. BOLT: Ten minutes or two hours. Length isn’t the point; showing up every day for nine days is.

Light, invoke, offer, read, ārati: a guest honoured every day.

FASTING AND FOOD

Customs that differ widely

Many ways to fast. Some devotees eat one meal a day for all nine days; some take only fruit and milk; some fast on the first and last days; some on Aṣṭamī alone; and many families do not fast at all. There is no single rule across India.

Fasting foods. In the north, special flours (buckwheat, water-chestnut), sago and potatoes replace grain on fast days. In the south, fasting is usually lighter, and festive cooking for the Goddess (and for guests) is a bigger part of the nine nights.

Food offered. In many homes each night has its own offering (naivedya) for the Goddess of that night, later shared as prasāda. We give the common North Indian customs in each Navadurgā part, and note where lists differ.

A word of sense. A fast is a discipline, not a punishment. Children, the elderly, the unwell and pregnant women are traditionally excused, and anyone with a medical condition should follow their doctor rather than a custom.

ZAPPY: Is a fast required to keep Navarātri?

PROF. BOLT: No. Many great devotees never fasted. Fasting is one tool among many: prayer, reading, service and kindness are others.

Fasting is optional and varied; offering food to the Goddess is near universal.

READING THE DEVĪ MĀHĀTMYA

Seven hundred verses over nine nights

The text. The Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts, also called the Durgā Saptaśatī or Caṇḍī Pāṭha, has about 700 verses in 13 chapters. It is the scripture of Navarātri above all others.

How it is spread. Some read the whole text every day; many divide it over the nine days so that it is finished by Navamī. A widely used plan reads the first story (chapter 1) early, the middle story (chapters 2–4) next, and the long last story (chapters 5–13) over the remaining days. Families and teachers use different divisions.

Before and after. Traditionally the reading is framed by the Devī Kavaca, Argalā and Kīlaka at the start (our Parts 1 to 4) and closing hymns at the end.

If that’s too much. Read one chapter a day with its meaning, or just the hymns: the Śakrādi Stuti (chapter 4), the Yā Devī verses (chapter 5) and the Nārāyaṇī Stuti (chapter 11). Our series gives the full text and meaning of each.

ZAPPY: Do I have to read it in Sanskrit?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition prizes the Sanskrit, but understanding is the point. Read the meaning first, then the verses. Our IAST guide will help you pronounce it.

Seven hundred verses, nine nights: read the Goddess’s own story to her.

IN A KARNATAKA HOME

Where the doll steps take the stage

The steps go up. In old Mysuru homes the first day of Navarātri is the day the gombe, the festival dolls, are brought out and arranged on steps, with the paṭṭada gombe, the royal doll couple, in pride of place. This often takes the place that the grain bed and kalaśa hold in the north. Part 13 tells the whole custom.

Cāmuṇḍī in the home. Many families also worship Cāmuṇḍeśvarī daily through the nine nights, often with a lamp before her picture, and read or hear the Devī Māhātmya.

Sarasvatī comes later. Books and instruments are placed for worship on the day of the Mūla star, near the end, and taken up again on Vijayadaśamī (Part 14).

Evenings of visiting. Each evening, women and children visit one another’s homes to see the dolls, sing, and receive a small gift of turmeric, kuṅkuma and a sweet.

ZAPPY: So Mysuru homes don’t do Ghaṭasthāpana?

PROF. BOLT: Some do, many don’t; the dolls are the local way of welcoming the Mother. Same invitation, different furniture.

In Karnataka, the doll steps are the welcome.
