The Devī and Mahiṣa

HOW THIS WORKS: The story told in order, from heaven lost to the Goddess born of the gods’ light, the battle and Mahiṣa’s many shapes, then what it means and why Mysuru carries the buffalo’s name.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 1 we drew the map of Navarātri. Now the story behind it. Every Navarātri points back to one great victory: the Devī slaying Mahiṣāsura, the buffalo demon who had taken heaven from the gods.

The story is told in the Devī Māhātmya (chapters 2 to 4), the scripture recited through the nine nights. Our Devī Māhātmya Parts 7 to 9 go through it verse by verse; here we tell it as one story, then ask what it means, and why Mysuru carries its name.

ZAPPY: Is Mahiṣa just a buffalo?

PROF. BOLT: He is a demon who can take any form. The buffalo is his favourite, and his name: mahiṣa means buffalo.

HEAVEN LOST

Devī Māhātmya 2.1–8

The story

Long ago the gods and the demons fought a war that lasted a hundred years.

Mahiṣāsura led the demons; Indra led the gods. After a hundred years the gods were beaten.
Mahiṣa took Indra’s throne and made himself lord of heaven. He took the places of the Sun, the Moon, Agni, Vāyu, Yama and Varuṇa as well.
The gods, driven out, wandered the earth like mortals.
Led by Brahmā, they went to Viṣṇu and Śiva and told them everything.
As they listened, Viṣṇu and Śiva grew furious, their faces twisted with anger.

ZAPPY: Why didn’t the gods just fight harder?

PROF. BOLT: They had fought for a hundred years. The story is saying: some troubles are beyond what even the strong can solve alone.

Mahiṣa takes heaven; the gods come to Viṣṇu and Śiva for help.

THE GODDESS BORN OF LIGHT

Devī Māhātmya 2.9–18

The story

Then something happened that had never happened before.

From Viṣṇu’s face, full of anger, came a great light; from Śiva and Brahmā came lights too.
Light poured from the bodies of Indra and all the other gods, and all of it ran together.
The gods saw it as a mountain of fire, filling every direction with flame.
That light, born of all the gods, became a woman, her radiance filling the three worlds.
Each part of her came from a god: her face from Śiva, her arms from Viṣṇu, her hair from Yama, her feet from Brahmā.

ZAPPY: So she is made of all the gods?

PROF. BOLT: Their power, gathered into one. Many read it the other way round too: the gods’ powers were always hers, and now she shows herself.

Not one god’s power but all of them together: that is the Devī.

THE GIFTS OF THE GODS

Devī Māhātmya 2.19–30: every god gives her a weapon

🔱 Śiva and Viṣṇu

Śiva drew a trident out of his own and gave it to her; Viṣṇu brought a discus out of his own discus.

⚡ Indra, Varuṇa, Agni, Vāyu

Indra gave a thunderbolt and the bell from his elephant Airāvata; Varuṇa a conch and a noose; Agni a spear; Vāyu a bow and quivers of arrows.

📿 Brahmā, Yama, Kāla, Sūrya

Brahmā gave a rosary and water pot; Yama his staff; Kāla (Time) a sword and shield; the Sun filled her pores with his rays.

🦁 Himavat and the rest

The Ocean gave a necklace and jewels; Viśvakarmā an axe and armour; Kubera a cup of drink; Śeṣa a serpent necklace; and Himavat, the mountain, gave her the lion.

ZAPPY: Why does she need so many weapons?

PROF. BOLT: Because each god gives what he has. She carries the whole of heaven’s strength in her hands.

Every god gives his best weapon; the lion comes from the mountain.

THE BATTLE

Devī Māhātmya 2.31–3.19

The story

She laughed aloud, and her roar shook the worlds.

Mahiṣa heard the sound and ran toward it with all his army: “What is this?”
He saw her, her light filling the three worlds, her feet bending the earth, her crown scraping the sky.
His generals Cikṣura, Cāmara, Udagra, Mahāhanu, Bāṣkala and many more attacked with thousands of chariots, elephants and horses.
She cut down their weapons as if in play; from her sighs, armies of her own sprang up and fought beside her. Her lion, shaking its mane, roamed the field.
One after another the generals fell, until only Mahiṣa himself was left.

ZAPPY: She was smiling while fighting?

PROF. BOLT: The text says she fought “as if in play”. For the Mother, this battle is not a struggle. The struggle is ours; she is simply putting things right.

Her roar starts the battle; his whole army falls.

THE MANY SHAPES OF MAHIṢA

Devī Māhātmya 3.20–41

The story

Then Mahiṣa came himself, in his own buffalo form.

He charged her troops, tossing them with his horns, lashing with his tail; mountains and clouds were thrown about.
She caught him in her noose. He became a lion; she cut off its head, and he came out as a man with a sword.
She shot the man with arrows; he became a great elephant and dragged her lion. She cut off its trunk.
He became a buffalo again. She drank the divine drink and laughed: “Roar, roar for a moment, fool, while I drink.”
She leapt on him, pressed his neck with her foot and struck with her spear. He began to come out of his own mouth, and with her great sword she cut off his head.

ZAPPY: Why does he keep changing?

PROF. BOLT: Many read Mahiṣa as the ego: chase it out of one shape and it slips into another. Only when she pins it down with her foot does it finally end.

Buffalo, lion, man, elephant: she follows every disguise to the end.

THE GODS’ HYMN AND HER PROMISE

Devī Māhātmya 4: the Śakrādi Stuti

Praise. When Mahiṣa fell, the gods led by Indra (Śakra) bowed and sang a long hymn, the Śakrādi Stuti. They praise her as the power behind the whole world, as good fortune in the homes of the good and misfortune in the homes of the wicked. Our Devī Māhātmya Part 9 reads it verse by verse.

A boon. Pleased, the Devī offered them a boon. The gods asked only this: that whenever they remembered her in danger, she would come and destroy their troubles, and that she would bless anyone who praised her with this hymn.

Her promise. She agreed, and vanished. This promise is the heart of Navarātri: the Mother who came once comes again whenever she is called. Every recitation in the nine nights is a calling.

Why it is read now. Because the story ends in a promise, it is read at the time of year set aside for the Goddess, and above all on the ninth and tenth days, when the victory is remembered.

ZAPPY: So she isn’t gone after the fight?

PROF. BOLT: She vanished, but she promised to return. That is why the festival repeats every year.

The gods’ only wish: “come whenever we remember you”. She said yes.

WHAT THE STORY MEANS

Readings across the tradition

The plain reading. The story is first of all what it says: the gods were helpless and the Mother saved them. Devotees take it as a promise for their own troubles.

The inner reading. Many teachers read Mahiṣa, the buffalo, as heaviness and pride: inertia (tamas) wearing the crown of heaven. The Devī, made of all the gods’ light, is the awakened power that alone can overcome it.

The shape-changing. The buffalo that becomes a lion, a man and an elephant is read as the ego slipping from one disguise to the next. The Devī’s foot on his neck is the moment it can no longer escape.

One power, many gods. Philosophers in the Śākta tradition read the birth from all the gods’ light as a statement that the Goddess is the single power (śakti) behind every god. These are interpretations; the text itself tells the story.

ZAPPY: Which reading is right?

PROF. BOLT: They stack, like floors of one house. The story, then the meaning inside, then the philosophy. You can live on any floor.

Outer story, inner battle: the same victory twice.

CĀMUṆḌĪ AND MYSURU

Why the city carries the buffalo’s name

The name. A local tradition holds that the city’s old name, Mahiṣūru, “buffalo town”, comes from Mahiṣāsura, who ruled here until the Devī slew him on the hill above. Mysuru is the later form.

Cāmuṇḍeśvarī. On that hill stands the temple of Cāmuṇḍeśvarī, the Devī as Mahiṣa’s slayer and the family deity of the Woḍeyars. A great statue of Mahiṣāsura, sword in hand, stands on the hill road. Our Around Mysuru article on Cāmuṇḍī Hill tells the temple’s story.

The name Cāmuṇḍā. In the Devī Māhātmya the name Cāmuṇḍā is earned later, in chapter 7, when Kālī slays the demons Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa (Part 12 of our series). At Mysuru, tradition joins the two victories in one goddess.

The festival. That is why Mysuru keeps Navarātri as its own festival: the Goddess of the story is the Goddess of the hill. On Vijayadaśamī her golden image rides on an elephant through the city (Part 16).

ZAPPY: So the demon is from Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: So the local story says. Scholars debate the name; devotees don’t. Either way, the city is proud of its Goddess.

Mahiṣūru, Cāmuṇḍī Hill: the story lives on the map.

OTHER STORIES OF THE SEASON

Not only Mahiṣa

🏹 Rāma and Rāvaṇa

The tenth day remembers Rāma’s victory over Rāvaṇa. Bengal’s tradition says Rāma first worshipped Durgā in autumn to win it.

🌳 The Pāṇḍavas and the śamī

The Pāṇḍavas hid their weapons in a śamī tree in their year in disguise and took them back for the battle: hence śamī worship on Vijayadaśamī.

⚔️ Śumbha and Niśumbha

The last story of the Devī Māhātmya: the demon brothers, Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, Raktabīja. Kālī and Cāmuṇḍā belong here, and the seventh and eighth nights often recall it.

🏡 Umā comes home

In Bengal the Goddess is also the daughter Umā visiting her parents with Gaṇeśa, Kārttikeya, Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī for four days.

ZAPPY: So many stories for one festival?

PROF. BOLT: A great festival collects stories the way a river collects streams. Part 15 tells the Rāma and Pāṇḍava ones in full.

Mahiṣa first, then Rāma, the Pāṇḍavas and Umā: one season, many victories.
