THE PAÑCĀṄGA

Tithi, vāra, nakṣatra, yoga, karaṇa

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Why is Dīpāvali in October one year and November the next? Why does Karnataka call a month by a different name from Uttar Pradesh? Why do two families keep Janmāṣṭamī on different days?

The answers lie in the pañcāṅga, the Hindu almanac of “five limbs”. This guide explains it simply, so that every festival guide that follows makes sense.

AT A GLANCE

What a pañcāṅga is
WordPañca + aṅga: “five limbs”
The fiveTithi, vāra, nakṣatra, yoga, karaṇa
KindLunisolar: months follow the moon, the year is kept in step with the sun
UseFixes festival dates, fasts, auspicious times
RegionsLunar in Karnataka and most of India; solar in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Bengal, Assam, Odisha
This yearParābhava saṃvatsara, from Ugādi, 19 March 2026
Why read thisEvery festival guide in this series uses these words

ZAPPY: Why does Dīpāvali move every year?

PROF. BOLT: Because it follows the moon, Zappy. The pañcāṅga tells you where the moon will be.

A pañcāṅga, “five limbs”, is the traditional Hindu almanac. Its five limbs are tithi, the lunar day; vāra, the weekday; nakṣatra, the moon’s star; yoga, a sum of sun and moon positions; and karaṇa, half a tithi.

The calendar is lunisolar: months follow the moon, while an occasional extra month keeps the year in step with the sun. Some states, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Bengal, Assam and Odisha, use solar months instead.

Every guide in this series uses these terms, so this is the place to start.

TITHI

The lunar day
DefinitionThe time the moon takes to gain 12° on the sun
Count30 in a lunar month: 15 bright (śukla), 15 dark (kṛṣṇa)
NamesPratipadā, dvitīyā, tṛtīyā … caturthī … aṣṭamī, navamī … ekādaśī … caturdaśī
EndsPūrṇimā (full moon) and amāvāsyā (new moon)
LengthVaries, roughly 19 to 26 hours
Odd daysA tithi can be “skipped” (kṣaya) or span two sunrises (adhika)

ZAPPY: A day that isn’t 24 hours?

PROF. BOLT: A tithi is moon-time, not clock-time. That’s why festivals sometimes fall on two dates.

A tithi is the time the moon takes to move 12° further from the sun (in the bright fortnight) or 12° closer (in the dark). There are thirty in a lunar month, fifteen in each fortnight, ending at full moon and new moon.

Because the moon’s speed varies, a tithi can be shorter or longer than a day. Sometimes a tithi begins and ends between two sunrises and is “lost”; sometimes one spans two sunrises. This is why festivals occasionally fall on two different dates in two almanacs.

Most Hindu festivals are fixed by tithi: Gaṇeśa Caturthī is the fourth, Rāma Navamī the ninth, Kṛṣṇa Janmāṣṭamī the eighth.

VĀRA AND NAKṢATRA

The weekday and the moon’s mansion
VāraThe seven-day week, sunrise to sunrise
DaysRavi, Soma, Maṅgala, Budha, Guru, Śukra, Śani
PlanetsSun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn
NakṣatraThe moon’s position among 27 star-groups, each 13°20′
ExamplesAśvinī first; Rohiṇī, Puṣya, Śravaṇa, Kṛttikā…
FestivalsKārtika Dīpa by Kṛttikā; Śrāvaṇa by Śravaṇa

ZAPPY: Why is Tuesday Maṅgala?

PROF. BOLT: Each weekday is named after a planet, like Monday after the moon. Same idea, older names.

Vāra is the weekday. The Indian week has the same seven planetary days as the Western one: Ravi (Sun), Soma (Moon), Maṅgala (Mars), Budha (Mercury), Guru or Bṛhaspati (Jupiter), Śukra (Venus) and Śani (Saturn). A day runs from sunrise to sunrise.

The nakṣatra is the moon’s position among 27 star-groups, each 13°20′ of the sky, beginning with Aśvinī. Some festivals depend on the nakṣatra as well as, or instead of, the tithi: Kārtika Dīpa on Kṛttikā, Ōṇam on Śravaṇa (Tiruvōṇam).

YOGA AND KARAṆA

The last two limbs
YogaSun’s and moon’s longitudes added, divided into 27 parts
NamesViṣkambha, Prīti, Āyuṣmān … Siddhi, Vyatīpāta …
KaraṇaHalf a tithi: 6° of sun–moon separation
KindsEleven: seven repeating, four fixed
60 halvesKiṃstughna first, the seven repeat eight times, three fixed end the month
ViṣṭiAlso called bhadrā; avoided for new beginnings

ZAPPY: Do people still use yoga and karaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Mostly priests and astrologers, to pick good times. For festivals, tithi matters most.

Yoga adds the longitudes of sun and moon and divides the result into 27 parts; karaṇa is half a tithi, 6° of separation between moon and sun. There are eleven karaṇas: seven repeat eight times in a month, and four are fixed, filling the sixty halves.

Yoga and karaṇa matter most to priests and astrologers choosing muhūrtas, auspicious times. One karaṇa, Viṣṭi or Bhadrā, is widely avoided for beginnings; Holikā Dahana, for example, is timed to avoid it.

THE TWELVE MONTHS

From Caitra to Phālguna
SpringCaitra, Vaiśākha
SummerJyeṣṭha, Āṣāḍha
RainsŚrāvaṇa, Bhādrapada
AutumnĀśvayuja, Kārtika
Pre-winterMārgaśīrṣa, Pauṣa
Winter / lateMāgha, Phālguna
NamingEach month named for the nakṣatra near which the full moon falls

ZAPPY: Six seasons?

PROF. BOLT: Six ṛtus of two months each: vasanta, grīṣma, varṣā, śarad, hemanta, śiśira.

The twelve lunar months begin with Caitra in spring and end with Phālguna. Each takes its name from the nakṣatra near which its full moon falls: Caitra from Citrā, Vaiśākha from Viśākhā, Kārtika from Kṛttikā, and so on.

Two months make each of six seasons (ṛtus): vasanta (spring), grīṣma (summer), varṣā (rains), śarad (autumn), hemanta (pre-winter) and śiśira (winter).

Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Maharashtra begin the year with Caitra at Ugādi or Guḍi Pāḍavā.

AMĀNTA AND PŪRṆIMĀNTA

When does a month end?
AmāntaMonth ends at new moon
WhoKarnataka, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Goa, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana
PūrṇimāntaMonth ends at full moon
WhoNepal and most of India north of the Vindhyas
EffectIn the dark fortnight the north is already in the next month
ExampleMahāśivarātri: Māgha kṛṣṇa in Karnataka, Phālguna kṛṣṇa in the north: the same night

ZAPPY: So the north and the south disagree?

PROF. BOLT: Only on the name of the month, Zappy. The night is the same.

There are two ways to end a lunar month. In the amānta system, used in Karnataka, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Goa, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, the month ends at new moon. In the pūrṇimānta system, used in Nepal and most of India north of the Vindhyas, it ends at full moon.

The bright fortnight has the same month name in both. In the dark fortnight, the north has already moved on to the next month. So Mahāśivarātri is Māgha kṛṣṇa caturdaśī in Karnataka and Phālguna kṛṣṇa caturdaśī in Uttar Pradesh, yet it is the same night everywhere.

This trips up many readers of festival lists; once you know it, the confusion disappears.

SOLAR MONTHS AND SAṄKRĀNTI

The sun’s calendar
RāśiThe zodiac divided into twelve signs of 30°
SaṅkrāntiThe sun’s entry into a sign
Solar monthNamed after the sign the sun is in
Makara SaṅkrāntiSun enters Capricorn, about 14–15 January
Meṣa SaṅkrāntiSun enters Aries, about 14 April: solar new year
Solar statesTamil Nadu, Kerala, Bengal, Assam, Odisha keep solar months

ZAPPY: Why is Saṅkrānti always mid-January?

PROF. BOLT: It follows the sun, not the moon. Solar festivals hardly move.

The sun’s path is divided into twelve rāśis, signs, of 30° each. The sun’s entry into a sign is a saṅkrānti, and in the solar calendar the month takes the sign’s name.

Solar festivals barely move against the Western calendar: Makara Saṅkrānti falls around 14–15 January and Meṣa Saṅkrānti, the solar new year (Viṣu, Puthāṇḍu, Pohela Boishakh, Bihu), around 14–15 April.

Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal, Assam and Odisha keep solar months for their civil calendar, while still using tithis for many festivals.

ADHIKA MĀSA

The extra month
ProblemTwelve lunar months are about 354 days, some 11 days short of the solar year
FixAn extra month about every 32–33 months
RuleA lunar month with no saṅkrānti is repeated
NamesAdhika, puruṣottama or mala māsa
2026Adhika Jyeṣṭha, 17 May – 15 June 2026
KṣayaVery rarely, a month is dropped

ZAPPY: A thirteen-month year?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. 2026–27 had one. It keeps Ugādi in spring and Dīpāvali in autumn.

Twelve lunar months come to about 354 days, some 11 days short of the solar year. Left alone, festivals would drift through the seasons. So, about every 32–33 months, an extra month is added: when a lunar month passes with no saṅkrānti, it is repeated.

The extra month is called adhika (extra), puruṣottama (dedicated to Viṣṇu) or mala (impure) māsa. Weddings and new ventures are generally avoided in it, but devotions are encouraged.

In 2026, Jyeṣṭha was doubled: adhika Jyeṣṭha ran from 17 May to 15 June. Very rarely, the opposite happens and a month is dropped (kṣaya māsa).

ERAS AND THE SIXTY YEARS

Counting the years
Vikrama SaṃvatBegins 57 BCE; 2083 from spring 2026
ŚakaBegins 78 CE; common in the south; 1948 from Ugādi 2026
Kali YugaTraditional reckoning, about 5,127 years
SaṃvatsaraA cycle of 60 named years
2026–27Parābhava, the 40th, after Viśvāvasu
UgādiWhere the named year begins in Karnataka and the Telugu states

ZAPPY: Sixty names, then repeat?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. A person who lives to sixty sees their birth-year name again: the ṣaṣṭyabdapūrti.

Several eras are used. Vikrama Saṃvat begins in 57 BCE and is common in the north and west; its year 2083 began in spring 2026. The Śaka era begins in 78 CE and is common in the south; it is also the base of India’s national calendar. The Kali Yuga reckoning counts about 5,127 years.

In the south, years also have names, from a cycle of sixty: Prabhava, Vibhava, Śukla … Krodhi, Viśvāvasu, Parābhava … Akṣaya. The year that began at Ugādi in March 2026 is Parābhava, the fortieth.

Completing the cycle, at sixty, is celebrated with the ṣaṣṭyabdapūrti.

HOW A FESTIVAL IS DATED

Which day counts?
BasicA festival is set by a tithi in a month
Sunrise ruleMany use the tithi prevailing at sunrise
MiddayRāma Navamī: navamī at midday
MidnightKṛṣṇa Janmāṣṭamī: aṣṭamī at midnight
NightMahāśivarātri: caturdaśī through the night
SolarSaṅkrānti festivals follow the sun
ResultDifferent almanacs may give different days

ZAPPY: Why does my friend’s family keep Janmāṣṭamī a day later?

PROF. BOLT: Different rules, Zappy: some follow the tithi, some the Rohiṇī star. Both are right for them.

A festival is usually defined by a tithi in a month: Caitra śukla navamī for Rāma Navamī, Śrāvaṇa kṛṣṇa aṣṭamī for Janmāṣṭamī. Because tithis do not line up with days, rules decide which day counts.

Many festivals take the tithi prevailing at sunrise. Others look to a particular time: Rāma Navamī to midday, when Rāma was born; Janmāṣṭamī to midnight, when Kṛṣṇa was born, with some traditions also requiring the Rohiṇī star; Mahāśivarātri to the night.

So almanacs, and regions, sometimes disagree by a day. Each guide in this series notes such differences.

READING A PAÑCĀṄGA

A sample day
SaṃvatsaraParābhava
AyanaDakṣiṇāyana (sun moving south)
ṚtuHemanta (pre-winter)
MāsaKārtika / Mārgaśīrṣa
Pakṣa, tithiŚukla, with the tithi and its end time
Vāra, nakṣatraWeekday and the moon’s star, with end times
AlsoSunrise, sunset, rāhukāla, good and bad hours

ZAPPY: Rāhukāla?

PROF. BOLT: A daily period of about an hour and a half that many avoid for new beginnings. Every pañcāṅga lists it.

A printed pañcāṅga gives, for each day, the year (saṃvatsara), half-year (ayana), season (ṛtu), month (māsa), fortnight (pakṣa) and tithi, with the times at which the tithi, nakṣatra, yoga and karaṇa change. It also lists sunrise and sunset, and daily periods such as rāhukāla, yamagaṇḍa and gulika, which many avoid for new beginnings.

Today most people use apps or websites, but the structure is the same as the palm-leaf almanacs.

ZAPPY: Will these ever change?

PROF. BOLT: Never, Zappy. The tithi stays fixed; only the Western date moves each year.

These festivals are fixed by month, fortnight and tithi, so their place in the Hindu calendar never changes; only their date in the Western calendar moves from year to year. Month names here are Karnataka’s amānta names.

In the dark fortnight, northern (pūrṇimānta) calendars already use the next month’s name, as noted for Janmāṣṭamī. Regional rules sometimes add a condition, such as the Rohiṇī star for Janmāṣṭamī.

ZAPPY: Dīpāvali isn’t in Kārtika?

PROF. BOLT: Its big night is the amāvāsyā ending Āśvayuja in Karnataka’s reckoning; the north calls the same night Kārtika amāvāsyā.

Dīpāvali spans several tithis: Naraka Caturdaśī, the Lakṣmī Pūjā on amāvāsyā, and Bali Pāḍyami on the first of Kārtika. In amānta reckoning the amāvāsyā closes Āśvayuja; in the north the same night is called Kārtika amāvāsyā.

Kārtika Dīpa, especially in Tamil Nadu, is set by the Kṛttikā nakṣatra in the solar month of Kārttikai, usually at or near the full moon.

ZAPPY: So I can find any festival with this?

PROF. BOLT: With this table and a pañcāṅga, Zappy, yes. Month names are Karnataka’s amānta.

Some festivals follow the sun or a star instead of a tithi: Makara Saṅkrānti and the mid-April solar new years follow the sun; Ōṇam, Tai Pūsam and Ārdrā Darśanam combine a nakṣatra with a solar month. Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī is the śukla ekādaśī that falls in the solar month of Dhanus.

Monthly observances, Ekādaśī, Saṅkaṣṭī, Pradoṣa and Māsa Śivarātri, recur in every lunar month.
