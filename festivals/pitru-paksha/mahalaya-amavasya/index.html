The New Moon for All Ancestors · explained and illustrated

HOW THIS WORKS: The new moon for all ancestors, Mahālaya in Bengal, and the day in Karnataka, leading into Navarātri.

WHERE WE ARE

The fortnight closes with its greatest day: Mahālaya Amāvāsyā, also called Sarva Pitṛ Amāvāsyā, the new moon for all ancestors. Whoever was forgotten, or whose day is not known, is remembered today.

The next day the Goddess arrives: Navarātri begins. In Bengal, Mahālaya dawn is also the moment Durgā is invited, with the famous recitation of the Caṇḍī. Our Navarātri series · 16 articles takes it from there.

THE NEW MOON FOR ALL

Sarva Pitṛ Amāvāsyā

Everyone at once. On this new moon, families offer tarpaṇa and śrāddha for all their ancestors together: those whose death days are known, those whose are not, those who died without descendants, even friends and teachers.

Forgetting forgiven. If someone missed their parent’s day in the fortnight, this day makes up for it.

Rivers and tanks. Large crowds gather at rivers, tanks and sacred places to offer water.

Feeding. Feeding the poor and giving food grains is especially praised.

ZAPPY: Even people who weren’t family?

PROF. BOLT: Many offer a handful of water “for all who have no one to remember them”. That is one of the kindest rites in the tradition.

One day for every ancestor, known and unknown.

MAHĀLAYA IN BENGAL

The Goddess is invited

Before dawn. In Bengal, Mahālaya begins long before sunrise with the recitation of the Caṇḍī, the Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts, and devotional songs invoking Durgā. A famous radio programme of these verses has been broadcast on this dawn for generations.

Tarpaṇa at the ghāṭs. At dawn men offer tarpaṇa in the Gaṅgā (Hooghly) for their ancestors.

The eyes. In the image-makers’ workshops, the eyes of the Durgā images are traditionally painted on Mahālaya (cokṣu dān), “giving them sight”.

The countdown. From Mahālaya, Bengal counts down to the great days of Durgā Pūjā.

ZAPPY: Ancestors and the Goddess on the same morning?

PROF. BOLT: In Bengal, yes: tarpaṇa at the river, the Caṇḍī on the radio. Remembering those who gave you life, then calling the Mother of all life.

Mahālaya dawn: the ancestors honoured, the Goddess invited.

IN KARNATAKA

Mahālaya Amāvāsyā in the south

Paksha. In Karnataka the fortnight is commonly called Pakṣa or Mahālaya, and families perform the rite at home or at a temple, with a priest.

Foods. Families prepare favourite dishes of the departed along with traditional items for the offering, and feed guests.

Śrīraṅgapaṭṇa. Many go to the Kāverī at Śrīraṅgapaṭṇa’s Paścimavāhinī to perform the rites (Part 4).

Next day, Navarātri. The doll steps go up the next morning in Mysuru homes; see our Gombe Habba.

ZAPPY: From śrāddha to dolls overnight?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the rhythm of the season: remember, then celebrate.

In Karnataka: the rite at home or the Kāverī, then Navarātri.

RIVERS ON MAHĀLAYA

Where people gather

Holy rivers. The Gaṅgā, Godāvarī, Kāverī, Narmadā and other rivers see huge crowds on Mahālaya dawn.

Temples by water. Temple tanks and seashores (Rāmeśvaram, Gokarṇa) also host the rites.

Priests on the banks. Priests sit on the banks guiding families through tarpaṇa.

Personal too. Many simply offer water at home, facing south.

ZAPPY: Can I do it at home?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Water, sesame and the names, said with love, facing south.

River banks crowded at Mahālaya dawn.

FOR THOSE WITH NO ONE

A rite for the forgotten

A special offering. On Mahālaya many offer tarpaṇa for all who died with no one to remember them: those without children, those lost in accidents, wars or disasters.

Friends and teachers. Others offer for friends, teachers, and even animals they loved.

Universal. The rite widens from family to all beings.

Compassion. Many find this the most moving part of the fortnight.

ZAPPY: Even pets?

PROF. BOLT: Some families do. The rite’s heart is gratitude and compassion.

A handful of water for those with no one.

THE CROWS ON MAHĀLAYA

Waiting for a sign

The last day. On Mahālaya the food offering for crows is especially important.

The sign. Families wait; a crow eating is taken as a sign the ancestors are satisfied.

Patience. If none comes, families feed a cow or put the food in water.

Symbol. The crow’s visit closes the fortnight on a hopeful note.

ZAPPY: What if no crow comes?

PROF. BOLT: Feed a cow or another creature. The offering is never wasted.

A crow at the leaf: the fortnight closes.

FROM ANCESTORS TO THE MOTHER

The turn of the season

Darkness to light. The new moon of Mahālaya is the darkest night; the next morning Navarātri begins with the waxing moon.

Gratitude to celebration. The fortnight of looking back opens into nine nights of the Mother.

Mahālaya’s name. Mahālaya can be read as “the great abode”: the time when the ancestors visit, and when the Goddess is invited.

Our series. See Starting the Nine Nights.

ZAPPY: The calendar is beautifully arranged.

PROF. BOLT: Remember, then rejoice. That is how the year turns here.

From remembrance to the Mother’s nights.

MAHĀLAYA IN BENGAL’S MEMORY

A dawn people wait for

The broadcast. For generations Bengali families have woken before dawn on Mahālaya to the radio programme of Caṇḍī recitation and songs.

A shared memory. For many, Mahālaya means that voice, that hour and the smell of the season.

Tarpaṇa after. Then men go to the river for tarpaṇa.

The images. Artisans paint the Goddess’s eyes; the countdown to Durgā Pūjā begins.

ZAPPY: A radio programme is a tradition?

PROF. BOLT: For Bengal, a beloved one. Traditions are made of such things too.

Bengal’s Mahālaya: the voice before dawn.
