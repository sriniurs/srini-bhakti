The Fortnight of the Ancestors · explained and illustrated

Water and sesame for those before us

HOW THIS WORKS: What Pitṛ Pakṣa is, Karṇa’s story, who is remembered on which day, and why it is a time of restraint.

WHERE WE ARE

Before the Goddess comes for Navarātri, the dark fortnight before it belongs to the ancestors. Pitṛ Pakṣa, “the fortnight of the forefathers”, also called Mahālaya Pakṣa, is when Hindu families remember their departed parents and forebears with offerings of water, sesame and food.

This 5-part series explains the fortnight, what the rites mean, the great new moon that closes it, the holy places of śrāddha, and the tradition’s picture of life after death. It describes; it is not a priest’s manual.

THE FORTNIGHT OF THE ANCESTORS

What Pitṛ Pakṣa is

The pitṛs. In Hindu thought the pitṛs, the departed ancestors, live in their own world, the pitṛ-loka, and are nourished by the offerings of their descendants.

The fortnight. For fifteen days in the dark half of the lunar month before Navarātri, these offerings are made with special care. Each family usually performs śrāddha for a parent on the tithi (lunar day) of that parent’s death.

The three debts. Tradition speaks of three debts every person carries: to the gods, to the sages, and to the ancestors (pitṛ-ṛṇa). Śrāddha is how the third is honoured.

Gratitude. At its heart the fortnight is thanksgiving: remembering those whose lives made ours possible.

ZAPPY: Do the ancestors really get the food?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition says the offering reaches them through the rite. Whatever one believes, the remembering is real.

Fifteen days to remember and thank the ancestors.

KARṆA AND THE GOLDEN FOOD

The story behind the fortnight

The story

A popular story explains why the fortnight exists.

Karṇa of the Mahābhārata was famous for his generosity: he gave gold to anyone who asked.
When he died and reached heaven, he was offered gold and jewels to eat, but no food.
He asked Indra why. Indra told him: in life you gave gold, but never food or water to your ancestors.
Karṇa had not known who his ancestors were. He was allowed to return to earth for fifteen days to offer food and water in their memory.
Those fifteen days, the story says, became Pitṛ Pakṣa.

ZAPPY: So even Karṇa forgot?

PROF. BOLT: He didn’t know his own family, as you know from the Mahābhārata. The story says: don’t let your ancestors go unremembered.

Gold is not food: Karṇa returns to feed his ancestors.

WHO IS REMEMBERED, AND WHEN

The tithis of the fortnight

By tithi. A parent who died on, say, the fifth lunar day is remembered on the fifth day of Pitṛ Pakṣa, whatever the calendar date.

Special days. Some days have special uses in many traditions: Navamī for mothers (Avidhavā Navamī), Caturdaśī for those who died violently or untimely, Dvādaśī for sannyāsins.

When the day is unknown. If the death tithi is not known, or for all ancestors together, śrāddha is done on Mahālaya Amāvāsyā (Part 3).

Who performs it. Traditionally a son performs śrāddha; families today increasingly include daughters and other relatives. Customs differ by community.

ZAPPY: Can a daughter do it?

PROF. BOLT: Many families now say yes, and several traditions allow it. Ask your family priest; practice is changing.

Each ancestor has their own day in the fortnight.

A TIME OF RESTRAINT

What families avoid

No new beginnings. Many families avoid weddings, house-warmings, new purchases and starting ventures during the fortnight, considering it a time to look back, not forward.

Simple food. Some avoid non-vegetarian food, onion and garlic, and eat simply.

Not inauspicious, but different. The fortnight is not “bad luck”; it is set aside for a different purpose. Not everyone observes these restrictions.

Then joy. The fortnight ends, and the next day Navarātri begins.

ZAPPY: Why no shopping?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition keeps this fortnight for the past. The new things wait for Navarātri and Dīpāvalī.

A fortnight given to the past, before the festivals.

AN ANCIENT PRACTICE

From the Veda to today

Vedic roots. Offerings to the fathers (pitṛs) appear in the Ṛg Veda, which has hymns to them, and in the Gṛhya Sūtras, which describe the rites.

The Purāṇas. The Garuḍa, Viṣṇu and Matsya Purāṇas detail śrāddha and the special merit of the dark fortnight before Navarātri.

Across India. Every region keeps Pitṛ Pakṣa, with local names and customs.

Across faiths. Many cultures set aside days for ancestors; Pitṛ Pakṣa is India’s great one.

ZAPPY: The Ṛg Veda has hymns to ancestors?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, to the pitṛs. Remembering ancestors is among the oldest Indian practices.

From the Veda to today: remembering the fathers.

WOMEN AND PITṚ PAKṢA

Mothers remembered

Avidhavā Navamī. The ninth day of the fortnight is set aside in many traditions for married women who died before their husbands (sumaṅgalīs), and for mothers.

Mātāmaha. Many rites include the maternal line (mother’s father and forebears) alongside the paternal.

Women performing. Practice is changing: many families now have daughters perform śrāddha.

Feeding sumaṅgalīs. Some families feed married women on Navamī in memory of a mother.

ZAPPY: There’s a day just for mothers?

PROF. BOLT: Navamī, in many traditions. The fortnight remembers both lines.

Navamī: a day for mothers.

GIVING IN THE FORTNIGHT

Dāna

Giving on their behalf. Gifts made in the fortnight are made in the ancestors’ names.

What. Food grains, cloth, sesame, a cow (in the old texts), utensils, or money to the needy.

Feeding. Feeding the hungry is the most praised gift.

Today. Many give to charities, orphanages or old-age homes in their parents’ memory.

ZAPPY: Can I donate to a charity instead?

PROF. BOLT: Many do: it is giving in their name. The tradition praises feeding most of all.

Giving in their name.

REMEMBERING WELL

Beyond the rite

Stories. Many families use the fortnight to tell children about grandparents and great-grandparents.

Names. Saying the names in the rite keeps three generations alive in memory.

Their values. Remembering what they stood for is a way of honouring them.

A family tree. Some families write down their gotra and the names of ancestors for the next generation.

ZAPPY: So I should learn my great-grandparents’ names?

PROF. BOLT: The rite asks for them. Ask your elders before the names are lost.

Tell the children who came before.
