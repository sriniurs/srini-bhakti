Śrāddha and Tarpaṇa

What the Rites Mean · explained and illustrated

Water, sesame and rice

HOW THIS WORKS: Śrāddha, tarpaṇa and the piṇḍa explained element by element, and why crows, cows and dogs are fed.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 1 we saw what Pitṛ Pakṣa is. Now the rites themselves: śrāddha, the offering made with faith (śraddhā), and tarpaṇa, the offering of water.

We explain what each element means and why it is used. The rites themselves are done with a priest or by those trained in them, following the family’s tradition (Ṛg-, Yajur- or Sāma-vedic, Smārta or Vaiṣṇava).

ŚRĀDDHA

An offering made with faith

The name. Śrāddha comes from śraddhā, faith: an offering whose power lies in the faith with which it is made.

Feeding on their behalf. In the classic rite, invited brahmins (or other worthy guests) are fed as representatives of the ancestors, and their satisfaction is said to reach the pitṛs.

Simple food. The meal is pure vegetarian, often including foods the departed loved; customs differ by region.

Giving. Gifts of food, clothes and money to the needy are part of the rite.

ZAPPY: The guests eat for the ancestors?

PROF. BOLT: That is the idea: feeding the living as a channel to the dead. Feeding the hungry is never wasted.

Food offered with faith, through the living.

TARPAṆA

Water and sesame

The offering of water. Tarpaṇa means “satisfying”: water is poured from the hands, with black sesame and darbha grass, while the names of the ancestors are spoken.

Sesame. Black sesame (tila) is traditionally used only for the ancestors; it is said to be dear to them and to purify.

Darbha. A ring of sacred darbha (kuśa) grass, the pavitra, is worn on the finger, and the water flows over darbha blades.

The sacred thread. Those who wear the thread shift it to the right shoulder (prācīnāvīta) for rites to the ancestors, the reverse of rites for the gods.

ZAPPY: Why reverse everything?

PROF. BOLT: Rites for the ancestors mirror rites for the gods: south instead of east, right shoulder instead of left. It marks them as a different world.

Water, sesame and darbha: tarpaṇa.

PIṆḌA AND THE THREE GENERATIONS

Rice balls for father, grandfather and great-grandfather

Piṇḍa. Balls of cooked rice mixed with sesame, honey or ghee are offered on darbha grass.

Three generations. Traditionally three piṇḍas honour the father, grandfather and great-grandfather (with parallel offerings for the maternal line in many traditions).

Beyond three. Older generations are believed to have moved on; the rite looks after the three nearest.

After the rite. Piṇḍas are given to crows or cows, or placed in water.

ZAPPY: Why only three generations?

PROF. BOLT: In the tradition, the nearest three are still “pitṛs” in need; beyond them, the ancestors are said to have gone on.

Three rice balls, three generations.

CROWS, COWS AND DOGS

Feeding all beings

Crows. Before anyone eats, a portion is placed for crows. In popular belief the ancestors come as crows; if a crow eats quickly, the offering is said to be accepted.

Cows and dogs. Portions are also set aside for a cow and a dog, and often for ants.

All beings. The custom grows from the old idea of bhūta-yajña: feeding all creatures is part of every household’s duty.

Patience. Families sometimes wait a long time for a crow to come. The waiting itself becomes part of the remembering.

ZAPPY: The ancestors are crows?

PROF. BOLT: A popular belief, beautifully symbolic. Scriptures speak of crows as messengers; many families simply see them as guests.

Crows, cows and dogs: feeding every being.

PREPARING FOR ŚRĀDDHA

The day before and the morning

Purity. The house is cleaned, and the cook and performer observe restraint the day before.

The priest. A family priest is engaged to guide the rite according to the family’s Veda and tradition.

The food. Special dishes are cooked fresh; certain items (black gram, sesame, honey) are traditional, others avoided.

The time. Śrāddha is usually performed around midday (aparāhṇa), the time said to be the ancestors’.

ZAPPY: Why midday?

PROF. BOLT: The texts set the afternoon for the ancestors, as the morning is for the gods.

Clean, prepare, at midday.

DARBHA GRASS

Why kuśa

Sacred grass. Darbha (kuśa) has been used in Vedic ritual for three thousand years.

Purity. It is held to purify and to protect the rite; a ring of it, the pavitra, is worn.

A seat. Darbha is spread as a seat for the ancestors and the piṇḍas.

A story. The Purāṇas say darbha was touched by the nectar of immortality when Garuḍa set it down.

ZAPPY: Nectar touched the grass?

PROF. BOLT: So the story says, when Garuḍa brought amṛta to free his mother. Ever since, darbha is pure.

Darbha: the grass touched by nectar.

BLACK SESAME

The ancestors’ seed

For the pitṛs. Black sesame (tila) is used in nearly every ancestral rite.

A story. One tradition says sesame arose from Viṣṇu’s sweat; another that it is dear to Yama.

Purifying. It is said to drive away evil spirits from the rite.

Not in god-rites. Black sesame is generally not used in the worship of gods, marking the difference.

ZAPPY: Why black sesame and not white?

PROF. BOLT: Black for the ancestors, by tradition. White sesame is used in festive foods.

Black sesame: the seed of the ancestors.

WORDS OF THE RITE

What is said

Names and gotra. The rite names each ancestor with their gotra (lineage) and relationship.

Svadhā. Offerings to the ancestors end with svadhā, as offerings to gods end with svāhā.

Vedic verses. Verses from the Veda addressed to the pitṛs are recited.

Your own words. Many also add a simple personal prayer for the departed.

ZAPPY: Svadhā instead of svāhā?

PROF. BOLT: The ancestors’ own word. You hear it and know: this offering is for the fathers.

Svadhā: the ancestors’ word.
