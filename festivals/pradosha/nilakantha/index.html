Nīlakaṇṭha

The Poison, the Blue Throat and the Dance on Nandi · explained and illustrated

The one who held the poison

HOW THIS WORKS: The story in order: the curse, the churning, the poison, Śiva drinks, the dance on Nandi, what came after, and what it means.

WHERE WE ARE

This is the story at the heart of Pradoṣa: the churning of the ocean, the poison that rose first, and Śiva who drank it. It is told in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (book 8) and many other Purāṇas, and loved in South Indian temple tradition, which adds the dance on Nandi.

We tell it in order, scene by scene, with what each part means.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why did the gods churn an ocean?

PROF. BOLT: For amṛta, the nectar of immortality. They had lost their strength and needed it back.

THE CURSE OF DURVĀSAS

How the gods lost their strength

The story

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa tells how it began.

The sage Durvāsas, famous for his temper, gave Indra a divine garland.
Indra carelessly placed it on his elephant Airāvata, who threw it down and trampled it.
Durvāsas cursed Indra and the gods to lose their glory and strength.
The demons attacked and defeated the weakened gods.
The gods went to Viṣṇu, who advised them: make peace with the demons, churn the ocean together, and win amṛta.

ZAPPY: All that over a garland?

PROF. BOLT: Over pride. Indra treated a holy gift carelessly. The whole churning starts from one act of arrogance.

A garland trampled, the gods’ strength lost.

THE CHURNING

Mandara, Vāsuki and the tortoise

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.6–7.

Gods and demons uprooted Mount Mandara to use as a churning rod, and wound the serpent king Vāsuki round it as the rope.
The demons took the head end, the gods the tail, and they pulled back and forth.
The mountain, with no base, began to sink into the ocean.
Viṣṇu took the form of Kūrma, a giant tortoise, and held the mountain on his back. See our Daśāvatāra series · 10 avatāras + overview.
The churning went on, and the ocean began to give up its hidden things.

ZAPPY: The demons took the head end on purpose?

PROF. BOLT: They insisted, thinking it the place of honour. They got the serpent’s fiery breath for their pride.

Mountain, serpent, tortoise: the ocean is churned.

THE POISON RISES

Hālāhala

The story

The first thing out of the ocean was not nectar.

Before any treasure, the ocean released hālāhala (also kālakūṭa), a poison so terrible that its fumes alone burned everything they touched.
Gods and demons alike dropped the serpent and ran.
The poison spread across the three worlds; creatures fainted, the sky darkened.
No god could stop it.
The gods ran to Kailāsa, to Śiva. South Indian tradition says it was the Trayodaśī evening: the first Pradoṣa.

ZAPPY: Why poison first?

PROF. BOLT: Many teachers read it as the inner churning: when you start to dig into yourself, the poison comes up before the nectar.

Before the nectar, the poison.

ŚIVA DRINKS

Nīlakaṇṭha

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.7.

The gods praised Śiva as the lord of all and begged him to save the worlds.
Śiva, full of compassion, told Pārvatī that a great one protects living beings even at a cost to himself.
He gathered the poison into his palm and drank it.
Pārvatī pressed his throat so it would not go down; in many tellings Śiva himself held it there. It stayed in his throat and turned it blue.
He became Nīlakaṇṭha, “the blue-throated”. A few drops spilled, the Bhāgavata says, and were taken by snakes, scorpions and poisonous plants.

ZAPPY: Did it hurt him?

PROF. BOLT: The texts say it only coloured his throat. Its mark is his badge: the one who takes the world’s poison.

The poison held in his throat: Nīlakaṇṭha.

THE DANCE ON NANDI

South Indian temple tradition

The story

Tamil Śaiva tradition completes the story.

In one telling Nandi had carried the poison to Śiva and was dazed by its fumes.
To show the worlds he was unharmed, and to restore Nandi, Śiva danced, standing between Nandi’s horns.
The gods gathered on Kailāsa and worshipped him at that twilight hour, the hour of Pradoṣa.
Ever since, at Pradoṣa, Nandi is worshipped first, and devotees look at Śiva between his horns.
Tellings differ; this is the version behind the temple practice of Part 7.

ZAPPY: Is this in the Bhāgavata too?

PROF. BOLT: No, it’s a living temple tradition. The Purāṇa gives the poison; the temples give the dance.

Śiva dances between Nandi’s horns.

WHAT CAME AFTER

The fourteen treasures and the nectar

Treasures. After the poison came the ratnas: Kāmadhenu the wish-cow, the horse Uccaiḥśravas, the elephant Airāvata, the Kaustubha gem, the wish-tree Pārijāta, the apsarās, Vāruṇī, and the moon, which Śiva wears. Lists vary.

Lakṣmī. The goddess Lakṣmī rose on a lotus and chose Viṣṇu.

Dhanvantari. Last came Dhanvantari, physician of the gods, carrying the pot of amṛta.

Mohinī. The demons seized it; Viṣṇu became Mohinī, the enchantress, and gave it to the gods. See our Mohinī Ekādaśī.

ZAPPY: The moon came out of the ocean too?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, yes, and Śiva wears it. That is why Monday, the moon’s day, is so dear to him.

Poison first, then the treasures, then nectar.

WHAT IT MEANS

Readings of the story

Compassion that costs. Śiva takes on what no one else can bear. The Bhāgavata has him say that the great serve beings even at their own risk.

Holding, not swallowing. The poison neither goes down nor comes out: he contains it. Many teachers read this as mastery of anger and pain, held without harming oneself or others.

Poison before nectar. Inner work brings up the worst first; persevere, and the treasures follow.

Pradoṣa. So at twilight, devotees bring their own poisons to Śiva, asking him to hold them.

ZAPPY: So I can bring my anger to Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: That is exactly what many devotees do at Pradoṣa. Nīlakaṇṭha holds what we can’t.

Nīlakaṇṭha: the one who holds the poison.

NĪLAKAṆṬHA IN ART AND TEMPLES

Where to see the blue throat

The blue throat. Images and paintings of Śiva often show the throat blue, even when the body is white with ash.

Named temples. Many temples honour him as Nīlakaṇṭha; the Nīlakaṇṭha Mahādeva temple near Ṛṣikeśa is said to mark the place where he drank the poison.

Viṣāpaharaṇa. Śiva is also worshipped as Viṣāpaharaṇa-mūrti, “remover of poison”, in some South Indian temples.

In hymns. “Nīlakaṇṭha” and “Śitikaṇṭha” recur in the Śrī Rudram · 3 parts and other hymns.

ZAPPY: Is there a Nīlakaṇṭha in Karnataka?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva has many temples under that name across India. Nañjanagūḍu’s Nañjuṇḍa, “the one who ate the poison”, is Karnataka’s own: see our article.

Nañjuṇḍa of Nañjanagūḍu: Karnataka’s poison-eater.
