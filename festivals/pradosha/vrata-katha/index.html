The Pradoṣa Vrata Kathā

Śāṇḍilya, the Widow and the Two Boys · explained and illustrated

A widow, two boys and a sage

HOW THIS WORKS: The Skanda Purāṇa’s story in six scenes: the foundling, Śāṇḍilya, the treasure, the gandharva princess, the kingdom, and what it teaches.

WHERE WE ARE

Every vow has its kathā, the story told at the end of the worship. Pradoṣa’s is told in the Skanda Purāṇa: a poor widow, her son, an orphaned prince she takes in, and the sage Śāṇḍilya who teaches them the vow.

The last two verses of the Pradoṣa hymn (Part 4) are Śāṇḍilya’s words to her. Here is the whole story, scene by scene. Names vary between editions; we follow the common telling.

ZAPPY: Who tells it at the pūjā?

PROF. BOLT: Often the priest or the eldest at home. It is read after the evening worship.

THE WIDOW AND THE FOUNDLING

The beginning

The story

The Skanda Purāṇa’s story opens in poverty.

A poor brahmin widow lived by begging, with her young son Śuciṣmat.
One day she found a baby boy, alone, by a pond: a prince, though she did not know it.
Out of pity she took him home and raised him with her own son as brothers.
She named him and fed both boys with what little she gathered.
The three lived on in poverty, the widow worrying for both their futures.

ZAPPY: She took in a stranger’s baby when she had nothing?

PROF. BOLT: That is where the story’s grace begins: her kindness before any vow.

A widow’s kindness: two sons where she had one.

ŚĀṆḌILYA REVEALS THE PAST

Why they suffer

The story

At a Śiva temple the family met the sage.

The widow took the boys to a temple, where the sage Śāṇḍilya sat.
He looked at them with his yogic sight and told her: the foundling is Dharmagupta, son of the king of Vidarbha, whose father was killed by enemies and whose mother died in grief.
He also told her about her own son: in his past life he had been a brahmin who lived by accepting gifts, never giving any. That is why he is poor now (the hymn’s verse 8).
“To remove that fault,” the sage said, “let him take refuge in Śaṅkara” (verse 9).
And he taught them the Pradoṣa vrata.

ZAPPY: So the hymn’s last verses are part of this story!

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The sage speaks them to the widow. That’s why the hymn ends so strangely.

Śāṇḍilya reveals the past and teaches the vow.

THE TREASURE

The poor boy’s fortune

The story

The boys kept the vow with devotion.

Both boys kept the Pradoṣa worship of Śiva faithfully, Trayodaśī after Trayodaśī.
One day, bathing or digging by a riverbank, Śuciṣmat found a buried pot of treasure.
He wanted to share it with his brother at once.
The family’s poverty ended.
Tellings differ on the details; all agree the poor boy’s fortune turned through the vow.

ZAPPY: The poor boy found gold?

PROF. BOLT: His fault was never giving. Notice: the first thing he wants is to share it.

The vow kept, a treasure found and shared.

THE GANDHARVA PRINCESS

The prince’s fortune

The story

Then came the prince’s turn.

As a young man, Dharmagupta wandered in a forest and came upon gandharva maidens playing by a lake.
Their princess, Aṃśumatī, saw him, and the two fell in love.
Her father, the gandharva king, came to him and said: Śiva had told him to give his daughter to this prince, a devotee of Pradoṣa.
The gandharva king also promised to help him regain his father’s kingdom.
So the orphan found a bride from the heavens.

ZAPPY: How did the gandharva king know?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva told him, the story says. The prince’s twilight worship had been seen.

An orphan prince, a heavenly bride.

THE KINGDOM REGAINED

The end of the story

The story

The story ends in Vidarbha.

With the gandharvas’ help, Dharmagupta marched on the usurpers who had killed his father.
He defeated them and took back the throne of Vidarbha.
He brought the widow who raised him to the palace as his mother, and made Śuciṣmat his chief companion.
He ruled long and justly, and kept the Pradoṣa vow all his life.
The Purāṇa ends: whoever keeps Pradoṣa, or hears this story, receives Śiva’s grace.

ZAPPY: He kept the widow as his mother?

PROF. BOLT: Of course. Her kindness started it all. The story honours her as much as the vow.

A throne regained, a foster mother honoured.

WHAT THE STORY TEACHES

Lessons of the kathā

Kindness first. The story begins with a poor woman taking in a stranger’s child. Grace follows generosity.

Giving, not only receiving. Śuciṣmat’s past life of only taking explains his poverty; his first impulse with the treasure is to share.

Persistence. The boys keep the vow fortnight after fortnight, long before any reward.

Grace for all. One poor, one royal: the vow lifts both.

ZAPPY: So the vow worked because they were good people?

PROF. BOLT: The vow and the goodness go together. The story would ring false if the widow had been cruel.

Kindness, giving, persistence: the heart of the kathā.

OTHER PRADOṢA STORIES

The moon, and more

The moon’s cure. A popular story says Candra, the moon, cursed with a wasting illness by Dakṣa, was relieved by Śiva on a Trayodaśī evening, which is why Pradoṣa is prayed for health. (The moon’s cure is also tied to Somanātha; see our Jyotirliṅga series · 13 articles.)

The gods and the demons. Another tradition says the gods, harassed by demons, ran round Kailāsa on a Trayodaśī evening, guided by Nandi, and Śiva saved them.

Weekday stories. Each weekday Pradoṣa has its own kathā in popular vrata books (Part 6).

One theme. In every story, twilight is when Śiva rescues.

ZAPPY: So many stories for one vow?

PROF. BOLT: A beloved vow collects stories. The Skanda Purāṇa one is the classic.

The moon’s cure, the gods’ rescue: twilight is rescue time.

HOW TO READ THE KATHĀ

At the end of the worship

When. After the evening worship and before breaking the fast, the family sits together to hear the kathā.

Who. A priest, an elder or anyone in the family reads it; children love it.

Then. Ārati, prasāda, and the fast is broken.

Our text. This page’s retelling can be read aloud; Part 4 gives the hymn.

ZAPPY: Can I read it to my kids?

PROF. BOLT: Perfect. It is a story about two boys, after all.

Read the kathā after the worship.
