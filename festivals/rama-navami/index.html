BORN AT NOON · CAITRA NAVAMĪ · PĀNAKA AND KOSAMBARI

Rāma Navamī

The Birth of Rāma at Noon · explained and illustrated

Four cradles, one noon

HOW THIS WORKS: Vālmīki’s birth verses, the story from Daśaratha’s pāyasa to the whole epic in brief, the Rāma-nāma verse, and how Karnataka keeps the day with pānaka, music and Hampi.

WHERE WE ARE

On the ninth day of the bright fortnight of Caitra, at noon, Kausalyā gave birth to Rāma. Vālmīki records the moment in astronomical detail, and India has kept the day ever since: Rāma Navamī, the last day of the spring nine nights that begin on Ugādi.

In Karnataka the day smells of pānaka and kosambari: jaggery water and soaked moong salad handed out from roadside stalls, with buttermilk for the heat. Bengaluru turns it into a month of classical music. Telugu country celebrates the wedding of Sītā and Rāma at Bhadrācalam.

This article gives Vālmīki’s birth verses, tells the story from Daśaratha’s sacrifice to the four brothers’ childhood and the whole epic in brief, gives the “Śrīrāma rāma rāmeti” verse, and explains every custom of the day.

THE NINTH OF CAITRA

Why this day

The date. Caitra Śukla Navamī: nine days after the lunar new year. In the amānta calendar of Karnataka Caitra begins at the new moon, so Rāma Navamī falls in late March or April.

The hour. The birth is placed at midday (madhyāhna), so the main worship happens around noon: the cradle is rocked, the image bathed, and the birth announced with conches and bells.

Nine nights. The nine days from Ugādi to Rāma Navamī are Vasanta (spring) Navarātri, in which many read the Rāmāyaṇa in nine parts (navāha pārāyaṇa) and finish with the birth on the ninth day; some begin readings on Rāma Navamī and continue after.

Two days in some years. When Navamī straddles two days, almanacs choose the day on which it prevails at midday.

ZAPPY: Nine nights again? I thought Navarātri was in autumn.

PROF. BOLT: There are two, Zappy. Autumn’s is for Devī; spring’s ends with Rāma.

Nine days, then Rāma at noon.

DAŚARATHA’S SACRIFICE

The pāyasa from the fire

The story

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa, chapters 8–17.

Daśaratha, king of Ayodhyā, ruled well for thousands of years but had no son, and grieved.
On his ministers’ advice he performed the horse sacrifice and then the putrakāmeṣṭi, the rite for a son, with the sage Ṛṣyaśṛṅga as priest.
Meanwhile the gods had asked Viṣṇu to be born as a man to end Rāvaṇa, whom no god or demon could kill. Viṣṇu chose Daśaratha as his father.
From the sacrificial fire rose a radiant being carrying a golden vessel of divine pāyasa, and gave it to the king.
Daśaratha gave half to Kausalyā, a portion to Kaikeyī, and the rest to Sumitrā in two shares. All three queens conceived.

ZAPPY: Four sons from one pot of pāyasa?

PROF. BOLT: From one divine gift, Zappy: Viṣṇu shared among four. That’s why the brothers are inseparable.

A king’s prayer, the fire’s answer.

The month, the day and the stars
tato yajñe samāpte tu ṛtūnāṃ ṣaṭ samatyayuḥtataś ca dvādaśe māse caitre nāvamike tithaunakṣatre ’ditidaivatye svoccasaṃstheṣu pañcasugraheṣu karkaṭe lagne vākpatāv indunā saha
When the sacrifice was over, six seasons passed. Then in the twelfth month, Caitra, on the ninth lunar day, under the star whose deity is Aditi (Punarvasu), with five planets exalted, the ascendant in Cancer, and Jupiter (Vākpati) rising with the moon,

A horoscope in verse. Vālmīki gives Rāma’s birth like an astronomer: month, lunar day, nakṣatra (Punarvasu, ruled by Aditi), five exalted planets, Cancer rising, Jupiter with the moon. These two verses are why the festival is the ninth of Caitra, and why astrologers have argued over the date for centuries. The sentence continues on the next page.

The child and the world. Vālmīki calls the newborn jagannātha, lord of the world, in the same breath as he names his mother. The following verses tell of Bharata born to Kaikeyī under Puṣya, and the twins Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna to Sumitrā under Āśleṣā, and of Ayodhyā erupting in festival. This verse is often recited at noon on Rāma Navamī as the cradle is rocked.

FOUR BROTHERS

Childhood, and Viśvāmitra’s arrival

The story

Bāla Kāṇḍa, chapters 18–22.

The four princes grew up together, learned the Vedas, archery, riding and statecraft. Lakṣmaṇa was always at Rāma’s side, and Śatrughna at Bharata’s.
When Rāma was still a youth, the sage Viśvāmitra came to court and asked for him: demons were wrecking his sacrifices, and only Rāma could stop them.
Daśaratha, terrified for his boy, refused; Vasiṣṭha persuaded him to trust the sage. Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa went.
On the way Viśvāmitra taught them the mantras Balā and Atibalā, that freed them from hunger and fatigue, and later the divine weapons.
Their adventures began: the demoness Tāṭakā, the protection of the sacrifice, Ahalyā freed, and at last Mithilā, where a great bow of Śiva waited, and Sītā.

ZAPPY: So Rāma left home very young?

PROF. BOLT: A teenager with a bow and a teacher, Zappy. The whole epic starts with a father learning to let go.

The brothers set out.

THE WHOLE RĀMĀYAṆA IN FIVE

From the bow to the throne

The story

The story the festival celebrates, in brief.

At Mithilā Rāma lifted and broke Śiva’s great bow and won Sītā; his three brothers married her sister and cousins.
On the eve of his coronation, Queen Kaikeyī claimed two old boons: Bharata as king, Rāma exiled for fourteen years. Rāma went gladly, with Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa; Daśaratha died of grief, and Bharata ruled only as regent, Rāma’s sandals on the throne.
In the forest Rāvaṇa carried Sītā off to Laṅkā. Rāma found allies in Kiṣkindhā (in today’s Karnataka, by Hampi), above all Hanumān, who leapt the sea and found her.
With a monkey army and a bridge across the ocean, Rāma fought and killed Rāvaṇa, and won Sītā back.
He returned to Ayodhyā and was crowned. Rāma-rājya, his reign, became India’s byword for just rule.

ZAPPY: Kiṣkindhā is in Karnataka?

PROF. BOLT: By tradition, around Hampi and Ānegondi, Zappy. Read our Hanumān Jayantī article for Añjanādri.

Duty, exile, and return.

SĪTĀ-RĀMA KALYĀṆA

The wedding celebrated on the birthday

Birth and wedding together. In Telugu country and many South Indian temples, Rāma Navamī is also celebrated as the kalyāṇa, the wedding, of Sītā and Rāma: the images are married with the full ritual, mangala-sūtra and all.

Bhadrācalam. The most famous is at Bhadrācalam on the Godāvarī in Telaṅgāṇa, the temple associated with the poet-devotee Bhakta Rāmadāsu (Kancharla Gopanna). The state traditionally sends silk clothes and pearls (talambrālu) for the ceremony.

In Karnataka. Many Rāma and Hanumān temples hold a kalyāṇotsava; in homes the noon pūjā is followed by a little cradle ceremony (toṭṭil) for baby Rāma.

Why together. The tradition sees Rāma’s life as one offering: to remember his birth is to remember his marriage, his exile and his rule. Some places hold the wedding on Rāma Navamī, others in the days after.

ZAPPY: A wedding on his birthday?

PROF. BOLT: Rāma without Sītā is half the story, Zappy. So the temples celebrate both on one day.

Born and married in one celebration.

IN THE VERSE

Śrīrāma rāma rāmeti

The name equal to a thousand
śrīrāma rāma rāmeti rame rāme manoramesahasranāma tattulyaṃ rāmanāma varānane
“Śrī Rāma, Rāma, Rāma”: thus I delight in Rāma, the delightful one. O lovely-faced one, the name of Rāma is equal to the thousand names.
WordMeaning

rameI delight

Śiva’s answer. At the close of the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma, Pārvatī asks for a short way to say the thousand names, and Śiva gives this verse: saying “Rāma” three times equals the whole. Many recite it 108 times on Rāma Navamī, and it closes countless pūjās in Karnataka homes.

RĀMA NAVAMĪ IN KARNATAKA

Pānaka, kosambari and majjige

🥤 Pānaka

Jaggery water with dry ginger, cardamom and pepper, sometimes lemon: offered to Rāma and drunk as prasāda. In the summer heat it is the taste of the day.

🥗 Kosambari

Soaked moong or bengal gram with cucumber, carrot, coconut and lemon, seasoned with mustard: cool, light and offered with the pānaka.

🥛 Majjige

Spiced buttermilk with ginger, curry leaves and coriander, ladled out to everyone who passes.

🏮 Street stalls

Neighbourhoods set up stalls (aravaṭṭige, shelters for giving water) under awnings and hand out pānaka, kosambari and majjige free to all comers, a tradition of serving travellers in the heat.

ZAPPY: Why such cool food?

PROF. BOLT: It’s April in Karnataka, Zappy. Rāma’s birthday is the hottest festival of the year, so we serve what the heat needs.

Cool food for a hot birthday.

BENGALURU’S RĀMA NAVAMĪ MUSIC

A month of concerts for Rāma

The festival. Bengaluru keeps Rāma Navamī with one of India’s longest classical music festivals, organised for decades by the Sree Ramaseva Mandali in Chāmarājapēṭe: about a month of Karnāṭaka and Hindustānī concerts by the great names of each generation, under a pandal, in Rāma’s honour.

Tyāgarāja. The music is soaked in Rāma: the saint-composer Tyāgarāja (eighteenth–nineteenth century) devoted almost all his songs to Rāma, and his kṛtis are the backbone of these concerts. Purandara Dāsa and the Haridāsas of Karnataka also sang Rāma in Kannada.

Neighbourhood bhajans. Beyond the big stage, every locality has its own Rāma Navamī: bhajana maṇḍaḷis, harikathā, street pandals with a Rāma picture, pānaka and music into the night.

Rāmāyaṇa readings. Temples and homes read the Rāmāyaṇa (Vālmīki in Sanskrit, or Kannada retellings such as Kumāra Vālmīki’s Torave Rāmāyaṇa) in the nine days, ending with the birth.

ZAPPY: A month of concerts?

PROF. BOLT: Ask any Bengaluru music lover, Zappy. Rāma Navamī is when the city’s concert year begins.

Rāma in music.

RĀMA IN KARNATAKA’S LANDSCAPE

Hampi, Kiṣkindhā and the hills

🏛️ Hampi

The Hazāra Rāma temple, the Vijayanagara kings’ royal chapel, carries the Rāmāyaṇa carved in panels round its walls; the Kōdaṇḍarāma temple stands by the Tuṅgabhadrā.

🐒 Kiṣkindhā

Tradition places Sugrīva’s kingdom around Ānegondi across the river, with Añjanādri hill as Hanumān’s birthplace and Ṛṣyamūka and Pampā sarovara nearby.

⛰️ Rāmadēvara Beṭṭa

A hill near Rāmanagara with a Rāma temple and vulture sanctuary, where Jaṭāyu’s story is remembered by local tradition.

🛕 Everywhere

Almost every Karnataka village has a Rāma or Āñjaneya temple; Rāma Navamī is its festival, with a car procession in many towns.

ZAPPY: Karnataka has so much Rāmāyaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Half the Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa happens here, Zappy, by tradition. Hampi is the Rāmāyaṇa in stone.

The epic on our map.

WHAT RĀMA NAVAMĪ MEANS

Dharma with a human face

God as a person. Rāma is Viṣṇu born as a man who suffers as men do: exile, the loss of a father, the loss of a wife, war. Vālmīki calls him vigrahavān dharmaḥ, dharma in a body. Rāma Navamī celebrates the arrival of goodness in human form. (Our reading.)

Keeping one’s word. Rāma goes into exile to keep his father’s word to Kaikeyī; the epic’s whole plot turns on promises kept.

The name. The tradition holds that the name “Rāma” itself carries the power of the person; hence the practice of writing and chanting it, the “Śrī Rāma” at the top of letters, the Rāma-nāma on the lips of the dying.

Service. Karnataka’s free pānaka stalls keep the festival’s spirit: Rāma is honoured by caring for the thirsty stranger.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Rāma Navamī?

PROF. BOLT: Read one chapter of the Rāmāyaṇa, Zappy, and give a stranger a cup of pānaka. Rāma would like both.

Goodness born among us.
