RĀMA NAVAMĪ

Noon, a cradle and pānaka

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the ninth day of Caitra, at noon, India rocks a cradle for Rāma. In Karnataka, every street corner hands out pānaka, kōsambari and buttermilk; in Bengaluru a month of music begins; at Bhadrācalam, Sītā and Rāma are married again.

This guide covers the date, the birth story from Vālmīki, home worship, Karnataka’s customs, and the great Rāma Navamīs of Bhadrācalam and Ayodhyā.

ZAPPY: Why noon?

PROF. BOLT: Vālmīki says Rāma was born at midday, Zappy. So the cradle rocks at noon.

Rāma Navamī is the birthday of Rāma, kept on the ninth day of the bright half of Caitra; in 2027 on Thursday 15 April. Because Vālmīki says he was born at midday, worship peaks at noon, when an image of baby Rāma is rocked in a cradle.

It closes the nine nights of the spring Navarātri that began at Ugādi. In Karnataka the day means pānaka, kōsambari and buttermilk handed out on every street; in Bengaluru it opens a month of classical music.

ZAPPY: Rāma came from pāyasa?

PROF. BOLT: The kheer from the sacrifice, shared among the queens, gave four sons. That’s Vālmīki’s telling.

In Vālmīki’s Bāla Kāṇḍa, King Daśaratha of Ayodhyā, without an heir, performs the putrakāmeṣṭi sacrifice. A divine being rises from the fire with a bowl of pāyasa, which the king shares among his queens. Kausalyā bears Rāma, Kaikeyī Bharata, and Sumitrā the twins Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna.

Rāma is born in Caitra, on the ninth tithi, under the Punarvasu star, at midday.

ZAPPY: Do I need to read the whole Rāmāyaṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Some families read it all over the nine nights. A chapter is fine too, Zappy.

Many devotees fast until noon or take only fruit, milk and pānaka. At midday a small image of Rāma is placed in a decorated cradle and rocked, with songs; Rāma, Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa and Hanumān are worshipped together.

Families read the Bāla Kāṇḍa or, over the nine nights, the whole Rāmāyaṇa, and chant Rāma’s name.

ZAPPY: Why jaggery water?

PROF. BOLT: April is hot, Zappy. Pānaka cools you and gives you energy. Rāma’s gift to the thirsty.

In Karnataka, local maṇḍaḷis set up stalls on streets and even footpaths, handing out free pānaka (jaggery water with lemon, dry ginger, pepper and cardamom), kōsambari (soaked moong dal with cucumber, coconut and lemon) and majjige (spiced buttermilk).

Rāma Navamī falls in the hottest weeks of the year, and these are cooling foods. Some households take no rice, eating phalāhāra instead.

ZAPPY: A month of concerts for one birthday?

PROF. BOLT: Bengaluru’s answer to Chennai’s December season. Rāma loved music, they say.

Rāma Navamī in Bengaluru is a music season. Public Rāmotsava concerts are traced to Mysore Sadāśiva Rao in the 19th century. The Sree Ramaseva Mandali of Chamarajpet, founded in 1939 by S. V. Narayanaswamy Rao at the age of fourteen, holds a month-long festival in the Old Fort High School grounds; its 88th festival ran from 27 March to 22 April 2026.

M. S. Subbulakshmi sang there 32 times. Other sabhās, in Seshadripuram and Malleswaram, keep their own Rāmotsavas.

ZAPPY: A birthday and a wedding on one day?

PROF. BOLT: In the Telugu tradition, yes: Rāma’s birth and his wedding are celebrated together.

At Bhadrācalam on the Godāvarī, in Telangana, Rāma Navamī is the day of the Sītā–Rāma kalyāṇam, the divine wedding, attended by huge crowds; the state presents silk clothes and pearls. The temple is linked with the 17th-century devotee Kañcarla Gōpanna, Bhadrācala Rāmadāsu, whose songs are sung everywhere.

Many Telugu towns hold their own kalyāṇams the same day.

ZAPPY: The sun puts a tilaka on Rāma?

PROF. BOLT: Mirrors and lenses guide a beam at noon. Engineering in the service of a very old idea.

In Ayodhyā, pilgrims bathe in the Sarayū and crowd the temples. At the new Rāma temple, a system of mirrors and lenses directs sunlight onto the image’s forehead at noon, a “sūrya tilaka”, first done on Rāma Navamī 2024.

Sītāmarhī in Bihar, Sītā’s birthplace by tradition, holds great celebrations, and processions and Rāmlīlā dramas take place in many northern towns.

ZAPPY: Did Rāma come to Karnataka?

PROF. BOLT: Kiṣkindhā, near Hampi, is where he met Hanumān, says tradition. See our Hanumān guides.

Rāmeśvaram, where Rāma worshipped Śiva, draws pilgrims. Tamil homes give nīr mōr and pānakam like Karnataka’s. Haridāsa songs to Rāma, by Purandaradāsa and others, are sung.

Tradition places Kiṣkindhā, where Rāma met Hanumān, near Hampi; our Hanumān stories tell it.

ZAPPY: Two Navarātris a year?

PROF. BOLT: Spring and autumn, Zappy. Mysuru is famous for the autumn one.

The nine nights from Ugādi to Rāma Navamī are Vasanta (spring) or Caitra Navarātri. In the north they honour Durgā’s nine forms; in the south many read the Rāmāyaṇa and hold Vasantotsava.

The autumn Navarātri of Āśvayuja, ending in Vijayadaśamī, is the one Mysuru is famous for; see our Śrīvidyā festival guide.

THE MEANING

ServiceFree drink and food for strangers

ZAPPY: Why is Rāma called the ideal man?

PROF. BOLT: He keeps his word when it costs him a kingdom. That’s rare, Zappy.

Rāma is called Maryādā Puruṣottama, the best of men who keeps to right bounds: the son who gives up a kingdom to keep his father’s word, the king who puts his people first.

The festival’s customs carry that spirit into the street: free drink for strangers in the heat, and music as worship.
