Upākarma

Renewing the Thread and the Veda · explained and illustrated

A thread renewed, the Veda begun again

HOW THIS WORKS: The sacred thread, the Upākarma rite step by step, and Hayagrīva, Lord of learning.

WHERE WE ARE

On the full moon of Śrāvaṇa, two very different observances fall on the same day. One is Upākarma: men who wear the sacred thread change it for a new one and ceremonially begin the year’s study of the Veda. The other is Rakṣā Bandhan (Part 2).

In Tamil Nadu Upākarma is Āvaṇi Aviṭṭam; the same day is also Hayagrīva Jayantī, birthday of Viṣṇu’s horse-headed form, Lord of learning.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why change a thread every year?

PROF. BOLT: To renew a vow. The thread stands for a commitment to study and discipline; once a year it is made new.

THE SACRED THREAD

Yajñopavīta

The thread. The yajñopavīta (Kannada janivāra, Tamil pūṇūl) is a cord of three strands worn from the left shoulder across the body, given at the upanayana, the ceremony that begins Vedic study.

Its meaning. The three strands are read in many ways: the three debts (to gods, sages and ancestors), the three guṇas, or the three goddesses of speech. The knot is the brahma-granthi.

Who wears it. Traditionally worn by boys and men of communities that perform upanayana; practice today varies.

Renewing. Worn threads are changed whenever needed, and always at Upākarma.

ZAPPY: Three strands, many meanings?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition loves layered meanings. The one everyone agrees on: a promise to learn and to live with discipline.

Three strands, a promise renewed.

THE UPĀKARMA RITE

How the day runs

The story

The rite varies by Veda and school; this is the common shape.

Bath and atonement. A holy bath, and a prāyaścitta, an atonement for lapses of the year, especially in study and daily rites.
New thread. The new yajñopavīta is worn with mantras, and the old one set aside.
Ṛṣi-tarpaṇa. Water is offered to the ṛṣis, the seers through whom the Veda came, in thanks.
Starting again. The Veda is ceremonially begun afresh, often with the opening portions of one’s own branch.
Gāyatrī japa. The next day many recite the Gāyatrī mantra 1008 times; see our Gāyatrī article · 4 parts in 1 article.

ZAPPY: Who are the ṛṣis thanked?

PROF. BOLT: The seers in whom the Vedic hymns arose: Vasiṣṭha, Viśvāmitra, Atri and the rest. Without them, no Veda.

Atone, renew, thank the seers, begin again.

HAYAGRĪVA JAYANTĪ

The Lord of learning

The horse-headed Viṣṇu. Hayagrīva is Viṣṇu with a horse’s head, white and shining, honoured as the Lord of knowledge.

His story. The Purāṇas tell that demons stole the Vedas from Brahmā; Viṣṇu took the horse-headed form, recovered them and restored them.

Upākarma’s patron. So he is worshipped on the day the Veda is begun afresh. In the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition he is especially loved, and Vedānta Deśika’s Hayagrīva Stotra is widely recited.

For students. Students pray to him before study and exams, as to Sarasvatī.

ZAPPY: A horse-headed god for students?

PROF. BOLT: He recovered the Veda itself. If anyone can help with your exams…

Hayagrīva: Viṣṇu who restored the Veda.

UPANAYANA

Where the thread begins

The second birth. Upanayana, “leading near” (to the teacher), is the rite that begins Vedic study; the boy becomes dvija, “twice-born”.

Gāyatrī. At upanayana the boy is taught the Gāyatrī mantra; see our Gāyatrī article · 4 parts in 1 article.

Age. Traditionally at 8, 11 or 12, depending on community.

Upākarma renews it. Every year Upākarma renews what upanayana began.

ZAPPY: So Upākarma is like a yearly birthday of the thread?

PROF. BOLT: A yearly renewal. Upanayana is the start; Upākarma keeps it new.

Upanayana begins, Upākarma renews.

THE ATONEMENT

Kāmo’kārṣīt japa

An honest admission. Part of the Upākarma rite in many traditions is a japa of a short formula admitting that desire and anger, not the self, caused one’s lapses (the kāmo’kārṣīt manyur akārṣīt japa).

Atonement. It is part of the prāyaścitta for the year’s faults.

Then the bath. The participants bathe and are purified.

Ready to begin. Only then is the new thread worn.

ZAPPY: “Desire did it, not me”?

PROF. BOLT: A humble way of saying: I slipped, and I want to begin again clean. Every new year needs that.

Admit, atone, begin clean.

DIFFERENT DAYS FOR DIFFERENT VEDAS

Not all on one day

Ṛg and Yajur. Followers of the Ṛg and Yajur Vedas commonly keep Upākarma on Śrāvaṇa Pūrṇimā (Yajur) or the Śravaṇa star day (Ṛg).

Sāma. Sāma Vedins usually keep it a month later, on the Hasta star in Bhādrapada.

Local calendars. Almanacs give the right day for each school.

Ask. Families follow their own Veda and sūtra.

ZAPPY: My Sāma Vedi friend changed his thread on a different day!

PROF. BOLT: Exactly right for him. Each Veda has its own Upākarma day.

Each Veda its own day.

GĀYATRĪ JAPA DAY

The day after

1008 times. On the day after Upākarma many recite the Gāyatrī mantra 1008 times, to atone for omissions and renew its power.

At dawn. Usually in the morning, after sandhyā.

Sandhyā. It reminds the wearer of the daily sandhyā-vandana, the twilight prayers.

Our article. See Gāyatrī · 4 parts in 1 article.

ZAPPY: 1008 is a lot.

PROF. BOLT: About two hours. Many do it together in temples or maṭhas.

1008 Gāyatrīs: renewing the mantra.

JANIVĀRA HUṆṆIME IN KARNATAKA

The Kannada name

The name. In Kannada Upākarma is often called Janivāra Huṇṇime, “thread full moon”, or Nūlu Huṇṇime.

Communities. Smārta, Mādhva and Śrīvaiṣṇava communities each keep it with their own priests and maṭhas.

Together. Many perform it together at temples or maṭhas, by a river or tank.

And Rakṣā. The same day, sisters tie rākhīs (Part 2).

ZAPPY: Thread full moon: that’s simple.

PROF. BOLT: Kannada names are often the clearest.

Janivāra Huṇṇime: the thread full moon.
