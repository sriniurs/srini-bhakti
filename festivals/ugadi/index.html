UGĀDI

Bitter and sweet: a new year begins

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the first day of Caitra, Karnataka and the Telugu lands begin their year: an oil bath before dawn, mango leaves over the door, bēvu-bella or the six-taste pacchaḍi, hōḷige for lunch, and the new year’s almanac read out in the evening.

This guide explains the date and the year-name, the stories, the customs, and Maharashtra’s Guḍi Pāḍavā.

New yearPlavaṅga saṃvatsara (41st); Śaka 1949

ZAPPY: Why not 1 January?

PROF. BOLT: For the moon and the season, Zappy. The year begins with spring and a new moon.

Ugādi, or Yugādi, “the beginning of an age”, is the new year of Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. It falls on the first day of the bright half of Caitra, in late March or April; in 2027 on Wednesday 7 April, when Plavaṅga saṃvatsara and Śaka 1949 begin.

Families rise early for an oil bath, hang mango leaves over the door, worship, and eat bēvu-bella, neem with jaggery, in Karnataka, or the six-taste Ugādi pacchaḍi in the Telugu states. Maharashtra keeps the same day as Guḍi Pāḍavā.

ZAPPY: Why was 2026 in March and 2027 in April?

PROF. BOLT: The adhika Jyeṣṭha of 2026 added a month. See our pañcāṅga guide.

Ugādi is the pratipadā following the new moon that ends Phālguna, so it is the first day of Caitra in the amānta calendar used in Karnataka. It begins a new named year in the sixty-year cycle.

Parābhava began on 19 March 2026; because 2026 had an extra month, Ugādi 2027 falls later, on 7 April, beginning Plavaṅga. In the north, the same day begins Nava Saṃvatsara (Vikrama 2084) and the nine nights of Caitra Navarātri.

ZAPPY: Did Brahmā really start on a Wednesday?

PROF. BOLT: The almanacs say creation began on Caitra śukla pratipadā. The weekday changes; the tithi stays.

Tradition holds that Brahmā began the creation of the world on Caitra śukla pratipadā, which makes it a fitting day to begin the year; Yugādi means “beginning of an age”. In western India the Śaka era is linked with the victory of King Śālivāhana, and the guḍi with Rāma’s return to Ayodhyā.

Underneath the stories is the season: vasanta, spring, when neem and mango flower and the year turns warm.

ZAPPY: Why mango leaves?

PROF. BOLT: Fresh, green and auspicious, Zappy. Spring is mango season.

The day starts before sunrise with abhyaṅga, an oil bath: oil is rubbed into the body and washed off. New clothes follow. Mango-leaf toraṇas are hung over doors, rangoli is drawn, and families worship at home and visit temples.

ZAPPY: Do I have to eat the bitter bit?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the point, Zappy. A year is never only sweet.

In Karnataka the day’s first taste is bēvu-bella: tender neem leaves or flowers with jaggery, sometimes with tamarind, raw mango or pepper added. The bitter and the sweet eaten together stand for accepting the year’s sorrows and joys alike.

Elders also call neem a spring tonic.

UGĀDI PACCHAḌI

Six tastes for the Telugu year
SweetJaggery: happiness
BitterNeem flowers: sorrow
SourTamarind: things we dislike
SaltySalt: interest in life
HotPepper or chilli: anger
AstringentRaw mango: surprise

ZAPPY: Six feelings in one spoon?

PROF. BOLT: The whole year in a mouthful. Some families’ meanings differ, but the idea is the same.

In the Telugu states, Ugādi pacchaḍi mixes six tastes: jaggery (sweet), neem flowers (bitter), tamarind (sour), salt, pepper or chilli (hot), and raw mango (astringent). Each stands for an experience of the coming year: commonly happiness, sorrow, dislike, interest, anger and surprise.

Families explain the tastes differently, but the idea is the same: take the year whole.

ZAPPY: Hōḷige and obbaṭṭu are the same?

PROF. BOLT: Same sweet, two Kannada names. Puran poḷī in Marathi, bobbaṭlu in Telugu.

The festival meal in Karnataka centres on hōḷige or obbaṭṭu, a flatbread stuffed with sweet lentil and jaggery, with mango chitranna, kōsambari and payasa. The same sweet is bobbaṭlu in Telugu and puran poḷī in Marathi.

ZAPPY: The planets have a cabinet?

PROF. BOLT: Each year a planet is ‘king’, others ‘ministers’. For 2027–28 the king is Budha, Mercury. It’s an old way of talking about the year.

In the evening, temples and community halls hold pañcāṅga śravaṇa: a priest reads out the new year’s almanac, naming the year, its “king” and “ministers” among the planets (for 2027–28 the king is Budha, Mercury, by Drik Pañcāṅg), and forecasts for rain, crops and each zodiac sign.

It is a cherished tradition; its forecasts are astrology, not science.

ZAPPY: Can I see it on Ugādi itself?

PROF. BOLT: Sometimes the next evening. It’s very thin on day one.

After Ugādi, many look for the new crescent moon in the evening sky, the visible start of the lunar year. Nine days later comes Rāma Navamī.

ZAPPY: A pot on a pole?

PROF. BOLT: Upturned, shining copper or silver. Visible from far: a flag for the new year.

Maharashtra and the Konkani communities keep the same day as Guḍi Pāḍavā. A guḍi, a pole dressed with bright cloth, neem, flowers and a sugar garland, topped with an upturned copper or silver pot, is raised at the door or window. It is read as a victory banner, for Rāma or Śālivāhana, or as Brahmā’s flag.

Cities hold shobhā yātrās, processions; neem with jaggery is eaten here too.

ZAPPY: So India has many new years?

PROF. BOLT: At least two kinds: lunar, like Ugādi, and solar, in mid-April. And 1 January for the office.

In the north the day begins Nava Saṃvatsara and Caitra Navarātri; Sindhis keep Ceṭī Cāṇḍ; Kashmiri Pandits, Navreh. States with solar calendars celebrate their new year in mid-April instead: Viṣu in Kerala, Puthāṇḍu in Tamil Nadu, Bohāg Bihu in Assam, Poyla Boiśākh in Bengal.

ZAPPY: A good day for resolutions?

PROF. BOLT: The best, Zappy. And eat the neem: practise taking the bitter well.

Ugādi is about beginning well: a clean house, a clean body, new clothes, and a deliberate taste of bitter and sweet together. As the sixty-year wheel turns, elders bless the young, and many start new ventures and habits on this day.
