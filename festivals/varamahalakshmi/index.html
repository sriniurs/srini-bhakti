VARAMAHĀLAKṢMĪ

The goddess who grants boons

FESTIVALS · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the Friday before the full moon of Śrāvaṇa, women across Karnataka and the south invite Lakṣmī home. A pot becomes the goddess, with a face, a crown and a silk sari; a yellow thread is tied on every wrist; and gifts of turmeric and kumkum go to every visiting woman.

This guide covers the date, the vrata kathā, the kalaśa, the day’s worship, the sharing, and what “prosperity” really means.

ZAPPY: Why a Friday?

PROF. BOLT: Friday is Śukravāra, the goddess’s day, Zappy. The vow picks the Friday nearest the full moon.

Varamahālakṣmī Vrata is kept on the Friday before the full moon of Śrāvaṇa; in 2027 on Friday 13 August. Mainly married women worship Lakṣmī as Vara-Mahālakṣmī, the goddess who grants boons, for the health and prosperity of their families.

At its heart is a kalaśa dressed as the goddess, with a face, sari and jewellery. In Karnataka it is a major habba, and Bengaluru’s flower markets overflow the day before.

ZAPPY: Where does the story come from?

PROF. BOLT: Booklets give their sources; scholars note the story is passed down mostly in vrata books. We tell it as the families do.

The vrata kathā tells of Cārumatī, a devoted and honest wife, to whom the goddess (or Viṣṇu, in some tellings) appears in a dream, asking her to worship Lakṣmī on the Friday before Śrāvaṇa pūrṇimā. She and her neighbours keep the vow, and their families prosper. Another story tells of Padmāvatī, a pious woman of Kosala, visited by Lakṣmī.

The story is read aloud at the end of the worship. Booklets often name the Skanda Purāṇa as its source; we have not traced the passage.

ZAPPY: The goddess has a face mask?

PROF. BOLT: A silver or brass face, often a family heirloom, Zappy. The pot becomes her body.

A brass or silver pot is filled with rice, betel leaves, turmeric, coins and sometimes fruit, topped with mango leaves and a coconut smeared with turmeric. A face of the goddess is painted on the coconut or a metal face, often a family heirloom, is fixed on; a crown, sari, jewellery and flowers complete her.

The decorated kalaśa is first set at the threshold to welcome Lakṣmī, then carried into the pūjā room.

ZAPPY: What does the thread do?

PROF. BOLT: It carries the goddess’s blessing and protection home on your wrist, Zappy.

The house is cleaned and decorated the day before. On the morning of the vrata, after bathing, the women prepare the kalaśa and worship the goddess with a lamp, flowers, āratī, hymns and songs.

A yellow, turmeric-dipped thread tied round the pot’s neck is untied, and a piece is tied on each woman’s right wrist as a blessing and protection. The vrata kathā is read. As always, we describe the day; family custom or a priest guides the rite itself.

ZAPPY: Why give gifts to other women?

PROF. BOLT: Because Lakṣmī stays where she is shared, the elders say. A vow kept alone feels half-done.

Married women invite others home and give them arisina–kumkuma (turmeric and kumkum), bangles, betel, fruit and sometimes a blouse piece; in Karnataka gift trays called bāgina are given. The idea is that Lakṣmī stays where she is shared.

In Tamil Nadu, the next day the kalaśa is placed in a drum of rice, facing west, to keep the goddess in the house until the next year.

ZAPPY: Nine kinds of fruit?

PROF. BOLT: Abundance on a plate, Zappy. She’s the goddess of plenty.

Karnataka homes cook hōḷige, payasa, kōsambari, chitranna and vaḍe; Tamil homes make four kinds of kozhukkaṭṭai, vaḍai and payasam. Altars are piled with fruit, often nine or more kinds. Food is shared with visiting women and neighbours.

ZAPPY: Why are flowers so expensive that week?

PROF. BOLT: Every home in Bengaluru wants them on the same Friday, Zappy!

Varamahālakṣmī habba falls in the heart of the monsoon festival season, between Nāgara Pañcamī and Gaurī–Gaṇeśa. In the days before, flower and fruit prices soar and markets are jammed; homes dress their kalaśas in silk and gold.

For the goddess herself, see our Aṣṭa Lakṣmī series, the Mahālakṣmī Aṣṭakam and the Kanakadhārā Stotram.

ZAPPY: Is it just praying for money?

PROF. BOLT: Lakṣmī has eight forms, Zappy. Money is one; courage and knowledge are others.

Vara means boon. Women ask the goddess for health, children, long life and harmony as much as wealth; Lakṣmī’s eight forms include courage, victory and knowledge as well as riches.

The sharing of gifts with other women makes the point that prosperity is meant to circulate.
