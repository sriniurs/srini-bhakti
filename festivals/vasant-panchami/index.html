SRĪ PAÑCAMĪ · SARASVATĪ · THE FIRST LETTER

Vasant Pañcamī

Spring, Sarasvatī and the First Letter · explained and illustrated

Yellow fields, white goddess, first letters

HOW THIS WORKS: The day and its names, the stories of Sarasvatī’s worship and of Kāma and Vasanta, the verses a child says before the first letter, the customs from Śṛṅgeri to Punjab, and what the day means.

WHERE WE ARE

On the fifth day of the bright fortnight of Māgha, the mustard flowers out across the northern plains and the cold begins to lift. Vasant Pañcamī, “the fifth day of spring”, greets the season and honours Sarasvatī, goddess of learning, speech and music. In the South it is called Śrī Pañcamī.

This article covers the date and its names, the Purāṇic story of how Sarasvatī came to be worshipped on this day, the story of Kāma and his friend Vasanta, the verses said before study, the child’s first letter, the colour yellow, and the regional ways of keeping it. Her own guide is Sarasvatī; her Navarātri worship is in Sarasvatī Pūjā and Durgāṣṭamī.

THE DAY AND ITS NAMES

Spring, Śrī and Madana

Vasanta. Spring, the first of the six Indian seasons in poetry and the “king of seasons”. Vasant Pañcamī is when people start to prepare for it; tradition says spring is in full bloom forty days later, at Holī.

Pañcamī. The fifth lunar day, here the fifth of the bright half (śukla pakṣa) of Māgha. Since the calendar is lunar, the solar date moves between late January and mid-February.

Śrī Pañcamī. In Andhra, Telangana, Karnataka and Odisha the day is often called Śrī Pañcamī, Śrī here meaning the Devī in her form as Sarasvatī.

Madana Pañcamī. An older name honours Madana or Kāma, god of love, whose companion is Vasanta, the spring itself. Two different worlds, learning and love, meet on one day.

ZAPPY: Learning and love on the same day?

PROF. BOLT: Both are kinds of longing, Zappy: one for knowledge, one for another person. Spring wakes both.

The fifth day of the bright half of Māgha.

KṚṢṆA’S BOON TO SARASVATĪ

Why she is worshipped on this day

The story

Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, Prakṛti Khaṇḍa, and Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Book 9, chapter 4; tellings differ in detail.

In the Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa’s account of creation, Sarasvatī emerges from the mouth of the supreme Kṛṣṇa as the power of speech and learning.
She is drawn to Kṛṣṇa himself. He tells her gently that her place is elsewhere, with Nārāyaṇa in Vaikuṇṭha, in the Purāṇa’s own telling.
To honour her, Kṛṣṇa grants a boon: on Māgha Śukla Pañcamī, and whenever learning begins, she will be worshipped across every world.
Kṛṣṇa is said to have worshipped her first; then gods, sages, Manus and human beings followed. The Devī Bhāgavata repeats the instruction for her worship and her kavaca (protective hymn).
So the day is both her festival and the day for vidyārambha, the beginning of study.

ZAPPY: Kṛṣṇa started the tradition?

PROF. BOLT: In these Purāṇas, yes, Zappy. Other tellings simply say she appeared on this day. Traditions differ; the worship is the same.

The day of her boon.

KĀMA, VASANTA AND THE THIRD EYE

The god of love meets the god of yoga

The story

Śiva Purāṇa (Rudra Saṃhitā) and Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava, canto 3; details differ.

The demon Tāraka could be killed only by a son of Śiva, but Śiva sat lost in meditation while Pārvatī served him.
The gods sent Kāma, god of love, with his bow of sugarcane and arrows of flowers. With him went his wife Rati and his friend Vasanta, the spring, who made the forest burst into bloom out of season.
Kāma aimed at Śiva just as Pārvatī came near. Śiva felt his mind stir, looked round, saw the archer, and the fire of his third eye burned Kāma to ash.
Rati’s lament moved the gods; Kāma was promised a new life (he is later born as Pradyumna, Kṛṣṇa’s son) and lives on without a body as Anaṅga, “the bodiless”.
Pārvatī then won Śiva by her own austerity, and their son Skanda killed Tāraka. Spring and love had done their part.

ZAPPY: So love lost?

PROF. BOLT: Love lost its body, Zappy, and won everywhere else. And Pārvatī showed that devotion and effort move even Śiva.

Spring prepares the ground.

IN THE VERSE

Yā kundendu

The dhyāna verse of Sarasvatī
yā kundendu tuṣārahāradhavalā yā śubhravastrāvṛtāyā vīṇāvaradaṇḍamaṇḍitakarā yā śvetapadmāsanāyā brahmācyuta śaṅkaraprabhṛtibhir devaiḥ sadā pūjitāsā māṃ pātu sarasvatī bhagavatī niḥśeṣajāḍyāpahā
She who is white as jasmine, the moon, frost and a pearl necklace, dressed in pure white, whose hands are adorned by the vīṇā and the staff of boons, seated on a white lotus, worshipped always by Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śiva and the other gods: may that Sarasvatī, the goddess who removes all dullness, protect me.
WordMeaning
kundajasmine
indumoon
tuṣārafrost
vīṇāthe lute
śvetapadmawhite lotus
jāḍyadullness, inertia

A picture to hold. A dhyāna verse paints the deity so the mind can rest on her. Every image here is white or bright, and the prayer at the end is precise: not for wealth or marks, but for the removal of jāḍya, the heaviness of mind that blocks learning. This verse is sung in schools across India each morning.

Text as given by shlokam.org (“before learning” prayers); variant readings exist (devais sadā / devaiḥ sadā).
Ask her to lift the dullness.

IN THE VERSE

Sarasvati namastubhyam

The verse before the first letter
sarasvati namastubhyaṃ varade kāmarūpiṇividyārambhaṃ kariṣyāmi siddhir bhavatu me sadā
Sarasvatī, salutations to you, giver of boons, who takes whatever form is desired. I am about to begin my learning: may success be mine always.
WordMeaning
namas tubhyamsalutations to you
varadegiver of boons
kāma-rūpiṇitaking any form at will
vidyārambhamthe beginning of study
siddhiḥsuccess, fulfilment

The child’s first prayer. This is the verse most Hindu children learn first for study, said before opening a book or writing the first letters. Kāmarūpiṇī is read either as “taking any form she wishes” or “who gives form to one’s wishes”; both fit a goddess who becomes whatever the learner needs. Variants replace kāmarūpiṇi with jñānarūpiṇi, “whose form is knowledge”.

Text as given by greenmesg.org and shlokam.org; variants in the second word of line one are noted above.
Begin study with a bow. Before maths too: it is one of her rooms.

THE FIRST LETTER

Akṣarābhyāsa and vidyārambha

The names. Akṣarābhyāsa, “practice of letters”, in Telugu and Kannada usage; vidyārambha, “beginning of learning”, in Sanskrit and Kerala usage. It is one of the life-rites of childhood (see The Sixteen Saṃskāras).

The rite. A child of about three to five sits on a parent’s or teacher’s lap. Guided by the elder’s hand, the child writes Oṃ, or a short salutation, and the first letters of the alphabet, in a plate of rice grains, on a slate or on sand; a gold ring sometimes traces the letters on the tongue.

When. Vasant Pañcamī is the great day in the North and in Andhra and Telangana; in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu the more common day is Vijayadaśamī after Navarātri. Families choose either, or a good day fixed by the priest.

Where. At home, at school, or at a Sarasvatī temple: Śāradā of Śṛṅgeri, Basara on the Godāvarī in Telangana, Paṇachikkāḍ in Kerala (the “Dakṣiṇa Mūkāmbikā”) and Koothanur in Tamil Nadu are famous for it.

ZAPPY: Letters in rice?

PROF. BOLT: Rice is food, Zappy, and letters are food for the mind. The child’s first writing goes on the thing that sustains life.

The first letter, in rice.

THE YELLOW DAY

Mustard, turmeric and saffron

The fields. On the North Indian plains, mustard (sarsoṃ) blooms in Māgha and turns the land yellow. The festival takes its colour from the fields.

What people wear. Yellow clothes, yellow turbans in Punjab, yellow flowers in the hair; images of Sarasvatī are dressed in yellow for the day, though her own colour is white.

What people eat. Saffron-yellow rice (kesar bhāt), boondi, yellow sweets and khichdi; in Bengal, a fruit called kul (jujube) is traditionally not eaten until it is first offered to Sarasvatī on this day.

Why yellow fits. Many read yellow as the colour of light and of ripening knowledge, a colour between the white of the goddess and the gold of the sun. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: But Sarasvatī wears white!

PROF. BOLT: She does, Zappy. On this day she lets the fields dress her.

The fields dress the goddess.

ACROSS INDIA

One day, many ways

🪁 Punjab and the North-west

Yellow turbans, yellow rice and kites: Basant is a season festival. Maharaja Ranjit Singh held a yearly Basant fair; Namdhari Sikhs mark the start of spring on this day.

📿 Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, Assam

Sarasvatī Pūjā in every school and home: a clay image, books and pens placed at her feet and not used until the next day; in Odisha children begin school with khaḍi chuan, touching chalk.

✍️ Andhra and Telangana

Śrī Pañcamī: crowds of children for akṣarābhyāsa at Basara and at the Jñāna Sarasvatī temples; Kanaka Durgā of Vijayawada is dressed as Sarasvatī.

🪔 Karnataka

Special worship of Śāradāmbā at Śṛṅgeri; in most Kannada homes the great Sarasvatī Pūjā remains the Navarātri one, and Vasant Pañcamī is kept more quietly.

🌸 Maharashtra, Uttarakhand, Rajasthan

Śiva and Pārvatī worshipped with mango blossom and ears of wheat; newly-wed couples visit temples in yellow; jasmine garlands in Rajasthan.

🌺 Beyond India

In Bali, Hari Raya Saraswati honours her in homes, schools and temples; at the Nizamuddin dargah in Delhi a Sufi Basant is kept with yellow flowers.

ZAPPY: Kites and books on one day?

PROF. BOLT: The sky and the slate, Zappy. Spring wind for the kite, fresh mind for the letters.

One spring, many customs.

KEEPING THE DAY

A simple Sarasvatī pūjā at home

Bath and yellow. An early bath, clean yellow or white clothes, the house swept and a rangoli drawn.

The altar. An image or picture of Sarasvatī on a low stool, with the household’s books, pens, instruments and tools of study placed before her: on this day they rest and are worshipped instead of used.

Offerings. Yellow and white flowers, sandal paste, akṣata (unbroken rice), fruit (in Bengal the jujube), sweets, a lamp and incense; the verses on the previous pages, the Yā Devī verse of buddhi, or any Sarasvatī hymn the family knows.

Next morning. The books are taken back with a short prayer and study resumes. Many schools hold their own pūjā and children are given a day free of homework.

ZAPPY: We don’t study on her day?

PROF. BOLT: The books rest at her feet, Zappy. You take them back the next day, as gifts from her.

Books at her feet.

WHERE SHE IS HONOURED

Śṛṅgeri, Basara and others

Śṛṅgeri. On the Tuṅgā in Chikkamagaḷūru district, the Śāradā Pīṭha founded by Ādi Śaṅkara keeps Śāradāmbā as its presiding goddess and performs akṣarābhyāsa as a regular seva. On Vasant Pañcamī (here also called Sārasvata Pañcamī) the Jagadguru offers special worship; on 23 January 2026, for example, it closed with the pūrṇāhuti of Sahasra Caṇḍī, Rudra and Lalitā homas. See our Śṛṅgeri Śāradā guide.

Basara. The Jñāna Sarasvatī temple on the Godāvarī in Telangana is the busiest place in India for a child’s first letter on Śrī Pañcamī.

Kerala and Tamil Nadu. Paṇachikkāḍ near Kōṭṭayam, called Dakṣiṇa Mūkāmbikā, and Koothanur near Kumbakōṇam, one of very few temples where Sarasvatī is the main deity.

Kollūr. Mūkāmbikā of Kollūr is honoured as a giver of speech and learning, especially by musicians and writers.

ZAPPY: Is Sarasvatī the main deity in many temples?

PROF. BOLT: Fewer than you’d think, Zappy. She lives mostly in homes, schools and in the throat of every singer.

She lives where learning lives.

THE SPRING CALENDAR

What follows Vasant Pañcamī

☀️ Two days later

Ratha Saptamī, the Sun’s chariot and the arka bath, on Māgha Śukla Saptamī.

🏹 The day after that

Bhīṣma Aṣṭamī, when the grandsire left his body in the sun’s northern course.

🔱 The dark fortnight of Māgha

Mahāśivarātri (by the amānta reckoning used in the South).

🎨 Forty days on

Holī at the full moon of Phālguna, when tradition says spring is in full bloom; Kāma’s story returns there too.

PROF. BOLT: The calendar is a story told across the year, Zappy. Pañcamī is its first page of spring.

WHAT THE DAY MEANS

Beginnings, effort and fresh minds

Beginnings. Spring is when seeds wake; the festival asks us to begin learning, or begin again, in the same season. Many artists and musicians start a new piece, or a new pupil, on this day.

Humility. Laying one’s books at the goddess’s feet is an admission that knowledge is received, not owned. The child is guided by an elder’s hand for the first letter, and in a sense never stops being guided.

Love and restraint. The Kāma story sets desire against discipline and lets neither simply win: love prepares the ground, and Pārvatī’s own tapas completes the work. Many teachers read this as the balance the season needs. (Interpretation.)

Clarity. The prayer in the dhyāna verse is for the removal of jāḍya, dullness. The aim of learning, in this view, is a clear mind more than a full one. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing on Vasant Pañcamī?

PROF. BOLT: Sit with your books, Zappy, say the verse once slowly, and begin one thing you’ve been putting off.

Begin with a clear mind.
