GAṆEŚA CATURTHĪ

Welcome, worship, farewell

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On Bhādrapada śukla caturthī, India welcomes Gaṇeśa: a clay image at home, a giant one under a pandal, modakas, dūrvā, āratī, and after a few days a joyful, tearful farewell into water.

This guide covers the festival from the first welcome to the last “Gaṇapati Bāppā Moryā”, its history from Pune’s Peshwas to Tilak, its regional flavours, and a greener way to keep it.

ZAPPY: Is it his birthday?

PROF. BOLT: That’s how most people see it, Zappy. The Purāṇas give his birth on Caturthī too.

Gaṇeśa Caturthī, on the fourth day of the bright half of Bhādrapada, is the greatest Gaṇeśa festival, widely kept as his birthday. A clay image is welcomed, worshipped and finally immersed in water.

Families keep him for anything from a day and a half to ten or eleven days; the ten-day festival ends on Ananta Caturdaśī. In cities, huge public images stand under decorated pandals.

Lokmanya Tilak made it a mass public festival in 1893, and it has grown ever since.

ZAPPY: Why does the date move?

PROF. BOLT: It follows the moon, not the sun. Every year it lands somewhere between late August and late September.

The festival falls on Bhādrapada śukla caturthī, which moves with the lunar calendar between late August and late September. In 2026 it began on Monday 14 September, with the ten-day festival ending on Friday 25 September.

In Karnataka, Gaurī is welcomed the day before, as the previous guide describes.

Ananta Caturdaśī, the fourteenth lunar day, is the day of the great immersion processions.

ZAPPY: Why invite him into clay?

PROF. BOLT: For a few days the clay becomes his home. Then he goes back, and the clay goes back to the earth.

On the first day, a clay image, from palm-sized to towering, is installed on a decorated altar at home or in a pandal. A priest performs prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā, inviting Gaṇeśa to dwell in the image, followed by the sixteen traditional offerings.

Some families keep the image’s face covered until it is installed.

In keeping with this series’ rule, we name the parts of the worship without giving the rite itself.

WHAT HE IS OFFERED

ZAPPY: Why twenty-one modakas?

PROF. BOLT: A favourite number in his worship: twenty-one modakas, twenty-one dūrvā bunches. Devotees love the generosity of it.

Gaṇeśa is offered coconut, jaggery and, by custom, twenty-one modakas, along with dūrvā grass and red flowers, especially hibiscus.

The food is shared as prasāda. Many households keep festival meals simple and vegetarian, without onion or garlic.

Twenty-one is a favourite number in his worship.

ZAPPY: Who wrote the Marathi āratī?

PROF. BOLT: It’s traditionally attributed to the saint Samartha Rāmadāsa, Shivaji’s guru.

Mornings and evenings are marked by āratī, the waving of lamps with song. In Maharashtra, the best-known is the Marathi “Sukhakartā Duḥkhahartā”, traditionally attributed to Samartha Rāmadāsa.

Many homes chant the Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa; pandals host music, plays and competitions.

The Hymns part of this series will take the Sanskrit hymns verse by verse.

HISTORY OF THE PUBLIC FESTIVAL

Pune and beyond
OldA household festival, honoured for centuries
ShivajiTradition says it was publicly celebrated in Pune in his time
PeshwasGaṇeśa their family deity; grand celebrations in 18th-century Pune
1893Tilak makes it a mass public festival
WhyTo gather people across caste and class, partly against British rule
ImmersionTilak set the tenth-day immersion of public images

ZAPPY: A festival for freedom?

PROF. BOLT: In part. Public gatherings were hard under British rule; a festival for Gaṇeśa brought people together openly.

Gaṇeśa Caturthī was for centuries mainly a household festival. Tradition says it was celebrated publicly in Pune in Shivaji’s time, though the evidence is thin; under the Peshwas, whose family deity was Gaṇeśa, it was grandly celebrated at court.

In 1893 Lokmanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak turned it into a mass public festival, partly to bring people together across caste and class in the national movement, and established the tenth-day immersion of public images.

Thapan notes that it is now Maharashtra’s most popular festival.

ZAPPY: Why so big?

PROF. BOLT: Pride, devotion and fun. Neighbourhoods compete to welcome him best.

Public images are housed in pandals, temporary shrine-stages built and decorated by neighbourhood groups called maṇḍaḷs. Some images stand many metres tall.

Pandal themes range from myths and famous temples to current events and social messages, and many run cultural programmes and charities.

The most famous draw enormous crowds throughout the festival.

ZAPPY: Is it the same everywhere?

PROF. BOLT: Same god, same day, different flavour. That’s the beauty of it.

See: Gaurī–Gaṇeśa Habba

Maharashtra’s Gaṇeśotsava is the largest public celebration. Karnataka keeps Gaurī–Gaṇeśa Habba; Tamil Nadu, Vināyaka Caturthī, often with clay or papier-mâché images; Andhra Pradesh and Telangana worship clay and turmeric Gaṇeśas at home; in Goa it is Chavath, a great family festival.

Wherever Indians live, from Mauritius to the Americas, the festival goes with them.

The god and the day are shared; the flavours are local.

ZAPPY: Why say ‘come back soon’?

PROF. BOLT: Because he’s family. Every farewell is a promise of next year.

At the end of the chosen days, the image is carried in procession, with drums, dancing and coloured powder, to the sea, a river, a lake, a tank, or a bucket at home, and immersed.

In Marathi the crowd calls “Gaṇapati Bāppā Moryā, pudhcyā varṣī lavkar yā”: come back soon next year.

He returns to Kailāsa; the clay returns to the earth.

ZAPPY: What if I look by accident?

PROF. BOLT: Then listen to the Syamantaka story, and smile. That’s the cure.

See: The moon’s curse

On Caturthī night many avoid looking at the moon, because of the story told in Part B: the moon laughed at Gaṇeśa and was cursed, so that whoever sees it that night risks false blame.

Kṛṣṇa himself, tradition says, was caught by it over the Syamantaka jewel; hearing that story is the remedy.

A GREENER FESTIVAL

Clay comes back

RespectFor the river that receives him

ZAPPY: Can a festival be green?

PROF. BOLT: It used to be: clay, water, flowers. We’re learning it again.

Plaster of Paris images and chemical paints pollute the water they are immersed in. In 2020 the Central Pollution Control Board barred plaster and thermocol idols for immersion.

Many families and maṇḍaḷs have returned to clay images and natural colours, and cities provide immersion tanks. Some images even hold seeds that sprout after immersion.

Clay is, after all, the traditional way.

WHAT THE FESTIVAL TEACHES

For the reader
WelcomeA god as a guest in your home
CommunityA neighbourhood around one image
Let goImmersion: love without holding on
ReturnEvery farewell promises next year
HistoryA festival that helped a nation gather
CareFor water and earth

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Welcome him fully, let him go gladly. That’s Caturthī.

Caturthī teaches welcome: a god received like a beloved guest. It teaches community, as neighbourhoods gather round one image.

And through visarjana it teaches letting go with love, and the hope of return.

Its history adds a lesson of its own: a festival helped a nation find its voice.
