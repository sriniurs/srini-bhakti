GAṆEŚA JAYANTĪ

A winter birthday

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

In the cold of Māgha, the Konkan coast celebrates Gaṇeśa’s birthday: Gaṇeśa Jayantī, or Tilakunda Caturthī, with sesame laḍḍus and quiet family worship.

This guide explains why Maharashtra keeps a winter birthday, what sesame has to do with it, and where it is celebrated most.

ZAPPY: Two birthdays?

PROF. BOLT: Different traditions, Zappy. In the Konkan, his birthday is in Māgha.

Gaṇeśa Jayantī falls on the fourth day of the bright half of Māgha, in January or February. In Maharashtra, the Konkan and Goa it is held to be his true birthday.

It is also called Tilakunda Caturthī, because sesame is offered and shared, and Varada Caturthī. It is quieter and more personal than the great Bhādrapada festival.

In 2026 it fell on 22 January.

ZAPPY: So which one is right?

PROF. BOLT: Each tradition answers for itself. Most of India picks Bhādrapada; the Konkan keeps Māgha.

Most of India keeps Bhādrapada śukla caturthī as Gaṇeśa’s birthday. In Maharashtrian tradition, however, Bhādrapada’s day is often understood as a vow, and Māgha’s as the birth itself; writers cite the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa and Viṣṇu Purāṇa for this.

Others say Bhādrapada marks his arrival on earth and Māgha his birth. The Marathi calendar actually counts three Gaṇeśa birthdays.

We report the traditions without choosing between them.

ZAPPY: Why sesame?

PROF. BOLT: It’s winter. Sesame and jaggery warm you, and Gaṇeśa likes sweets anyway.

Tila means sesame. On this day sesame and jaggery laḍḍus are offered to Gaṇeśa and shared, and some bathe with sesame paste.

Sesame is considered warming and healthy in the cold of Māgha. The day comes soon after Makara Saṅkrānti, the great sesame-sweet festival of the season.

ZAPPY: Do people put up pandals?

PROF. BOLT: Some do, especially in Mumbai, but it’s much smaller than in Bhādrapada.

At home, an image is worshipped with til laḍḍus, modakas, dūrvā and red flowers, and some keep a fast. Gaṇeśa temples hold special worship.

Some neighbourhoods, especially in Mumbai, hold small Māghī Gaṇeśotsavas with public images.

In keeping with this series’ rule, we describe the observance without giving a rite.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa guards the west coast?

PROF. BOLT: That’s Ganapatipule’s title: the western sentinel god of India.

Māghī Gaṇeśa is strongest along the Konkan coast of Maharashtra and in Goa. The seaside temple at Ganapatipule, whose Gaṇeśa is called the “western sentinel god of India”, celebrates Jayantī.

In Goa the day is kept with devotion in homes and temples, and the Aṣṭavināyaka temples hold special worship.

ZAPPY: Is there ever a month without a Gaṇeśa day?

PROF. BOLT: No. Two every month, and three birthdays a year in Marathi homes.

See: Saṅkaṣṭī Caturthī

Gaṇeśa Jayantī is the Vināyaka Caturthī of Māgha, one of the twelve bright-fortnight caturthīs of the year. Saṅkaṣṭī falls in every dark fortnight.

In northern India, the dark-fortnight caturthī of Māgha is kept as Sakat Chauth.

Gaṇeśa’s calendar is never empty.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: In Māgha, Gaṇeśa’s birthday comes wrapped in sesame and jaggery.

Gaṇeśa Jayantī shows a quieter devotion: family worship, seasonal food, no crowds.

It is also a reminder that Indian traditions can hold more than one answer, even to the question of a god’s birthday.
