MĀGHA CATURTHĪ · TILAKUNDA · THE KONKAN

Gaṇeśa Jayantī

His Winter Birthday in Māgha · explained and illustrated

Sesame for a winter birthday

HOW THIS WORKS: Why Gaṇeśa has more than one birthday, the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa’s avatāras, sesame and the season, the Aṣṭavināyaka, the Konkan and Karnataka’s shrines, and three verses.

WHERE WE ARE

Most of India keeps Gaṇeśa’s birthday in the rains, on Bhādrapada’s Caturthī. Maharashtra, the Konkan coast and Goa keep another, in the cold of Māgha: Gaṇeśa Jayantī, the bright fourth of Māgha, which their tradition holds to be the birth itself. It comes wrapped in sesame and jaggery, and is quieter, more personal, without the crowds of the great festival.

This article explains why Gaṇeśa has more than one birthday, tells the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa’s stories of his avatāras born for the ages of the world, visits the Aṣṭavināyaka and the Konkan coast, and Karnataka’s own great Gaṇeśa shrines, and gives three verses for the day.

WHICH IS THE BIRTHDAY?

Three Gaṇeśa days in one year

Bhādrapada. Most of India keeps Bhādrapada Śukla Caturthī as Gaṇeśa’s birthday: the great festival of the rains (read our Gaṇeśa Caturthī article).

Māgha. Maharashtrian tradition, citing the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, often treats Bhādrapada’s day as a vow (vrata) and Māgha’s as the birth itself. Others say Bhādrapada marks his arrival on earth and Māgha his birth.

Three. The Marathi calendar counts three Gaṇeśa birthdays, including one in Vaiśākha in some traditions. We report the traditions without choosing.

Every month. Each bright Caturthī is a Vināyaka Caturthī, each dark one a Saṅkaṣṭī · 17 articles. Gaṇeśa’s calendar is never empty.

ZAPPY: So which one is right?

PROF. BOLT: Each tradition answers for itself, Zappy. Most of India picks Bhādrapada; the Konkan keeps Māgha.

More than one birthday is fine.

MAHOTKAṬA, SON OF ADITI

The avatāra of the first age

The story

Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, Krīḍā Khaṇḍa, chs. 1–72 (as summarised in our four yuga avatāras guide).

In the Satya age two demon brothers, Devāntaka (“ender of gods”) and Narāntaka (“ender of men”), won great boons and terrorised the three worlds.
The gods prayed. Gaṇeśa was born as a child to the sage Kaśyapa and Aditi, and named Mahotkaṭa Vināyaka.
Even as a boy he destroyed the demons sent to kill him, one after another, in a series of adventures the Purāṇa tells at length.
Grown to his full form, ten-armed, red, riding a lion, he faced the two brothers and destroyed them.
The Māghī tradition connects Gaṇeśa’s birth to this kind of descent: God born as a child to set the world right.

ZAPPY: Ten arms and a lion?

PROF. BOLT: The biggest form, Zappy, for the first and greatest age. The forms grow smaller as the ages decline.

Born to set things right.

MAYŪREŚVARA

The peacock Gaṇeśa of Morgaon

The story

Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, Krīḍā Khaṇḍa, chs. 73–126.

In the Tretā age the demon Sindhu won immortality, held as a pot of nectar in his belly, and tormented the worlds.
Gaṇeśa was born to Śiva and Pārvatī as Mayūreśvara, six-armed and white, riding a peacock.
He fought Sindhu and drew the nectar out of his belly, and with it the demon’s power.
Morgaon, first of the eight Aṣṭavināyaka shrines, keeps him as Moreśvar, “lord of the peacock” (Marathi mor).
Popular tradition says he later gave the peacock to his brother Skanda, who still rides it.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa on a peacock?

PROF. BOLT: In that age, yes, Zappy. And it’s why Morgaon’s Gaṇeśa is Moreśvar.

The peacock lent to his brother.

IN THE VERSE

Gaṇeśa Pañcaratnam, verse 1

Holding the modaka with joy
mudākarāttamodakaṃ sadāvimuktisādhakaṃkalādharāvataṃsakaṃ vilāsilokarakṣakamanāyakaikanāyakaṃ vināśitebhadaityakaṃnatāśubhāśunāśakaṃ namāmi taṃ vināyakam
I bow to Vināyaka, who holds the modaka in his hand with joy, who always brings liberation, who wears the crescent moon as an ornament, protector of the world at play, the one leader of those who have no leader, destroyer of the elephant-demon, who quickly destroys the misfortune of those who bow.
WordMeaning
mudāwith joy
kara-ātta-modakammodaka held in the hand
kalādhara-avataṃsakamcrowned with the crescent
anāyaka-eka-nāyakamsole leader of the leaderless
vināśita-ibha-daityakamslayer of the elephant-demon
natāśubha-āśu-nāśakamquickly ending devotees’ misfortune

Śaṅkara’s hymn. The Gaṇeśa Pañcaratnam, attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara, is sung in Karnataka homes and temples on every Gaṇeśa day, Jayantī included. “Leader of those who have no leader” is one of the tenderest titles any god has.

Text as on our Gaṇeśa Pañcaratnam page.
The leader of those with no leader.

TILAKUNDA CATURTHĪ

Sesame for the cold month

Tila. Tila is sesame, and the day is also called Tilakunda Caturthī: sesame and jaggery laḍḍus are offered to Gaṇeśa and shared; some bathe with sesame paste.

The season. Māgha is cold; sesame and jaggery are warming foods, which is why they also fill the plates at Makara Saṅkrānti a few weeks earlier.

Varada Caturthī. The bright Caturthī is also called Varada, “boon-granting”: Gaṇeśa is worshipped as the giver of boons.

The worship. A clay or metal image at home, a day’s fast for some, modakas and til laḍḍus, dūrvā and red flowers; Gaṇeśa temples hold special worship, and some Mumbai neighbourhoods keep a small public Māghī Gaṇeśotsava.

ZAPPY: Why sesame?

PROF. BOLT: It’s winter, Zappy. Sesame and jaggery warm you, and Gaṇeśa likes sweets anyway.

A birthday wrapped in sesame.

THE AṢṬAVINĀYAKA

Eight self-born Gaṇeśas around Pune

🦚 Morgaon

Moreśvar, the peacock Gaṇeśa: the pilgrimage begins and ends here.

⛰️ Siddhaṭek and Pālī

Siddhivināyaka on the Bhīmā, where Viṣṇu is said to have worshipped him; Ballāḷeśvar at Pālī, named after a boy devotee.

🏞️ Mahaḍ, Theur, Lenyādri

Varadavināyaka; Cintāmaṇi; Girijātmaja in a Buddhist rock-cave.

🛕 Ojhar and Rāñjaṇgāv

Vighneśvara; Mahāgaṇapati, worshipped by Śiva before he fought Tripura.

ZAPPY: Eight Gaṇeśas in one trip?

PROF. BOLT: A circuit of several hundred kilometres round Pune, Zappy. On Māghī Jayantī they’re all crowded. Read our Aṣṭavināyaka guide.

Eight shrines, one circuit.

THE KONKAN AND GOA

Where Māghī is strongest

Gaṇapatipuḷe. On the Ratnāgiri coast, the seaside temple of Gaṇapatipuḷe, whose self-born Gaṇeśa is called the Paścima Dvāradevatā, the western guardian god of India, keeps Māghī Jayantī as its great day; pilgrims walk round the hill that is held to be the god himself.

Goa. In Goa the day is kept with devotion in homes and the old temples of Ponda; Konkani families who also keep the great Bhādrapada festival mark Māghī quietly.

Mumbai. Some neighbourhoods hold Māghī Gaṇeśotsavas with public images for a day or more, a small echo of the rainy-season pandals.

A family day. Everywhere, the day is mainly for the home: one image, a lamp, laḍḍus, the Atharvaśīrṣa and the elders’ blessings.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa guards the west coast?

PROF. BOLT: That’s Gaṇapatipuḷe’s title, Zappy: the western sentinel god of India.

The western sentinel.

GAṆEŚA IN KARNATAKA

Shrines to visit on any Gaṇeśa day

🛕 Iḍagunji

On the Uttara Kannaḍa coast, the standing two-armed Vināyaka of Iḍagunji, one of the most visited Gaṇeśa temples of the coast.

🪨 Hampi

The great monolithic Sāsivekāḷu and Kaḍalekāḷu Gaṇeśas among the ruins: see Hampi.

🌊 Gōkarṇa

Where the boy Gaṇeśa tricked Rāvaṇa into setting down the Ātmaliṅga: see Gōkarṇa.

🏙️ Bengaluru

The Doḍḍa Gaṇapati of Basavanagudi, a huge boulder image covered in butter on special days: see Doḍḍa Gaṇapati.

ZAPPY: Do Karnataka temples keep Māghī?

PROF. BOLT: Many hold special worship on every Vināyaka Caturthī, Zappy, Māgha’s included. The great crowds come in Bhādrapada.

Karnataka’s own Gaṇeśas.

THE GAṆEŚA GĪTĀ

The teaching of Gajānana

The Dvāpara avatāra. In the Dvāpara age Gaṇeśa came as Gajānana, four-armed on his mouse, to defeat the demon Sindūra; popular tradition says he smeared himself with the demon’s red, which is why sindūra is offered to him.

The teaching. To King Vareṇya he taught the Gaṇeśa Gītā (Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, Krīḍā Khaṇḍa 138–148), a Gāṇapatya counterpart of the Bhagavad Gītā on yoga, action and devotion, with Gaṇeśa as the supreme.

The fourth. In our own Kali age, the Purāṇa says, he will come as Dhūmraketu, two-armed, smoke-coloured, on a horse, at the end of the age.

Why it fits. A birthday is a good day to remember that the god is born again and again, in every age, as the need arises.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa has a Gītā too?

PROF. BOLT: The Gāṇapatyas’ own, Zappy. Same idea as Kṛṣṇa’s: God teaches when a king is ready to listen.

Born in every age.

IN THE VERSE

Agajānana padmārkaṃ

The one-tusked lord of the mountain’s daughter
agajānana padmārkaṃ gajānanam aharniśamanekadaṃ taṃ bhaktānām ekadantam upāsmahe
We worship day and night the elephant-faced one, who is a sun to the lotus that is the face of Pārvatī (the mountain-born), the one-tusked who gives much to his devotees.
WordMeaning
aga-ja-ānanathe face of the mountain-born (Pārvatī)
padma-arkamthe sun to the lotus
ahar-niśamday and night
anekadamgiver of many things
eka-dantamthe one-tusked
upāsmahewe worship

A word game. The verse plays on aneka (many) and eka (one): the one-tusked who gives many gifts. And it puts the mother first: Gaṇeśa is the sun that makes Pārvatī’s face bloom like a lotus. A good birthday verse.

Text as on our Gaṇeśa daily ślokas page (verse 3).
The one-tusked who gives many gifts.

WHAT THE DAY TEACHES

A quieter devotion

Quiet. Gaṇeśa Jayantī is devotion without crowds: one image, one lamp, a plate of sesame sweets, the family.

Many answers. The day is a reminder that Indian traditions can hold more than one answer, even to the question of a god’s birthday, without anyone being wrong.

The season. Its food and worship suit the weather, as Bhādrapada’s suit the rains: the festival follows the year.

Return. Gaṇeśa comes back in winter too. The remover of obstacles visits twice: once loudly, once softly.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Gaṇeśa Jayantī?

PROF. BOLT: Make a few til laḍḍus, Zappy, offer one to Gaṇeśa with the Pañcaratnam, and give the rest away.

In Māgha, softly.
