Gaṇeśa and the Moon

Why the Moon Is Worshipped Tonight · explained and illustrated

Why the moon is worshipped tonight

HOW THIS WORKS: The moon’s laugh and Gaṇeśa’s curse, the forgiveness, Bhālacandra, the Syamantaka jewel, and the meaning of arghya.

WHERE WE ARE

Why does the Saṅkaṣṭī fast end with the moon? And why, on Gaṇeśa’s great festival in Bhādrapada, are people told not to look at it?

The answer is one story told several ways: the moon laughed at Gaṇeśa, was cursed, and was forgiven. This part tells it and what it means for the night of Saṅkaṣṭī.

THE MOON LAUGHS

The story in order

The story

Tellings differ in detail; this is the common outline.

One night Gaṇeśa, full of modakas from a feast, was riding home on his mouse. A snake crossed the path, the mouse shied, and Gaṇeśa tumbled; his belly split and the sweets spilled out.
Gaṇeśa tied up his belly with the snake itself.
The moon, proud of his own beauty, watched from the sky and laughed.
Gaṇeśa cursed him: whoever looked at the moon would suffer false accusation. In some tellings the moon was to vanish altogether.
The gods pleaded and the moon repented; Gaṇeśa softened the curse to one night only, the bright fourth of Bhādrapada.

ZAPPY: So that’s why we don’t look on Gaṇeśa Caturthī.

PROF. BOLT: Right. And see our Moon’s Curse story for the full version.

A laugh that cost the moon dearly.

THE MOON FORGIVEN

From curse to honour

Repentance. The moon’s apology turns the story.

Penance. In some tellings the moon worships Gaṇeśa on a caturthī and is restored.

The link to Saṅkaṣṭī. Tradition joins the two: on the dark fourth the moon, forgiven, is honoured, and the devotee breaks the fast only after seeing him. We mark this link as traditional interpretation.

Two nights, one lesson. On one night the moon reminds us of pride; on the other, of forgiveness.

From mockery to arghya.

WHO IS CANDRA

The moon as a god

Candra, Soma. The moon is a deity in his own right, one of the nine planetary deities, lord of Monday.

His wives. The twenty-seven nakṣatras, daughters of Dakṣa. The moon favoured Rohiṇī alone, and Dakṣa cursed him to waste away.

Waxing and waning. Śiva sheltered him on his head and the curse was softened: he wanes, then grows again. (Purāṇic tellings differ.)

A moon of faults. So the moon is twice a figure of pride cursed and pardoned: by Dakṣa, and by Gaṇeśa.

ZAPPY: The moon gets cursed a lot.

PROF. BOLT: Beautiful and vain, in the stories. And each time forgiven. That’s why he’s good company on a night of release.

Twice cursed, twice forgiven.

BHĀLACANDRA

The moon on Gaṇeśa’s brow

The name. Bhālacandra: “moon on the forehead”. Gaṇeśa wears the crescent, as his father Śiva does.

A sign of forgiveness. Wearing the moon shows the quarrel is over; the mocker is now an ornament.

A Saṅkaṣṭī name. Bhālacandra is the name of the Phālguna Saṅkaṣṭī; see Bhālacandra.

In the stotra. The ninth of the twelve names in the Saṅkaṭanāśana Stotra.

ZAPPY: He wears the one who laughed at him?

PROF. BOLT: That’s grace. Not just forgiving, but honouring.

The mocker becomes an ornament.

THE SYAMANTAKA JEWEL

Even Kṛṣṇa was not spared

The tale. Kṛṣṇa, it is said, glimpsed the moon on Bhādrapada’s bright fourth.

The accusation. Soon after, the Syamantaka jewel went missing and Kṛṣṇa was accused of taking it.

The clearing. He tracked it to Jāmbavān’s cave, won it back, and cleared his name. The jewel story is in the Bhāgavata and Viṣṇu Purāṇas; its link to the moon-curse belongs to the Gaṇeśa Caturthī tradition.

The remedy. Hearing the Syamantaka story is the traditional remedy for anyone who sees that moon by mistake.

False accusation, cleared.

THE WANING MOON

What the timing suggests

Four days after full. On Saṅkaṣṭī the moon is still bright but diminishing.

An image of trouble. Teachers read it as life after a high point, when things start to go wrong. That is when Saṅkaṣṭī is kept. (Interpretation.)

Waiting. The late moonrise makes the vow a lesson in patience.

Light returns. However late, the moon does rise; that is the promise of the night.

However late, the moon rises.

OFFERING WATER TO THE MOON

Candra-arghya

Arghya. Water offered as to an honoured guest, from a small vessel or the cupped hands.

With it. Flowers, sandal paste, a few grains of rice; some add milk.

To whom. To the moon as a deity (Candra), and through him to Gaṇeśa who forgave him.

The prayer. Use the short prayer printed in your family’s vrata book; we do not print one here because we could not verify a single standard text.

Water for the honoured guest.

TELLING CHILDREN

The lesson in a minute

The story. The moon laughed when Gaṇeśa fell; Gaṇeśa was hurt and cursed him; the moon said sorry and was forgiven.

Ask them. Have you ever laughed at someone who fell?

The answer. Saying sorry fixed it; and Gaṇeśa even wears the moon now.

Tonight. Let the child spot the moonrise and pour the arghya.

Don’t laugh at a fall; say sorry if you did.
