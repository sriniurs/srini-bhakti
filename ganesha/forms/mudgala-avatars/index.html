THE EIGHT AVATĀRAS

Eight battles in the mind

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

The Mudgala Purāṇa describes eight avatāras of Gaṇeśa. Each conquers a demon who is a fault of the mind: envy, pride, delusion, greed, anger, desire, possessiveness and ego.

This guide tells each story, gives its mount and meaning, and shows the pattern: faults tamed and forgiven, not destroyed.

Śrī Vakratuṇḍāya namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

PROF. BOLT: Ego, Zappy. That’s why it comes last.

DemonsEach one a fault of the mind: envy, pride, delusion, greed, anger, desire, possessiveness, ego
MountsLion, mouse, peacock, serpent, horse
EndingOften surrender and forgiveness, not slaughter

ZAPPY: Demons named after faults?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly, Zappy. The battlefield is the mind.

The Mudgala Purāṇa, one of the two Purāṇas wholly devoted to Gaṇeśa, describes eight avatāras. Each conquers a demon who personifies a fault of the mind, from envy to ego.

The battles are typical Purāṇic stories, but they often end not in slaughter but in surrender and forgiveness. Philosophically, each avatāra is a stage in which Brahman unfolds into creation.

These eight are different from the four yuga avatāras of the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, described in the next guide.

THE MUDGALA PURĀṆA

A Gāṇapatya scripture
KindAn upapurāṇa, one of two Purāṇas wholly about Gaṇeśa
StructureSections for each avatāra; tradition counts nine parts, the last on yoga
IntroducedThe eight avatāras at 1.17.24–28
DateDebated: older than the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa (Hazra) or later (Granoff); added to until the 17th–18th c.
EditionNo critical edition; printed versions differ
AlsoFirst to list the 32 forms; 22 chapters praise Morgaon

ZAPPY: Who was Mudgala?

PROF. BOLT: A sage to whom the text is ascribed. The Purāṇa takes his name.

The Mudgala Purāṇa is an upapurāṇa, a secondary Purāṇa, and with the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa one of only two devoted entirely to Gaṇeśa. It introduces the eight avatāras at 1.17.24–28 and gives each its own section; tradition counts nine parts, the last on yoga.

Its date is debated. Hazra thought it older than the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa; Granoff argues it is later, since it names the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa; Thapan sees an old core with additions into the 17th–18th centuries. There is no critical edition.

It is also the first text to list the thirty-two forms, and devotes twenty-two chapters to Morgaon.

1. VAKRATUṆḌA

Conqueror of jealousy
Name meansCurved trunk
DemonMātsaryāsura
FaultJealousy, envy
MountA lion
MeaningThe form of Brahman as all bodies together
OutcomeThe demon surrenders and is forgiven

ZAPPY: Why does envy come first?

PROF. BOLT: Envy is where many faults begin. Gaṇeśa starts at the root.

Born of Indra’s carelessness, the demon Matsara won a boon of fearlessness from Śiva and conquered the worlds. Vakratuṇḍa came on his lion; the demon’s sons fell, and Matsara surrendered and was forgiven.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Vakratuṇḍa represents the form of Brahman as all bodies together.

2. EKADANTA

Conqueror of pride
Name meansOne-tusked
DemonMadāsura
FaultPride, intoxication
MountA mouse
MeaningBrahman’s essential nature: all souls together
OutcomeThe demon surrenders and is forgiven

ZAPPY: Did Ekadanta fight?

PROF. BOLT: Hardly. The demon lost his nerve at the sight of him. Pride often collapses when faced.

Madāsura, drunk on power, conquered the three worlds. At the sage Sanatkumāra’s advice the gods invoked Ekadanta, who came on his mouse; the demon lost courage, surrendered, and was forgiven.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Ekadanta represents brahman’s essential nature: all souls together.

3. MAHODARA

Conqueror of delusion
Name meansGreat-bellied
DemonMohāsura
FaultDelusion
MountA mouse
MeaningBrahman’s wisdom entering creation; Vakratuṇḍa and Ekadanta joined
OutcomeThe demon surrenders and is forgiven

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu helps Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: In this telling he advises the demon to surrender. The gods cooperate in the Mudgala.

Mohāsura, born of the gods’ own delusion, conquered the worlds after long penance. Mahodara came on his mouse; Viṣṇu advised the demon to surrender, and he became Mahodara’s devotee.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Mahodara represents brahman’s wisdom entering creation; Vakratuṇḍa and Ekadanta joined.

4. GAJĀNANA

Conqueror of greed
Name meansElephant-faced
DemonLobhāsura
FaultGreed
MountA mouse
MeaningThe counterpart of Mahodara
OutcomeThe demon is subdued

ZAPPY: Greed sent underground?

PROF. BOLT: Not destroyed, but put in its place. Greed is useful in small doses, the story might say.

Lobhāsura, the demon of greed, seized the worlds. Gajānana subdued him and sent him to the underworld, restoring balance.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Gajānana represents the counterpart of Mahodara.

5. LAMBODARA

Conqueror of anger
Name meansPot-bellied
DemonKrodhāsura
FaultAnger
MountA mouse
MeaningŚakti, Brahman’s pure power
OutcomeThe demon is subdued

ZAPPY: A belly big enough for anger?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the picture. Patience is a big belly: it can take in anger without bursting.

Krodhāsura, anger itself, rampaged through creation. Lambodara’s belly, the story says, was large enough to hold even that anger; the demon was subdued and peace returned.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Lambodara represents śakti, Brahman’s pure power.

6. VIKAṬA

Conqueror of desire
Name meansOf unusual form
DemonKāmāsura
FaultDesire, lust
MountA peacock
MeaningSūrya, Brahman’s illuminating nature
OutcomeThe demon surrenders and is forgiven

ZAPPY: A peacock this time?

PROF. BOLT: Vikaṭa rides a peacock, Skanda’s bird. Desire is colourful; so is the mount.

Kāmāsura, desire, conquered the worlds. Vikaṭa came riding a peacock; the demon repented and was forgiven.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Vikaṭa represents sūrya, Brahman’s illuminating nature.

7. VIGHNARĀJA

Conqueror of possessiveness
Name meansKing of obstacles
DemonMamāsura
FaultPossessiveness, “mine-ness”
MountThe serpent Śeṣa
MeaningViṣṇu, Brahman’s preserving nature
OutcomeThe demon is subdued

ZAPPY: What is ‘possessiveness’?

PROF. BOLT: Mamatā: the sense of ‘mine’. Vighnarāja rides the serpent who supports Viṣṇu.

Mamāsura, the demon of “mine”, was subdued by Vighnarāja, who rides the celestial serpent Śeṣa.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Vighnarāja represents viṣṇu, Brahman’s preserving nature.

8. DHŪMRAVARṆA

Conqueror of ego
Name meansSmoke-coloured
DemonAbhimānāsura (Ahaṅkārāsura)
FaultEgo, conceit
MountA blue horse
MeaningŚiva, Brahman’s dissolving nature
OutcomeThe demon is subdued

ZAPPY: The last one helps Kalki?

PROF. BOLT: That’s what the Mudgala says: ego is the last enemy, defeated at the end of the age.

Dhūmravarṇa, a fierce smoke-coloured form on a blue horse, conquers the demon of ego. The text says he will help Kalki begin the new great age.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa’s philosophy, Dhūmravarṇa represents śiva, Brahman’s dissolving nature.

THE PATTERN

How the eight stories work
BirthA fault is born, often from a god’s own lapse
BoonThe demon wins power by penance
ConquestHe seizes the three worlds
PrayerThe gods turn to Gaṇeśa
EndingSurrender, forgiveness, or banishment
LessonFaults are not killed; they are tamed and put in their place

ZAPPY: Why are the demons forgiven?

PROF. BOLT: Because faults can’t be destroyed, only mastered. Even anger has its place, once tamed.

The eight stories follow a pattern. A fault is born, often from a lapse of the gods themselves; it wins power by penance; it conquers the three worlds; the gods pray to Gaṇeśa; and the avatāra comes.

Often the demon surrenders and is forgiven, or is sent to the underworld rather than destroyed. Faults, the stories suggest, cannot be killed; they can be mastered.

THE PHILOSOPHY

Brahman unfolding
VakratuṇḍaBrahman as all bodies
EkadantaBrahman as all souls
Mahodara, GajānanaBrahman’s wisdom entering creation
Lambodara, VikaṭaŚakti and Sūrya
Vighnarāja, DhūmravarṇaViṣṇu and Śiva
SoThe other gods are stages of Gaṇeśa, in this Gāṇapatya view

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa contains Viṣṇu and Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: In the Mudgala Purāṇa, yes. That’s the Gāṇapatya view; other traditions see it their own way.

Granoff’s summary gives each avatāra a philosophical meaning: the first two are Brahman as all bodies and as all souls; Mahodara and Gajānana, Brahman’s wisdom entering creation; Lambodara and Vikaṭa correspond to Śakti and Sūrya; Vighnarāja and Dhūmravarṇa to Viṣṇu and Śiva.

In this Gāṇapatya view, the other great gods are stages of Gaṇeśa. Other traditions see the relationship differently; this site does not choose between them.

WHAT THE AVATĀRAS TEACH

For the reader
Know your faultsEnvy, pride, delusion, greed, anger, desire, possessiveness, ego
Name themNaming a fault is half the battle
Tame, don’t hateThe demons are forgiven
Ego lastThe hardest, at the end of the age
PrayEach avatāra for the fault you face
Same namesThese names are also among his twelve and sixteen

ZAPPY: Which avatāra do I need?

PROF. BOLT: Look at your worst day this week. The fault will tell you the avatāra.

The eight avatāras give a practical map of the mind’s faults: envy, pride, delusion, greed, anger, desire, possessiveness and ego. Naming them is the first step to taming them.

And the stories counsel taming over hatred: the demons are forgiven once they surrender.
