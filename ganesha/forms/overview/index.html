THE 32 FORMS

One Gaṇeśa, thirty-two faces

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Devotees speak of thirty-two forms of Gaṇapati: a child with fruit, a warrior with weapons, a dancer, a yogi, five heads on a lion, a lion’s face with a trunk.

The best-known pictures of them were painted for the Mysuru palace in the Śrītattvanidhi, and all thirty-two are carved at Nanjangūḍu. This overview introduces the set.

ZAPPY: Thirty-two Gaṇeśas?

PROF. BOLT: One Gaṇeśa, Zappy, seen thirty-two ways. Each form is a face for a need.

Devotional literature often speaks of thirty-two forms of Gaṇapati, each with its own name, colour, number of arms and objects. The Mudgala Purāṇa is the first text to list them.

Their best-known descriptions and pictures come from the Śrītattvanidhi, an illustrated encyclopedia of deities made for the Mysuru court in the nineteenth century, and the 32 are carved on temple walls at Nanjangūḍu and Cāmarājanagara.

This overview introduces the set; four guides then take eight forms each, and two more cover his avatāras.

ZAPPY: A Mysuru book?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The best-known pictures of the 32 forms were painted in the Mysuru palace.

The list of thirty-two forms first appears in the Mudgala Purāṇa, the Gāṇapatya scripture. Each form has a short Sanskrit meditation verse, a dhyāna-śloka, listing its attributes.

The fullest descriptions are in the Śivanidhi section of the Śrītattvanidhi, where each form is painted and its verse written in Kannada script. The verses there are attributed to the Mudgala Purāṇa.

The Śrītattvanidhi was published by Kannada University, Hampi, in 1993.

ZAPPY: So the 32 forms came to the world through Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: Their most famous pictures did. A Mysuru king gave Gaṇeśa’s thirty-two faces to every Indian calendar printer.

The Śrītattvanidhi was made at the order of Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar III of Mysuru, who ruled from 1799 to 1868 and to whom some fifty works are ascribed. He had it illustrated by the miniaturists of his palace.

It brings together forms of Śiva, Viṣṇu, Skanda, Gaṇeśa, many goddesses, the nine planets and the guardians of the directions.

The thirty-two Gaṇapati paintings from it have been reproduced so widely that they shape how the forms are pictured across India today.

ZAPPY: Can I see them?

PROF. BOLT: At Nanjangūḍu, walk round the outer walls and count. All thirty-two are there.

See: Nanjangūḍu

The same king commissioned sculptures of all thirty-two forms on the temples at Nanjangūḍu, the Śrīkaṇṭheśvara temple south of Mysuru, and at the Cāmarājeśvara temple in Cāmarājanagara.

At Nanjangūḍu the forms are carved on the outer precincts, where visitors can walk round and identify each one. Few temples anywhere show the complete set.

Our Nanjangūḍu guide in the Around Mysuru series describes the temple itself.

ZAPPY: Why start with a child?

PROF. BOLT: Life starts with childhood. The list grows up as it goes.

The first eight forms are Bāla (the child), Taruṇa (the youth), Bhakti (lord of devotees), Vīra (the valiant), Śakti (the powerful), Dvija (the twice-born), Siddhi (the accomplished) and Ucchiṣṭa.

They move from childhood and youth to devotion and courage, then to power, learning and success. Śakti and Ucchiṣṭa are shown with a consort.

The next guide takes each of these eight in turn.

ZAPPY: Why are the first sixteen special?

PROF. BOLT: They became the popular set, the Ṣoḍaśa Gaṇapati. Ramachandra Rao notes that.

Forms nine to sixteen are Vighna, Kṣipra, Heramba, Lakṣmī, Mahā, Vijaya, Nṛtya and Ūrdhva. Heramba has five heads and rides a lion; Mahā is the great ten-armed form; Nṛtya dances.

Ramachandra Rao notes that the first sixteen forms are popularly worshipped together as the Ṣoḍaśa Gaṇapati, and that Mahāgaṇapati is especially widely worshipped. Heramba is popular in Nepal.

Dīkṣitar’s songs on sixteen Gaṇapatis around Tiruvārūr are a separate, temple-based set.

ZAPPY: Named after sounds?

PROF. BOLT: Two of them. Gaṇeśa and Oṃ are closely linked in devotion.

Forms seventeen to twenty-four are Ekākṣara, Vara, Tryakṣara, Kṣiprapraśāda, Haridrā, Ekadanta, Sṛṣṭi and Uddaṇḍa.

Two are named for sounds: Ekākṣara for a one-syllable seed mantra, which we do not give, and Tryakṣara for A-U-M, that is Oṃ. Haridrā, turmeric-yellow, was the focus of one Gāṇapatya branch.

Uddaṇḍa, with twelve arms, has the most arms in this group.

ZAPPY: A lion-faced Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: Siṃha Gaṇapati, with a trunk still. You’ll meet him in the last guide.

The last eight are Ṛṇamocana, Ḍhuṇḍhi, Dvimukha, Trimukha, Siṃha, Yoga, Durgā and Saṅkaṭahara.

They include forms with two and three faces, a lion-faced form, a meditating form and the “remover of troubles”, linked to the monthly Saṅkaṣṭī fast.

Ḍhuṇḍhi gives his name to Kāśī’s famous Ḍhuṇḍhirāja Gaṇeśa.

ZAPPY: Do the colours mean anything?

PROF. BOLT: Often: white for calm, red for energy, gold for success. But the old texts don’t always explain.

Red is the commonest colour, as it is for Gaṇeśa generally. Several forms are white or golden; two are blue; Haridrā is yellow; Heramba and Lakṣmī are fair; Dvimukha greenish-blue.

Colours carry associations, white for calm, red for energy, gold for success, but the verses seldom explain them.

Martin-Dubost notes that the colours in the verses do not always match the paintings.

ZAPPY: Why do some have a goddess and some don’t?

PROF. BOLT: Different traditions, different needs. The married forms stress power and prosperity.

Several forms show Gaṇeśa with a consort: Śakti, Ucchiṣṭa, Lakṣmī (with both Siddhi and Buddhi), Mahā, Ūrdhva, Vara, Uddaṇḍa, Ṛṇamocana and Saṅkaṭahara.

They express the god joined with his power. Several belong to tantric worship; in line with this series’ rule, we describe their forms without teaching rites.

In much of the south, where Gaṇeśa is usually a bachelor, these forms are less common in temples.

HOW TO RECOGNISE A FORM

A checklist
ColourRed, white, golden, blue, yellow?
ArmsFour, six, eight, ten, twelve?
HeadsOne, two, three, four, five, or a lion’s?
ObjectsFruit? Weapons? Book and rosary? Vīṇā?
Mount or seatMouse, lion, lotus?
CompanyAlone, with one consort, or two?

ZAPPY: Can I name a temple image with this?

PROF. BOLT: Often, yes. Use this checklist with the four form guides.

To recognise a form, look at its colour, the number of arms and heads, the objects in its hands, its mount or seat, and whether it has a consort.

With these clues and the four form guides, many temple images and calendar pictures can be identified.

Nanjangūḍu’s outer walls make a fine place to practise.

ZAPPY: So there are more than 32?

PROF. BOLT: Many more in history. The 32 are the classic list, thanks largely to Mysuru.

The scholar Paul Martin-Dubost points out that the Śrītattvanidhi is a recent South Indian text. Its list does not include the early two-armed images of the fourth century, nor the fourteen- and twenty-armed forms found in central India in the ninth and tenth centuries.

Other texts give other forms and other numbers.

The thirty-two are one classic set, the most popular today, rather than a complete catalogue.

ZAPPY: Which form should I like best?

PROF. BOLT: Whichever one you need. Start with Bāla and see who you meet on the way.

The forms show one god meeting many needs: learning, success, courage, protection, prosperity, release from debts and troubles.

They also run through a life, from the child Bāla to the meditating Yoga Gaṇapati.

And for Mysuru readers, they are a local treasure: the best-known pictures of them were painted in the Mysuru palace and carved at Nanjangūḍu.
