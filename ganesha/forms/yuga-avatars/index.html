THE FOUR YUGA AVATĀRAS

A Gaṇeśa for every age

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa gives Gaṇeśa one avatāra for each age: Mahotkaṭa on a lion, Mayūreśvara on a peacock, Gajānana on a mouse, and Dhūmraketu, still to come, on a horse.

This guide tells each story, links Mayūreśvara to Morgaon and Gajānana to the Gaṇeśa Gītā, and compares the Mudgala Purāṇa’s eight.

ZAPPY: Four ages, four Gaṇeśas?

PROF. BOLT: One for each yuga, Zappy. Like Viṣṇu’s avatāras, but Gāṇapatya.

The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa describes four avatāras of Gaṇeśa, one for each of the four ages of the world. Each has a different number of arms, mount and colour, and defeats a different demon.

Mayūreśvara of the Tretā age is the Gaṇeśa of Morgaon; Gajānana of the Dvāpara age teaches the Gaṇeśa Gītā; Dhūmraketu will come at the end of our own Kali age.

These four are distinct from the eight avatāras of the Mudgala Purāṇa in the previous guide.

THE GAṆEŚA PURĀṆA

The other Gāṇapatya Purāṇa
Upāsanā Khaṇḍa92 chapters on worship and theology
Krīḍā Khaṇḍa155 chapters of stories (also called Uttara Khaṇḍa)
YugasSatya ch. 1–72, Tretā 73–126, Dvāpara 127–137
Gaṇeśa GītāKrīḍā Khaṇḍa 138–148
FrameTold by Vyāsa, traced to the sages of Naimiṣa
DateCore 12th–13th c. (Thapan); 1100–1400 (Preston, Hazra); later dates proposed too

ZAPPY: Is it widely read?

PROF. BOLT: Very, from the 17th to 19th centuries. It was translated into Tamil in the 18th century as the Vināyaka Purāṇa.

The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa has two parts: the Upāsanā Khaṇḍa, 92 chapters on worship and theology, and the Krīḍā Khaṇḍa, 155 chapters of stories. In the Krīḍā Khaṇḍa, chapters 1–72 tell the Satya age, 73–126 the Tretā, and 127–137 the Dvāpara; chapters 138–148 are the Gaṇeśa Gītā.

Scholars date it variously: Thapan puts the core in the 12th–13th centuries, Preston and Hazra 1100–1400, others later. It was very popular from the 17th to 19th centuries, and was translated into Tamil in the 18th century as the Vināyaka Purāṇa.

With the Mudgala Purāṇa and the Atharvaśīrṣa, it is central to the Gāṇapatya tradition.

1. MAHOTKAṬA VINĀYAKA

The avatāra of the Satya age
AgeSatya (Kṛta) yuga
Arms10
MountA lion
ColourRed
BirthSon of Kaśyapa and Aditi
DefeatsDevāntaka and Narāntaka

ZAPPY: Ten arms and a lion?

PROF. BOLT: The biggest form, for the first and greatest age. A ten-armed Gaṇeśa in a golden age.

Two demon brothers, Devāntaka and Narāntaka, won powerful boons and terrorised the three worlds. Gaṇeśa was born as Mahotkaṭa, son of the sage Kaśyapa and Aditi, and defeated them.

Popular summaries add that he overcame them almost effortlessly. In the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa this yuga takes up chapters 1–72 of the Krīḍā Khaṇḍa.

2. MAYŪREŚVARA

The avatāra of the Tretā age
AgeTretā yuga
Arms6
MountA peacock
ColourWhite
BirthSon of Śiva and Pārvatī
DefeatsSindhu

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa on a peacock?

PROF. BOLT: In this age, yes. And it’s why Morgaon’s Gaṇeśa is Moreśvar, lord of the peacock.

The demon Sindhu won immortality, held as a pot of nectar in his belly, and tormented the worlds. Gaṇeśa was born to Śiva and Pārvatī as Mayūreśvara, rode a peacock, and drew the nectar from the demon’s belly, ending his power.

He is the Gaṇeśa of Morgaon, first of the Aṣṭavināyaka, whose name Moreśvar comes from the peacock (mayūra, Marathi mor). Popular tradition says he later gave the peacock to his brother Skanda.

3. GAJĀNANA

The avatāra of the Dvāpara age
AgeDvāpara yuga
Arms4
MountA mouse
ColourRed
BirthSon of Śiva and Pārvatī
DefeatsSindūrāsura

ZAPPY: Why is Gaṇeśa so often orange?

PROF. BOLT: One story says it’s from this battle: Sindūra’s red. Devotees still smear sindūr on him.

In the Dvāpara age Gaṇeśa came as Gajānana, four-armed on his mouse, to defeat the demon Sindūra. Popular tradition says he smeared himself with the demon’s red, which is why sindūr is offered to him.

In this avatāra he teaches the Gaṇeśa Gītā to King Vareṇya, in chapters 138–148 of the Krīḍā Khaṇḍa. That Gītā has its own part in this series.

4. DHŪMRAKETU

The avatāra of the Kali age
AgeKali yuga
Arms2 (4 in some sources)
MountA horse
ColourSmoke-coloured
Birth—
DefeatsThe forces of chaos at the end of the age

ZAPPY: He hasn’t come yet?

PROF. BOLT: Not yet, in this telling. He’s the Gaṇeśa of the future.

In the Kali age, our own, Gaṇeśa will come as Dhūmraketu, smoke-coloured, two-armed, riding a horse, to destroy the demons and lawless armies of the age’s end.

The idea echoes Kalki, Viṣṇu’s future avatāra on a white horse. Sources differ on his number of arms.

FEWER ARMS EACH AGE

A pattern
SatyaTen arms
TretāSix arms
DvāparaFour arms
KaliTwo arms
MeaningAs dharma declines, the avatāra grows simpler
EchoDharma stands on four legs in Satya and one in Kali, in Purāṇic imagery

ZAPPY: Why fewer arms?

PROF. BOLT: The texts don’t say. One reading: as the ages get harder, grace comes in plainer forms.

The arms decrease from ten to six to four to two as the ages pass. The Purāṇa does not explain this, but it fits the Purāṇic picture of dharma declining from age to age, standing on four legs in the first and one in the last.

One devotional reading is that grace comes in simpler forms as the times grow harder.

TWO SETS OF AVATĀRAS

Gaṇeśa Purāṇa and Mudgala Purāṇa
Gaṇeśa PurāṇaFour, one per yuga; demons of history and myth
Mudgala PurāṇaEight; demons that are faults of the mind
Shared nameGajānana appears in both lists
HorseBoth have a future-facing horse rider: Dhūmraketu, Dhūmravarṇa
InfluenceThe two Purāṇas may have influenced each other
Our viewTwo Gāṇapatya schemes, side by side

ZAPPY: Which list is right?

PROF. BOLT: Both are Gāṇapatya scripture. One counts ages, the other counts faults.

The two Gāṇapatya Purāṇas give different schemes. The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa counts ages, with one avatāra for each; the Mudgala counts faults of the mind, with eight.

Gajānana appears in both lists, and both end with a smoke-coloured horseman of the future. Scholars think the two texts may have influenced each other.

ZAPPY: Can I visit Mayūreśvara?

PROF. BOLT: At Morgaon, near Pune. He’s the first stop of the Aṣṭavināyaka.

Mayūreśvara is worshipped at Morgaon, the first of the Aṣṭavināyaka temples, which the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa counts among its most important places. Morgaon will have its own guide.

The orange sindūr on many Gaṇeśa images, especially in Maharashtra, is popularly linked to Gajānana’s victory over Sindūra.

WHAT THE AVATĀRAS TEACH

For the reader
Every ageGaṇeśa comes in every age
SimplicityGrace grows plainer as times grow harder
TeachingThe Gaṇeśa Gītā is given in the third age
HopeA protector still to come
SindūrA colour with a story
PlaceMorgaon roots the myth in Maharashtra

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Every age gets its Gaṇeśa, including ours.

The yuga avatāras promise that Gaṇeśa comes in every age, including our own, and that grace adapts to the times.

They also root Gāṇapatya myth in real places, above all Morgaon.
