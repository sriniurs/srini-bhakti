THE BIRTH STORIES

How he got his elephant head

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

How did Gaṇeśa get his elephant head? The best-known answer comes from the Śiva Purāṇa: a boy made by Pārvatī, a door, a battle, and a head brought from the north.

But there are other answers too: Śani’s glance, a child of two mothers, a boy born of Śiva’s laughter. This guide tells them all.

Śrī Gaṇeśāya namaḥ,

AT A GLANCE

How the elephant-headed god was born

Best knownŚiva Purāṇa: Pārvatī makes a boy to guard her door
BeheadingBy Śiva in the Śiva Purāṇa; by Śani’s glance in the Brahmavaivarta
Elephant headThe first head found facing north, in the best-known telling
ResultRevived, honoured, and made lord of the gaṇas
PromiseTo be worshipped first, before all gods
Our wayWe tell each version and name its source

ZAPPY: Which story is the true one?

PROF. BOLT: All of them are tradition, Zappy. Each tells you something different about him.

Several Purāṇas tell the story of Gaṇeśa’s birth, and they do not agree. In the best known, from the Śiva Purāṇa, Pārvatī makes a boy to guard her door, Śiva beheads him, and he is brought back to life with an elephant’s head.

In the Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, the baby’s head falls at the glance of Śani. In others he is born elephant-headed from the start, or from Śiva’s laughter.

This guide tells each version and names its source. Read together, they show how different schools saw the same god.

PĀRVATĪ’S WISH

Śiva Purāṇa, Rudra Saṃhitā, Kumāra Khaṇḍa
SettingKailāsa, where Śiva’s gaṇas guard every door
ProblemThey obey Śiva, not Pārvatī; he walks in when he likes
Her friendsJayā and Vijayā suggest she have an attendant of her own
Her wishSomeone who is hers alone
Her bathShe is about to bathe
Her actShe shapes a boy from the paste on her skin

ZAPPY: Why didn’t she just lock the door?

PROF. BOLT: Because the guards were Śiva’s. She wanted one who’d listen only to her.

The Śiva Purāṇa’s version is set on Kailāsa. Śiva’s gaṇas guard every door, but they are Śiva’s, not Pārvatī’s, and Śiva comes and goes as he pleases.

Her friends Jayā and Vijayā suggest that she should have an attendant of her own. When she is about to bathe, Pārvatī decides to act.

She takes the fragrant paste from her skin and shapes it into a boy.

THE BOY AT THE DOOR

Śiva Purāṇa
Made fromThe fragrant paste (ubtan) she had rubbed on her body
Brought to lifeHandsome, strong, and devoted to her
Her commandLet no one in while I bathe
His weaponA staff
Śiva arrivesThe boy does not know him, and blocks the way
WordsŚiva sends his gaṇas to talk, then to fight

ZAPPY: He blocked Śiva himself?

PROF. BOLT: He didn’t know who Śiva was, and he’d promised his mother. He kept his word.

The boy is brought to life, handsome and strong, devoted entirely to his mother. She gives him a staff and one command: let no one in while I bathe.

When Śiva arrives, the boy does not know who he is and refuses to let him pass. Śiva sends his gaṇas to argue, and then to fight.

The story makes his loyalty the centre: he is only doing what his mother asked.

THE BATTLE

Śiva Purāṇa

GodsThe gods join in, and are beaten too

ZAPPY: All the gods couldn’t beat one boy?

PROF. BOLT: He was made from the Goddess herself. That’s the point of the story.

The boy defeats Śiva’s gaṇas, and then the gods who come to help them. Some tellings add that Skanda and Viṣṇu join in; Pārvatī sends two powers of her own to aid her son.

At last Śiva himself cuts off the boy’s head with his trident. The fighting stops.

That one boy could hold off the gods is the story’s way of saying he was made of the Goddess’s own power.

PĀRVATĪ’S ANGER

Śiva Purāṇa

FuryShe brings forth fierce goddesses who begin to destroy the worlds
Gods afraidNārada and the sages beg her to stop

AgreedŚiva and the gods accept
LessonThe Goddess’s anger is a mother’s grief

ZAPPY: Did she really nearly end the world?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, yes. Don’t upset a mother.

When Pārvatī learns that her son has been killed, her grief turns to fury. She brings forth fierce goddesses who begin to destroy the worlds.

The gods are terrified. Nārada and the sages go to her and beg her to stop. Her terms are simple: bring my son back to life, and give him an honoured place among you.

Śiva and the gods agree.

THE ELEPHANT HEAD

Śiva Purāṇa and popular telling
CommandBring the head of the first creature you find facing north
FoundAn elephant with a single tusk, in many tellings
JoinedThe head is placed on the boy’s body
RevivedŚiva sprinkles water and he rises
Why northNorth is Śiva’s direction, Kailāsa’s direction
VariantSome tellings name the elephant as Gajāsura

ZAPPY: Why an elephant?

PROF. BOLT: The story says it was the first head facing north. Devotees add: the wisest, strongest, gentlest of animals.

Śiva sends his gaṇas north with orders to bring the head of the first creature they find. In the best-known telling they find an elephant, and bring back its head.

The head is placed on the boy’s body, and Śiva brings him back to life. North is Śiva’s own direction, the direction of Kailāsa.

Some retellings identify the elephant as Gajāsura, a demon who had asked to be remembered; this is a popular variant rather than the Śiva Purāṇa’s main account.

THE HONOURS

ZAPPY: So he got the job of the guards he beat?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The boy at the door became the god at every door.

Śiva accepts the boy as his son, and makes him lord of the gaṇas, the very attendants he had defeated. He is to be worshipped before all other gods, and is made lord of obstacles.

The fourth lunar day is made especially his; the gods each bless him in turn.

The boy who guarded one door became the god who guards every door.

THE GLANCE OF ŚANI

Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa

BirthKṛṣṇa himself is born as her child
GuestsAll the gods come to see the baby
ŚaniCursed so that his gaze destroys, he looks away
Pārvatī insistsHe glances, and the baby’s head falls
RemedyViṣṇu brings back an elephant’s head from a river bank

ZAPPY: Why did Pārvatī insist?

PROF. BOLT: She was proud and happy, and wanted everyone to look. The story is gentle with her.

The Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, a Vaiṣṇava text, tells it differently. Pārvatī keeps the Puṇyaka vow to Viṣṇu, here Kṛṣṇa, for a son, and Kṛṣṇa himself is born as her child.

All the gods come to see the baby. Śani, whose gaze is cursed to destroy, keeps his eyes lowered; Pārvatī, proud of her child, insists that he look. He glances, and the baby’s head falls.

Viṣṇu rides out on Garuḍa and returns with an elephant’s head from a river bank, and the child is restored.

TWO MOTHERS

Matsya and Padma Purāṇas
MadePārvatī shapes an elephant-headed figure from her unguent
WaterShe places it in the Gaṅgā
GrowsIt comes alive and grows huge
Two mothersPārvatī and Gaṅgā both call him son
NameDvaimātura, “of two mothers”
LexiconDvaimātura is one of his names in the Amarakośa

ZAPPY: Born with the elephant head?

PROF. BOLT: In this version, yes. No beheading at all.

In the version summarised from the Matsya and Padma Purāṇas, Pārvatī shapes an elephant-headed figure from her unguent and places it in the Gaṅgā. It comes alive and grows to a great size.

Both Pārvatī and Gaṅgā call him their son, so he is called Dvaimātura, “of two mothers”. The name appears among his synonyms in the Amarakośa, an early Sanskrit lexicon.

In this version there is no beheading at all.

BORN OF A LAUGH

Varāha Purāṇa
Gods’ requestA god to put obstacles in the way of wrongdoers
Śiva laughsA radiant boy is born from his laughter
Too beautifulPārvatī is uneasy at his beauty
Her curseAn elephant’s head and a big belly
Śiva’s blessingLord of the gaṇas and of obstacles
NameVināyaka

ZAPPY: A curse that turned into a blessing?

PROF. BOLT: That’s how most Gaṇeśa stories go. What looks like loss is the start of something.

The Varāha Purāṇa tells that the gods asked Śiva for a power to place obstacles in the way of wrongdoers. Śiva laughed, and from his laughter a radiant boy was born.

Pārvatī, uneasy at the boy’s beauty, cursed him to have an elephant’s head and a large belly. Śiva blessed him as lord of the gaṇas and of obstacles, and he was called Vināyaka.

As so often with Gaṇeśa, what looks like a misfortune becomes the shape of his blessing.

THE VERSIONS COMPARED

Why so many?
Different PurāṇasWritten in different times and schools
ŚaivaŚiva Purāṇa: the conflict and reconciliation with Śiva
VaiṣṇavaBrahmavaivarta: a gift of Viṣṇu (Kṛṣṇa)
Common threadPārvatī’s son, an elephant head, and a high honour
Most toldThe Śiva Purāṇa version, at Gaṇeśa Caturthī
Our viewRead them together; none cancels the others

ZAPPY: Doesn’t that make it confusing?

PROF. BOLT: Rich, not confusing. Each school saw him through its own god.

The Purāṇas were composed over many centuries by different schools. The Śaiva Śiva Purāṇa tells of conflict with Śiva and reconciliation; the Vaiṣṇava Brahmavaivarta makes him a gift of Kṛṣṇa.

The common thread is clear: he is Pārvatī’s son, he has an elephant’s head, and he receives a high honour among the gods.

The Śiva Purāṇa version is the one most often told, especially at Gaṇeśa Caturthī.

WHAT THE STORIES MEAN

Devotional readings
LoyaltyThe boy keeps his word even against Śiva
EgoOne reading: the small self is cut away; a wider head is given
WisdomThe elephant’s head as wisdom and patience
MotherPārvatī’s love restores him
ReconciliationConflict ends in a family made whole
Our noteThese are later interpretations, not the Purāṇas’ own words

ZAPPY: Is it about ego?

PROF. BOLT: Many teachers read it that way: the head that said ‘I’ is replaced with a head that holds the world.

Devotees and teachers have read many meanings into the stories. The boy’s loyalty to his mother is the plainest.

A popular reading sees the beheading as the cutting away of the small self, the ego, and the elephant’s head as a wider wisdom given in its place. Others focus on Pārvatī’s love, or on a family conflict ending in reconciliation.

These readings are later interpretations, not statements in the Purāṇas themselves, but they are part of how the stories live today.

THE STORY AT CATURTHĪ

How it is remembered
RecitedThe birth story is told during Gaṇeśa Caturthī worship
Clay imagesMade of earth, as Pārvatī made him
Gaurī firstIn Karnataka, Gaurī is welcomed the day before
ImmersionThe image returns to water at the end
EchoMade, brought to life, honoured, returned
ComingA full festival guide in this series

ZAPPY: Why clay images?

PROF. BOLT: Because he was made from earth by his mother. Every Caturthī repeats the first one.

At Gaṇeśa Caturthī, the birth story is recited as part of the worship. Images are made of clay, as Pārvatī made her son of the paste on her skin.

In Karnataka, Gaurī, a form of Pārvatī, is welcomed the day before Gaṇeśa. At the end of the festival, the clay image is returned to water.

The whole festival can be read as a re-enactment: made, brought to life, honoured and returned.
