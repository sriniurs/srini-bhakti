GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Elephant head, fan ears, curved trunk, one tusk, a big belly, four hands and a mouse: Gaṇeśa’s form is instantly recognisable, and almost every part of it carries a meaning.

This guide goes part by part, with the Sanskrit name for each feature, what the texts say, and the meanings tradition gives.

AT A GLANCE

Every part tells a story
HeadAn elephant’s, with one tusk broken
EarsLarge, like winnowing fans
BellyLarge: Lambodara, “pot-bellied”
ArmsUsually four: axe, noose, tusk, modaka
MarksA snake for a belt or thread; sometimes a moon or third eye
MountThe mouse
Our wayWe give the meanings tradition gives, and say they are interpretations

ZAPPY: Does every bit of him mean something?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees say so, Zappy. Let’s go part by part.

Gaṇeśa’s form is one of the most recognisable in the world, and almost every part of it has been given a meaning by devotees and teachers.

This guide goes part by part: head, ears, trunk, tusk, belly, hands, snake and moon, colour and posture, and the mouse. For each, it gives the name, what the old texts say, and the meanings tradition attaches.

Those meanings are interpretations, often modern ones. We give them as such.

THE ELEPHANT HEAD

Gajānana
NameGajānana, Gajavaktra: elephant-faced
OriginThe birth stories explain how he got it
ElephantStrong, wise, patient, gentle; clears paths through forests
SizeA large head for large thinking, devotees say
EyesSmall: fine focus
Earliest imagesAlready elephant-headed, from the start

ZAPPY: Why an elephant and not a lion?

PROF. BOLT: A lion conquers by force. An elephant walks through the forest and the path is simply there behind him.

Gajānana and Gajavaktra mean “elephant-faced”. The birth stories explain how he came to have an elephant’s head; the earliest images already show him so.

The elephant is strong, wise, patient and gentle. In the forest it simply walks, and a path opens behind it, a fitting image for a remover of obstacles.

Devotees often read the large head as large thinking and the small eyes as fine concentration.

THE EARS

Śūrpakarṇa
NameŚūrpakarṇa, “ears like winnowing fans”
Winnowing fanSeparates grain from chaff
MeaningHe hears everyone, and keeps only what matters
ListeningDevotees take it as a lesson: listen much, sift well
Meditation versesMany describe him with these ears
FanningSome say his ears fan away obstacles

ZAPPY: Big ears to hear prayers?

PROF. BOLT: And to sift them. Grain kept, chaff blown away.

Śūrpakarṇa means “one whose ears are like winnowing fans”. A winnowing fan separates grain from chaff, keeping what is useful and letting the rest blow away.

Devotees read this as a lesson: he hears every prayer, and keeps what matters. Some add that his flapping ears fan away obstacles.

The name appears in many meditation verses that describe his form.

THE TRUNK

Vakratuṇḍa
NameVakratuṇḍa, “curved trunk”
UsualTurned to his left, toward the sweet in his left hand
ValampuriTurned to his right: rare, thought powerful, needing strict worship
StraightRare; some read it as balance
MeaningCan lift a tree or pick up a needle: strength and delicacy
NoteThe left/right meanings are popular belief, not fixed scripture

ZAPPY: Does it matter which way it turns?

PROF. BOLT: Many families think so. Most home images turn left; right-turned ones are often kept in temples.

Vakratuṇḍa, “curved trunk”, is one of his best-known names and the first of the Mudgala Purāṇa’s eight avatāras. In most images the trunk turns to his left, toward the sweet in his left hand.

A trunk turned to his right, called Valampuri or Dakṣiṇābhimukhī, is considered rare and powerful, and is popularly said to require strict worship; such images are more often kept in temples than homes. A straight trunk is rarer still.

These meanings are popular belief rather than fixed scripture. The elephant’s trunk itself, able to uproot a tree or pick up a needle, is read as strength joined with delicacy.

THE BROKEN TUSK

Ekadanta
NameEkadanta, “one-tusked”, perhaps his earliest name
Early imagesOften show him holding the broken piece
Story 1He broke it to write the Mahābhārata when his pen failed
Story 2Paraśurāma’s axe struck it off (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa)
MeaningSacrifice for knowledge; one-pointedness
Mudgala PurāṇaEkadanta is the second of his eight avatāras

ZAPPY: Which story is right?

PROF. BOLT: Both are told. The Stories part tells them in full.

Coming: the tusk stories in Part B

Ekadanta, “one-tusked”, may be his earliest name, and some of the earliest images show him holding his broken tusk.

Two stories explain it. In one, he broke off his tusk to keep writing when his pen failed as Vyāsa dictated the Mahābhārata. In the other, told in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Paraśurāma’s axe, a gift from Śiva, struck it off when Gaṇeśa barred his way.

Devotees read the broken tusk as sacrifice for the sake of knowledge. The Stories part of this series tells both tales in full.

THE BELLY

Lambodara
NameLambodara, “pot-bellied”; also Mahodara
Earliest featureA big belly marks his earliest Gupta-period images
Brahmāṇḍa PurāṇaAll universes, past, present, future, are inside him
DigestionHe takes in good and bad alike, and is untroubled
Snake beltTied round the belly in many images
StoryA feast, a fall, a laughing moon (Part B)

ZAPPY: So it’s not just the modakas?

PROF. BOLT: The modakas helped. But the old texts say the whole universe is in there.

Lambodara and Mahodara mean “pot-bellied”. The large belly marks his very earliest images, from the Gupta period.

The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa explains the name by saying that all universes, past, present and future, are inside him. Devotees add that he digests good and bad alike and remains untroubled.

A snake is often tied round the belly like a belt. A story of a feast, a fall and a laughing moon explains it, in the Stories part.

THE FOUR HANDS

What he holds
Axe (paraśu)Cuts attachment and obstacles
Noose (pāśa)Draws devotees close; binds what must be held
Goad (aṅkuśa)Sometimes in place of the axe; urges forward
Broken tuskSacrifice and learning
ModakaThe sweetness of knowledge; the reward
AbhayaIn a modern form, the lower right hand blesses: “do not fear”

ZAPPY: Why a weapon and a sweet together?

PROF. BOLT: Cut the knots, then taste the sweet. That’s the order.

In the usual four-armed form, his hands hold an axe (or a goad), a noose, his broken tusk and a modaka. In a modern variant the lower right hand is raised in the abhaya gesture, “do not fear”.

Tradition reads the axe as cutting attachment and obstacles, the noose as drawing devotees close, the goad as urging forward, the tusk as sacrifice, and the sweet as the reward of knowledge.

Some images show a bowl of sweets rather than a single modaka.

HOW MANY ARMS?

From two to sixteen
EarliestTwo arms
UsualFour
Larger formsEight, ten, twelve and more in later images
Fourteen, twentySeen in central India in the 9th–10th centuries
HeadsSome forms have five heads (Heramba)
ComingThe 32 forms, with their arms and colours, in Part C

ZAPPY: Which is the ‘right’ number?

PROF. BOLT: Whichever the image in front of you has. Each form is a different face of him.

The earliest images show two arms. Four became standard; later forms have eight, ten, twelve or more, and images with fourteen or twenty arms appear in central India in the ninth and tenth centuries.

Some forms have more than one head, like the five-headed Heramba, popular in Nepal.

Part C of this series describes the 32 named forms, each with its own arms, objects and colour.

THE SNAKE AND THE MOON

Marks of Śiva’s son
SnakeRound his neck, as a sacred thread, or as a belt
Gaṇeśa PurāṇaNames the snake round his neck as Vāsuki
MoonA crescent on the forehead in some forms
Bhālacandra“Moon on the forehead”, a named form
Third eyeSometimes, like his father
TilakaOr the three lines of Śiva’s ash

ZAPPY: Why the moon on his head?

PROF. BOLT: Like father, like son. And there’s a story of the moon laughing at him: Part B.

A snake appears in many images: round his neck, as a sacred thread across the chest, or tied as a belt. The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa names the snake round his neck as Vāsuki.

A crescent moon on the forehead marks the form called Bhālacandra; the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa prescribes a tilaka and a crescent moon. Some images show a third eye, like Śiva’s, or Śiva’s three lines of ash.

These marks tie him visibly to his father.

COLOUR AND POSTURE

How he sits
ColourUsually red, or orange with sindūr
ExceptionsWhite for Heramba and Ṛṇamocana; blue for Ekadanta (in some lists)
SeatedOften one leg folded, one hanging: relaxed
StandingCommon in older temple images
DancingNṛtta Gaṇapati, the dancing form
SindūrMany temple images are coated in orange sindūr

ZAPPY: Why is he so often orange?

PROF. BOLT: Sindūr, the red-orange paste devotees smear on him, especially in Maharashtra and the north.

Gaṇeśa is usually red. Many temple images, especially in Maharashtra and the north, are coated in orange sindūr paste. Some forms have their own colours: white for Heramba and Ṛṇamocana, blue for Ekadanta in some lists.

He is often seated with one leg folded and one hanging, a relaxed posture. Older temple images often show him standing; the dancing form is called Nṛtta Gaṇapati.

Colour and posture are among the clues for identifying his forms.

THE MOUSE

Mūṣaka
Earliest imagesNo mount
StandardThe mouse, from about the 7th century in sculpture
TextsFirst in the Matsya Purāṇa, then the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa
ReadingsDesire held in check; selfishness overcome; pests subdued
Mudgala PurāṇaThe mouse is his mount in five of eight avatāras
OthersLion, peacock, the serpent Śeṣa

ZAPPY: Why would a mouse carry him?

PROF. BOLT: The small thing that gnaws everything, desire, is made to carry wisdom. That’s one reading.

The earliest images have no mount. The mouse (or shrew) became his standard mount in central and western Indian sculpture from about the seventh century. It first appears in writing in the Matsya Purāṇa and later in the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa, the mouse is his mount in five of the eight avatāras; others ride a lion, a peacock or the serpent Śeṣa.

Interpretations vary: desire held in check, selfishness overcome, or pests and small obstacles subdued.

WHAT HIS FORM ASKS FOR

Offerings that match
ModakaFor the hand that holds it
DūrvāGrass for the elephant
Red flowersFor the red god; hibiscus is common
SindūrFor the orange skin
CoconutBroken at the start of a task
Twenty-oneTwenty-one modakas or dūrvā bunches, by custom

ZAPPY: Why grass for a god?

PROF. BOLT: He’s an elephant, Zappy. And dūrvā is humble and grows back however often it’s cut.

The offerings he receives match his form: modakas for the hand that holds them, dūrvā grass for the elephant, red flowers and sindūr for the red god.

Many households offer twenty-one modakas or twenty-one bunches of dūrvā by custom, and a coconut is broken before him at the start of a task.

Dūrvā is humble and grows back however often it is cut, which devotees take as a lesson of its own.

READING AN IMAGE

A checklist
Arms and objectsWhich four (or more)? Tusk, sweet, axe or goad, noose?
TrunkLeft, right or straight?
ConsortsAlone, or with Buddhi and Siddhi?
MountMouse, lion, peacock?
PostureSeated, standing, dancing?
Name itThese clues tell you which of the 32 forms it is

ZAPPY: Can I tell the forms apart myself?

PROF. BOLT: With this checklist and Part C, yes. Try it on the next image you see.

When you see an image of Gaṇeśa, a few questions tell you a great deal. How many arms, and what do they hold? Which way does the trunk turn? Is he alone or with consorts? What is his mount? Is he seated, standing or dancing?

The answers usually identify which named form he is. Part C of this series lists the 32 forms with these details.

It is a good exercise at any temple or museum.

WHAT HIS FORM TEACHES

For the reader
ListenBig ears, sifting
FocusSmall eyes
Strength and careThe trunk
Sacrifice for learningThe broken tusk
Contain it allThe belly
Our noteThese readings are devotional; enjoy them as such

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: He looks strange on purpose. Every odd part is a lesson waiting to be noticed.

Read together, his features make a picture of a teacher: big ears that listen and sift, small eyes that focus, a trunk both strong and delicate, a broken tusk given up for learning, a belly that holds the whole world.

These are devotional readings, and different teachers give different ones.

The form is strange on purpose. Every unusual part invites a question, and every question leads to a lesson.
