THE GĀṆAPATYA TRADITION

Those who hold Gaṇeśa supreme

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

For most Hindus Gaṇeśa is the god at the door. For the Gāṇapatyas, he is also the god at the centre: the supreme reality, with every other god as his form.

This guide describes their beliefs, six branches and scriptures, the saint Moraya Gosāvī and the Devs of Chinchwad, the Peshwas, and how Gāṇapatya ideas spread into all Hindu worship.

ZAPPY: Isn’t every Hindu a bit Gāṇapatya?

PROF. BOLT: Everyone worships him first, Zappy. Gāṇapatyas worship him above all.

Every Hindu worships Gaṇeśa first. The Gāṇapatyas go further: for them he is the supreme reality itself, and every other god is one of his forms.

The tradition probably emerged between the sixth and ninth centuries, peaked around the tenth, and later flourished in Maharashtra around the saint Moraya Gosāvī and the patronage of the Peshwas.

This guide describes what Gāṇapatyas believe, their branches and scriptures, their saint, and how their ideas spread into all Hindu worship.

WHAT THEY BELIEVE

ZAPPY: Even Śiva is his form?

PROF. BOLT: In Gāṇapatya texts, yes. Every tradition puts its own god at the top of the hill.

Gāṇapatyas worship Gaṇeśa as Parabrahman, the supreme reality, and as saguṇa Brahman, the supreme with form and qualities. They give him the title Parameśvara, usually reserved for Śiva.

In their texts, Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī and Sūrya are forms of Gaṇeśa. The Atharvaśīrṣa addresses him as creator, sustainer and destroyer of the world.

The tradition held that he is known through contemplation and that his grace is won through worship.

THE SIX DEITY TRADITIONS

Where they fit

GāṇapatyaGaṇeśa supreme

ZAPPY: Which is right?

PROF. BOLT: This site doesn’t choose. Each tradition is a road up the same hill, its followers would say.

Hindu tradition often speaks of five or six great deity traditions: Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta, Saura and Gāṇapatya, each holding its own deity supreme. The Smārta tradition, following Advaita, treats all five deities as forms of one Brahman and worships them together in the pañcāyatana.

The Gāṇapatyas were never as numerous as Śaivas or Vaiṣṇavas, but they belong in this list.

This site describes each tradition on its own terms and does not rank them.

ZAPPY: Why would Śaṅkara debate them?

PROF. BOLT: The Śaṅkaravijaya shows him debating every school of his day. The Gāṇapatyas were important enough to include.

Gaṇapati was worshipped within Śaivism from at least the fifth century. A distinct Gāṇapatya tradition probably appeared between the sixth and ninth centuries.

Ānandagiri’s Śaṅkaravijaya, a life of Śaṅkara, describes Śaṅkara debating the Gāṇapatyas and names six branches. The work’s own date is uncertain, placed anywhere from the tenth to the fourteenth century.

The tradition reached a peak around the tenth century.

ZAPPY: Six kinds of Gaṇeśa worshippers?

PROF. BOLT: Six streams, each devoted to one form. Several of these forms are among the 32 in Part C.

See: Ucchiṣṭa Gaṇapati · Mahāgaṇapati

The six branches named in the Śaṅkaravijaya each centred on one form of Gaṇapati: Mahāgaṇapati, Haridrā (“turmeric”) Gaṇapati, Ucchiṣṭa Gaṇapati, Navanīta (“butter”) Gaṇapati, Svarṇa (“golden”) Gaṇapati and Santāna (“offspring”) Gaṇapati.

Some streams, including the Ucchiṣṭa, were tantric, and some worshipped Gaṇapati together with his śakti. This series gives their names and character only, not their rites.

Mahāgaṇapati and Ucchiṣṭa Gaṇapati also have guides in our Śrīvidyā series.

ZAPPY: A Gītā for Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, closely modelled on the Bhagavad Gītā, with Gaṇeśa as teacher. It has its own part in this series.

The Gāṇapatya scriptures are the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, in two parts, the Upāsanā Khaṇḍa on worship and the Krīḍā Khaṇḍa on his deeds; the Mudgala Purāṇa, with its eight avatāras and the first list of the 32 forms; and the Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa.

The Gaṇeśa Gītā, within the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, has Gaṇeśa teaching King Vareṇya in a dialogue closely modelled on the Bhagavad Gītā. The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa also contains a Sahasranāma.

The Atharvaśīrṣa and the Gaṇeśa Gītā have their own parts later in this series.

ZAPPY: A tradition at the top of a rock?

PROF. BOLT: The Rock Fort at Tiruchi, with Gaṇeśa at the very summit. You climb hundreds of steps to reach him.

In the south, the tradition’s largest temple was the Ucchi Pillaiyār, at the summit of the Rock Fort in Tiruchirāppaḷḷi.

Tamil Nadu is full of Pillaiyār shrines, from great temples like the ancient rock-cut shrine at Pillaiyārpaṭṭi to small images under trees at crossroads. In the south he is usually a bachelor.

Gāṇapatya ideas shaped temple worship across South India. The Temples part of this series covers several of these sites.

MORAYA GOSĀVĪ

The saint of Morgaon
FamilyThe Bhaṭ family; named after Morgaon’s Gaṇeśa, Moreśvar
DevotionWalked regularly to worship at Morgaon
LegendOnce found the temple shut, and the locks fell open
ChinchwadSettled there when too old to travel; built a temple
Saṃjīvan samādhiEntered his tomb alive, by tradition
DatesDisputed: from the 13th–14th century to 1610–59

ZAPPY: Why does everyone shout ‘Moryā’?

PROF. BOLT: Because of him. His name and Gaṇeśa’s became one in the shout.

Moraya Gosāvī, of the Bhaṭ family, was named after Moreśvar, the Gaṇeśa of Morgaon. He worshipped there devotedly, walking the distance regularly; a legend says that once, arriving after the temple was shut, he found the locks fall open for him.

When he grew too old to travel, he settled at Chinchwad, where Gaṇeśa is said to have appeared to him, and built a temple. Tradition says he entered his tomb alive, in saṃjīvan samādhi.

His dates are much disputed, from the 13th–14th century to 1610–59. The cry “Gaṇapati Bāppā Moryā” keeps his name alive.

ZAPPY: A family of gods?

PROF. BOLT: A family revered as his living forms. The idea is old in India: the god present in the saint.

Moraya’s son Cintāmaṇi was revered as a living form of Gaṇeśa and given the title “Dev”, god. Six more Devs followed him in the line.

Tradition says Cintāmaṇi revealed his true form as Gaṇeśa to the Vārkarī poet-saint Tukārām. Cintāmaṇi built a shrine over his father’s tomb.

The samādhi and temple at Chinchwad still draw many devotees, especially on Moraya Gosāvī’s anniversary.

ZAPPY: Is this where the Aṣṭavināyaka come from?

PROF. BOLT: They’re older, but Moraya’s devotion and the Peshwas’ gifts made them famous.

Devotees still make an annual pilgrimage between Chinchwad and Morgaon, the route Moraya once walked, by way of Theur, whose Cintāmaṇi temple he often visited.

Morgaon is the first of the Aṣṭavināyaka, the eight Gaṇeśa temples around Pune, and both main Gāṇapatya scriptures praise it.

The Aṣṭavināyaka will each have a guide in the Temples part of this series.

ZAPPY: Did the kings make the tradition?

PROF. BOLT: They enriched it. The devotion was there first; their gifts made it shine.

In the 18th century the Peshwas, the Brahmin rulers of the Maratha state at Pune, worshipped Gaṇeśa as their family deity. They gave land and money to Gaṇeśa temples and built additions to them.

Mādhavrao I renovated the Cintāmaṇi temple at Theur. Around 250 temples were built in Pune in the Peshwa period, and most Aṣṭavināyaka temples took their present form then.

Their patronage turned a devotional tradition into a regional identity.

HOW GĀṆAPATYAS WERE KNOWN

Signs of the tradition

ChantThe Atharvaśīrṣa

ZAPPY: Brands?

PROF. BOLT: In the old days some sects marked their bodies. It’s rare now.

Gāṇapatyas were once recognised by a red circle on the forehead, and some groups bore brands of an elephant face and tusk on the shoulders.

Their practice centred on modakas, dūrvā and red flowers, on the fourth lunar day, and on the chanting of the Atharvaśīrṣa.

Today few outward marks remain; the tradition lives mostly in temple custom and household devotion.

Into everyone’s worship

GrimesThe tradition never had huge numbers, but its view shaped all-India practice
First worshipGaṇeśa’s place before every rite

ZAPPY: So it won by being absorbed?

PROF. BOLT: In a way. Everyone took Gaṇeśa in, and the tradition’s ideas went with him.

John Grimes notes that the Gāṇapatyas never matched the numbers of Śaivas or Vaiṣṇavas, but their view of Gaṇeśa as the supreme strongly influenced Smārta ritual and temple worship across India.

Gaṇeśa’s place before every rite, and the chanting of the Atharvaśīrṣa by people of every tradition, are part of that legacy. In Śrīvidyā, Mahāgaṇapati is worshipped first.

The tradition is small; its reach is enormous.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: For Gāṇapatyas, the doorkeeper is also the master of the house.

For Gāṇapatyas, the god at the door is also the god at the centre. That idea gives every Gaṇeśa shrine, however small, a depth beyond its size.

Their history shows how a saint’s love can make places holy, and how royal gifts and public festivals can carry that love to millions.

And it is a reminder that each Hindu tradition sees the highest through its own chosen form.

Saṃjīvan samādhi, “living samādhi”, is the tradition that a saint entered his tomb alive in meditation and remains there. Several Maharashtrian saints, including Jñāneśvara, are remembered in this way.
