GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

When did people start worshipping Gaṇeśa? This guide follows the historians’ story: a Vedic word, troublesome spirits, clay and stone images, the classic form of the Gupta age, the Purāṇas, a tradition of his own, and Maharashtra’s great festival.

Dates are debated, and we say so. Devotion and history sit side by side.

ZAPPY: Wasn’t he always there?

PROF. BOLT: For devotees, yes. For historians, he appears step by step. We’ll look at both, Zappy.

For a devotee, Gaṇeśa has always been there. For a historian, he appears step by step: a Vedic word, troublesome spirits in ritual manuals, clay figures, then stone images in their classic form by the fifth century, then the great Purāṇic stories and a tradition devoted to him alone.

This guide follows that history, giving the dates scholars propose and saying where they disagree.

History and devotion answer different questions. Neither cancels the other.

THE VEDIC WORD

Ṛg Veda 2.23.1
The verse“gaṇānāṃ tvā gaṇapatiṃ havāmahe”: “you, lord of the hosts, we invoke”
Addressed toBṛhaspati (Brahmaṇaspati), the gods’ priest
RV 10.112.9Uses the word for Indra
Later useThe verse is chanted today to invoke Gaṇeśa
YajurvedaVerses on a “tusked one” are often held to be later additions
ScholarsRocher, Krishan, Thapan, Dhavalikar on these questions

ZAPPY: So the Veda isn’t about him?

PROF. BOLT: The word is there; the elephant isn’t. Tradition later heard Gaṇeśa in it, and still does.

The Ṛg Veda (2.23.1) contains the famous words “gaṇānāṃ tvā gaṇapatiṃ havāmahe”, “you, lord of the hosts, we invoke”. In context the verse is addressed to Bṛhaspati, also called Brahmaṇaspati, the priest of the gods. Another verse (10.112.9) uses the word for Indra.

Later tradition took up the verse as an invocation of Gaṇeśa, and it is still chanted before his worship.

Some Yajurveda passages mention a “tusked one” or “curved trunk”. Scholars such as Krishan and Thapan regard these as later additions.

THE VINĀYAKAS

Obstacle-makers
Mānava GṛhyasūtraFour Vināyakas: spirits that cause trouble
EffectsBad dreams, failure, confusion
RemedyRites to appease them
DateUncertain; Thapan suggests the 3rd–2nd century BCE for this section
LaterThe four merge into one Vināyaka, made lord of the gaṇas
TheoryOne view of his origin, accepted by Krishan

ZAPPY: He started as a demon?

PROF. BOLT: One theory says the obstacle-spirits were tamed into one lord of obstacles. Others disagree.

The Mānava Gṛhyasūtra, a manual of household rites, describes four Vināyakas: spirits who cause bad dreams, failure and confusion, and must be appeased by ritual. Thapan dates this section to the third or second century BCE.

In later literature the four merge into one Vināyaka, whom Śiva makes lord of the gaṇas, and whose role is to place or remove obstacles.

Some scholars, including Krishan, see this as one root of Gaṇeśa. It remains a theory.

THE EPICS

ZAPPY: So he didn’t write the Mahābhārata?

PROF. BOLT: Not in the oldest manuscripts. But the story is old and loved, and it’s why writers pray to him.

Gaṇeśa plays no early role in the epics. The famous story of his writing down the Mahābhārata appears in only 37 of the 59 manuscripts consulted for the critical edition, is absent from southern manuscripts, and is left out of the critical text.

Scholars date the story to about the eighth or ninth century.

It has nonetheless shaped his image as patron of writers and learning, and is told in the Stories part of this series.

THE FIRST IMAGES

Clay and stone

CoinsAn elephant-headed figure on Indo-Greek coins: claimed, strongly contested
Early formTwo arms, a big belly, often holding a sweet

InscriptionA Shahi king consecrated a “Mahāvināyaka”

ZAPPY: Afghanistan?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Some of the earliest dated Gaṇeśas come from there.

Elephant-headed figures in terracotta appear from about the first century CE; stone images from Mathura date to the Kushan period, in the second and third centuries. Some scholars have claimed an elephant-headed figure on Indo-Greek coins as Gaṇeśa, but this is strongly contested.

Early images often show two arms and a large belly, with a sweet in one hand.

Some of the earliest dated images come from Afghanistan: from Kabul in the fourth century and from Gardez in the fifth, the latter with an inscription. An inscription records a Shahi king consecrating a “Mahāvināyaka”.

ZAPPY: Why so sudden?

PROF. BOLT: Perhaps older images were in wood or clay and didn’t survive. Getty suggested that in 1936.

The Udayagiri Gaṇeśa stands beside the Saptamātṛkās, the seven Mothers

By the fourth and fifth centuries Gaṇeśa appears in his classic form. One of the oldest is carved in Cave 6 at Udayagiri near Vidisha, beside the Mother goddesses; another is at Bhumara.

Dhavalikar dates real worship of Gaṇeśa to about 450–500 CE. Narain finds no convincing textual evidence before the fifth century.

Alice Getty, writing in 1936, noted how suddenly the classic form appears, and suggested earlier images may have been made in materials that did not survive.

THE PURĀṆAS

Stories take shape
WhenDetailed stories in texts of about 600–1300, says Brown
Earlier PurāṇasKrishan sees their Gaṇeśa passages as 7th–10th-century additions
Śiva PurāṇaThe birth at Pārvatī’s door
BrahmavaivartaA whole section, the Gaṇapati Khaṇḍa
BrahmāṇḍaLambodara, the tusk and Paraśurāma
ResultThe family, stories and names we know today

ZAPPY: So the stories came after the images?

PROF. BOLT: Mostly, yes. People loved him first; the stories grew round that love.

The detailed stories of his life are found in Purāṇic texts that Robert Brown dates to roughly 600–1300. Krishan argues that Gaṇeśa passages in some older Purāṇas were added in the seventh to tenth centuries.

The Śiva Purāṇa tells of his birth at Pārvatī’s door; the Brahmavaivarta has a whole section, the Gaṇapati Khaṇḍa; the Brahmāṇḍa explains Lambodara and the broken tusk.

In other words, devotion and images came first; the stories grew round them.

FIVE GODS

The pañcāyatana
WhatWorship of five deities together
The fiveŚiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, Sūrya and Gaṇeśa
Credited toŚaṅkara, 9th century, by tradition
WhoSmārta households
EffectGaṇeśa placed among the great gods
In practiceOne deity in the centre, four around

ZAPPY: Did Śaṅkara really start it?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says he popularised it. Historians date the system’s spread to about his time.

In the pañcāyatana, five deities are worshipped together: Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, Sūrya and Gaṇeśa, one at the centre and four around. Tradition credits Śaṅkara with popularising it in the ninth century, and it is typical of Smārta households.

Its effect was to place Gaṇeśa firmly among the great gods of Hinduism.

Many homes still keep a small set of five stones or images for this worship.

A TRADITION OF HIS OWN

The Gāṇapatyas
EmergedProbably between the 6th and 9th centuries
PeakAround the 10th century
TemplesIncluding the Ucchi Pillaiyār on the Rock Fort, Tiruchi
BeliefGaṇeśa as the supreme reality
TextsGaṇeśa and Mudgala Purāṇas
Next guideThe tradition in full

ZAPPY: Did they ignore Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: They honoured all the gods, but as forms of Gaṇeśa. The next guide tells their story.

A distinct Gāṇapatya tradition, holding Gaṇeśa supreme, probably emerged between the sixth and ninth centuries, and reached a peak around the tenth. Its largest temple was the Ucchi Pillaiyār on the Rock Fort at Tiruchirāppaḷḷi.

Its main texts are the Gaṇeśa and Mudgala Purāṇas.

The next guide is devoted to this tradition.

SahasranāmaVersions in the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa
Scholars disagreeWe give the range

ZAPPY: The Atharvaśīrṣa is that recent?

PROF. BOLT: As a text, probably. For devotees its authority doesn’t depend on its date.

The main Gaṇeśa texts are late. Thapan dates the core of the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa to the 12th–13th centuries; Preston suggests 1100–1400. Hazra thought the Mudgala Purāṇa older; Granoff argues it is later, and was still being added to in the 17th–18th centuries.

The Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa, the most chanted Gaṇeśa text, is probably from the 16th or 17th century. The Gaṇeśa Gītā and a Gaṇeśa Sahasranāma are found in the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa.

For devotees, the authority of these texts does not depend on their date.

ZAPPY: Why is Maharashtra so Gaṇeśa-mad?

PROF. BOLT: Saints, then kings, then Tilak. Three centuries of love stacked up.

In Maharashtra the Gāṇapatya saint Moraya Gosāvī worshipped at Morgaon and later settled at Chinchwad, where his tomb and temple still stand. His dates are much disputed, from the 13th–14th century (Krishan) to 1610–59 (Courtright and Feldhaus). His son Cintāmaṇi was revered as “Dev”, a living form of Gaṇeśa.

In the 18th century the Peshwas, rulers of the Maratha state at Pune, worshipped Gaṇeśa as their family deity and gave land and gifts to the Aṣṭavināyaka and other temples. Around 250 temples were built in Pune in their time.

This long patronage made Maharashtra the heartland of Gaṇeśa worship.

ImmersionHe set the practice of immersing public images on the tenth day

ZAPPY: A festival with a political start?

PROF. BOLT: Partly. It was also a way of gathering everyone, across caste, round one god.

Until the late 19th century, Gaṇeśa Caturthī was mainly a household festival. In 1893 Lokmanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak turned it into a public celebration in Pune, partly to bring people together in the national movement against British rule.

He established the practice of immersing public images on the tenth day. The festival is now Maharashtra’s most popular.

The Festivals part of this series tells this story in detail.

A GOD WHO TRAVELLED

Across Asia

TibetThirty texts in the canon concern him

ZAPPY: 531 in China?

PROF. BOLT: Earlier than many of his Indian temples. He moved fast.

Gaṇeśa travelled quickly across Asia. A stone image in northern China carries an inscription dated 531. Images of the seventh and eighth centuries survive in Indonesia, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam.

In Japan, the cult of Kangiten is recorded from 806. Thirty texts in the Tibetan Buddhist canon concern him. Jain images survive from about the ninth century.

Part H of this series will follow these journeys.

What scholars argue

Elephant cultsElephant reverence is old; worship of a god is later
Tribal, DravidianProposed; Courtright finds no evidence
UnresolvedNo consensus
Our viewWe report; we don’t decide

ZAPPY: Who’s right?

PROF. BOLT: Nobody knows for certain. And it doesn’t change what a devotee feels at Caturthī.

Scholars have proposed several origins: the tamed Vināyaka spirits; elephant-headed yakṣa figures, though Thapan doubts they can be identified with Gaṇapati; ancient reverence for elephants; or tribal or Dravidian roots, which Courtright dismisses for lack of evidence.

There is no consensus.

We report the theories without choosing between them.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: He wasn’t imposed from above. People loved him, and the love became history.

History shows a god whose popularity grew over two thousand years, carried by devotion more than by texts. In many cases images and worship came first, and texts followed.

It also shows how far he travelled, from Afghanistan to Japan.

A devotee can read this history with interest and still bow at the threshold. The two ways of knowing him sit side by side.
