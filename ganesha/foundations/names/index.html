From Gaṇapati to Pillaiyār

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Gaṇeśa, Gaṇapati, Vināyaka, Vighneśvara, Ekadanta, Lambodara, Pillaiyār, Bappā: Gaṇeśa has more names than almost any other god, and each one tells you something about him.

This guide sorts them, from an old Sanskrit dictionary and the lists devotees chant every day, to the names he goes by from Tamil Nadu to Japan.

ZAPPY: Why so many names?

PROF. BOLT: Each one shows a different side of him, Zappy. Call him by the one you need.

Gaṇeśa has more names than almost any other god, and each one shows a different side of him: his role, his form, his family, his stories.

This guide sorts them: the main names; names from his form and family; the lists of eight, twelve and sixteen that devotees know by heart; the eight avatāra names of the Mudgala Purāṇa; and the names he goes by across India and Asia.

The hymns built on these names come later in the series, verse by verse.

GAṆEŚA AND GAṆAPATI

Lord of the hosts
GaṇaGroup, host, multitude
Īśa, patiLord
LiteralLord of the gaṇas, Śiva’s attendants
WiderLord of all groups, of all categories of things
Ṛg Veda“Gaṇapati” appears in RV 2.23.1, but there it is a title of Bṛhaspati
ChantedThose Vedic words are still used to invoke Gaṇeśa

ZAPPY: So the Ṛg Veda names him?

PROF. BOLT: It uses the word ‘Gaṇapati’, but for Bṛhaspati. Later tradition took the verse for Gaṇeśa. The history guide explains.

Gaṇeśa and Gaṇapati both mean “lord of the gaṇas”: gaṇa means a group or host, īśa and pati mean lord. In the stories the gaṇas are Śiva’s attendants; some commentators widen it to all groups and categories of things.

The word gaṇapati appears in the Ṛg Veda (2.23.1), but there it is a title of Bṛhaspati, the priest of the gods. Later tradition took up the verse as an invocation of Gaṇeśa, and it is still chanted that way.

The history guide that follows explains how the word became the god.

VINĀYAKA AND VIGHNEŚVARA

Leader, and lord of obstacles
Vināyaka“Leader”, or “he who has no leader above him”
Common inThe Purāṇas and Buddhist Tantras
AṣṭavināyakaEight Vināyaka temples of Maharashtra
Vighneśvara, VighneśaLord of obstacles
VighnarājaKing of obstacles
VighnahartāRemover of obstacles

ZAPPY: Lord of obstacles or remover of them?

PROF. BOLT: Both. The one who owns the obstacles can move them.

Vināyaka is explained as “the leader”, or as “one who has no leader above him”. It is common in the Purāṇas and in Buddhist Tantric texts, and names the eight Aṣṭavināyaka temples of Maharashtra.

Vighneśvara and Vighneśa mean “lord of obstacles”; Vighnarāja, “king of obstacles”; Vighnahartā, “remover of obstacles”.

The double sense is deliberate: as owner of obstacles, he can place them or remove them.

THE AMARAKOŚA’S EIGHT

An old Sanskrit dictionary
AmarakośaA Sanskrit thesaurus of the early centuries CE
Eight namesListed together as synonyms for him
From formEkadanta, Lambodara, Gajānana
From roleVināyaka, Vighnarāja, Gaṇādhipa
From storyDvaimātura, “of two mothers”
HerambaVariously explained: protector, or “dear to the mother”

ZAPPY: A dictionary of gods?

PROF. BOLT: A thesaurus. If you wanted a word for Gaṇeśa in a poem, here were eight.

The Amarakośa, an early Sanskrit thesaurus, lists eight synonyms for Gaṇeśa: Vināyaka, Vighnarāja, Dvaimātura, Gaṇādhipa, Ekadanta, Heramba, Lambodara and Gajānana.

Three come from his form, three from his role, and one, Dvaimātura, from a birth story. Heramba is explained in different ways, as “protector” or as “dear to the mother”.

The list shows that by the time of the Amarakośa he was well enough known to need a set of poetic names.

ZAPPY: Aren’t these just descriptions?

PROF. BOLT: They are. But each one has a story and a meaning, as the form guide showed.

See: His form and symbols

Many of his names simply describe him. Gajānana and Gajavaktra mean elephant-faced; Ekadanta, one-tusked; Lambodara and Mahodara, pot-bellied; Vakratuṇḍa, curved-trunked; Śūrpakarṇa and Gajakarṇaka, with ears like winnowing fans or like an elephant’s.

Bhālacandra, “with the moon on his forehead”, names a feature of some forms.

Each of these features has a meaning and a story, explained in the guide on his form and symbols.

NAMES FROM HIS FAMILY

Whose son
GaurīputraSon of Gaurī (Pārvatī)
Umāsuta, PārvatīnandanaSon of Umā; joy of Pārvatī
ŚivasutaSon of Śiva
SkandapūrvajaBorn before Skanda, the elder brother
DvaimāturaOf two mothers
Siddhi-Buddhi-patiHusband of Siddhi and Buddhi

ZAPPY: So in this name he’s the elder?

PROF. BOLT: Skandapūrvaja makes him the elder, as in the north. In the south Murugan is often the elder.

Other names place him in his family. Gaurīputra, Umāsuta and Pārvatīnandana make him Pārvatī’s son; Śivasuta, Śiva’s. Skandapūrvaja, “born before Skanda”, makes him the elder brother, as in northern tradition.

Dvaimātura, “of two mothers”, comes from the story in which both Pārvatī and Gaṅgā called him son.

Where he is shown with consorts, he is called the husband of Siddhi and Buddhi.

THE TWELVE NAMES

Saṅkaṭanāśana Gaṇeśa Stotra
1–4Vakratuṇḍa, Ekadanta, Kṛṣṇapiṅgākṣa, Gajavaktra
5–8Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Vighnarājendra, Dhūmravarṇa
9–12Bhālacandra, Vināyaka, Gaṇapati, Gajānana
KṛṣṇapiṅgākṣaDark and tawny-eyed
WhenChanted at dawn, noon and dusk
SourceAscribed to the Nārada Purāṇa; not easily found in printed editions

ZAPPY: Is this a hymn?

PROF. BOLT: A short one, beginning ‘praṇamya śirasā devam’. It gets its own verse-by-verse guide in the Hymns part.

The Saṅkaṭanāśana Gaṇeśa Stotra, “the hymn that destroys troubles”, is built around twelve names: Vakratuṇḍa, Ekadanta, Kṛṣṇapiṅgākṣa, Gajavaktra, Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Vighnarājendra, Dhūmravarṇa, Bhālacandra, Vināyaka, Gaṇapati and Gajānana.

The hymn says that one who recites these names at dawn, noon and dusk is freed from the fear of obstacles.

It is popularly ascribed to the Nārada Purāṇa, though, as the sanskritdocuments.org edition notes, it is not readily found in the printed Purāṇa. It will have its own verse-by-verse guide in the Hymns part.

ZAPPY: Sumukha?

PROF. BOLT: ‘Lovely-faced’. Even with an elephant’s head, he’s beautiful to his devotees.

A popular verse lists sixteen names: Sumukha, Ekadanta, Kapila, Gajakarṇaka, Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Vighnanāśa, Vināyaka, Dhūmraketu, Gaṇādhyakṣa, Bhālacandra, Gajānana, Vakratuṇḍa, Śūrpakarṇa, Heramba and Skandapūrvaja.

It is traditionally recited at the beginning of study, at weddings, before journeys and at the start of any work.

Sumukha means “lovely-faced”; Kapila, “tawny”; Dhūmraketu, “smoke-bannered”. The verse is included with the daily ślokas in the Hymns part.

THE EIGHT AVATĀRA NAMES

Mudgala Purāṇa
VakratuṇḍaConquers jealousy (mātsarya)
EkadantaConquers pride (mada)
MahodaraConquers delusion (moha)
GajānanaConquers greed (lobha)
Lambodara, VikaṭaConquer anger (krodha) and desire (kāma)
Vighnarāja, DhūmravarṇaConquer possessiveness (mamatā) and ego (abhimāna)

ZAPPY: Each name defeats a fault?

PROF. BOLT: In the Mudgala Purāṇa, yes. The Forms part tells each avatāra’s story.

The Mudgala Purāṇa describes eight avatāras of Gaṇeśa, each named for a feature of his form and each defeating a demon who stands for a fault.

As commonly summarised: Vakratuṇḍa conquers jealousy; Ekadanta, pride; Mahodara, delusion; Gajānana, greed; Lambodara, anger; Vikaṭa, desire; Vighnarāja, possessiveness; and Dhūmravarṇa, ego.

The Forms part of this series tells each avatāra’s story.

ZAPPY: Why ‘Bappā Moryā’?

PROF. BOLT: Bappā is ‘father’. Moryā is linked to the Gāṇapatya saint Moraya Gosāvī. The Gāṇapatya guide tells his story.

In Tamil he is Pillaiyār, explained as “noble child” (some scholars suggest the root first meant a young elephant), and Vināyakar. In Kannada he is Gaṇapa, Gaṇapati or Vināyaka.

In Marathi he is Gaṇapati Bāppā, “father Gaṇapati”. The cry “Gaṇapati Bāppā Moryā” is traditionally linked to Moraya Gosāvī, the Gāṇapatya saint of Chinchwad.

In Nepal he is Binayak; across North India, Gaṇeśjī.

ZAPPY: Tshogs bdag?

PROF. BOLT: Tibetan for ‘lord of the hosts’: a straight translation of Gaṇapati.

Beyond India his names follow the languages. In Thailand he is Phra Phikanet, from Vighneśa; in Sri Lanka, Sinhalese Buddhists call him Gaṇa deviyo; in Burma, Maha Peinne.

Tibetan Buddhism calls him Tshogs bdag, “lord of the hosts”, a direct translation of Gaṇapati. In Japan he is Kangiten or Shōten.

Indonesia once printed his image on its 20,000-rupiah note.

ZAPPY: Bhāskararāya again?

PROF. BOLT: The same scholar who explained the Lalitā Sahasranāma. He wrote on Gaṇeśa’s thousand names too.

See: Bhāskararāya

For daily worship, many families use a list of 108 names, the Aṣṭottara-śata-nāmāvalī, offering a flower or a blade of dūrvā with each.

The Gaṇeśa Sahasranāma, a thousand names, is found in the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa. Bhāskararāya, the great Śrīvidyā commentator, wrote a commentary on it called the Khadyota, “the firefly”.

The 108 names will be covered in the Hymns part; the Sahasranāma needs a printed text before it can be done properly.

WHAT THE NAMES TEACH

FaultsEach avatāra name defeats a fault

PracticeTwelve names, three times a day, says the hymn

ZAPPY: Which name should I use?

PROF. BOLT: The one that comes to your lips. He answers to all of them.

His names show how many ways devotees have found to approach him. Even the oddest features, a pot belly, a single tusk, have become names of praise.

Some names place him in a family; some describe what he does; the Mudgala Purāṇa’s avatāra names each defeat a fault of the mind.

And every language has its own name for him, which says more about his nearness than any list.
