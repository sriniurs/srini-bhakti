WHO IS GAṆEŚA?

Lord of obstacles, lord of beginnings

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Before any pūjā, any journey, any new book or new house, millions of people first bow to Gaṇeśa, the elephant-headed son of Śiva and Pārvatī, lord of obstacles and of beginnings.

This first guide of the Gaṇeśa section gives the whole picture: his name, why he comes first, his family, his form, his mouse, his offerings, his festivals and his travels beyond India.

Śrī Gaṇeśāya namaḥ,

ZAPPY: Why is he always first?

PROF. BOLT: Because he’s the gatekeeper, Zappy. You don’t enter a house by climbing in the window.

Gaṇeśa is one of the most widely worshipped gods in India. He is the son of Śiva and Pārvatī, the elephant-headed lord of Śiva’s attendants, and above all the lord of obstacles, who is worshipped first, before any other god, at the start of anything.

He is easy to recognise: an elephant’s head with one tusk broken, a large belly, usually four arms holding an axe, a noose, his broken tusk and a sweet, and a mouse at his feet.

This guide is the first in a full section on Gaṇeśa. It gives an overview; later guides take each subject in depth.

THE NAME

Lord of the hosts
GaṇeśaGaṇa (host, group) + īśa (lord)
GaṇapatiThe same: gaṇa + pati, “lord of the group”
Which gaṇas?Śiva’s attendants; read by some as “all categories of things”
Vināyaka“The leader”, or “one with no leader above him”
VighneśvaraLord of obstacles
MoreA whole guide on his names comes in this section

ZAPPY: Lord of which group?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva’s gang of attendants, the gaṇas. Some read it bigger: lord of every group of things there is.

Gaṇeśa and Gaṇapati both mean “lord of the gaṇas”, the hosts or groups. In the stories, the gaṇas are Śiva’s attendants; some commentators read the word more widely, as all the categories of created things.

Vināyaka is another common name, “the leader”, and gives its name to the Aṣṭavināyaka temples of Maharashtra. Vighneśvara, “lord of obstacles”, names his chief role.

A separate guide in this section is devoted to his names, from the old Sanskrit lexicon to Tamil Pillaiyār.

StoryThe race round the world explains his place (Part B)

ZAPPY: Does he cause obstacles too?

PROF. BOLT: In the old texts, yes. He’s lord of obstacles both ways. Start humbly and he clears the road.

In nearly every Hindu ritual Gaṇeśa is worshipped first, and many people begin any new undertaking, a journey, a house, a business, a school year, with a prayer to him. Books and letters often open with “Śrī Gaṇeśāya namaḥ”.

He is lord of obstacles in both directions: he removes them for those who approach him sincerely, and in the older stories he could also place them in the way of the proud or careless.

The story of the race round the world, told in the Stories part, is the best-known explanation of why he comes first.

ZAPPY: Married or not?

PROF. BOLT: Depends where you stand. In Maharashtra, two wives; in Tamil Nadu, a bachelor under a tree. Both are tradition.

Gaṇeśa is the son of Śiva and Pārvatī. His brother Skanda, known in the south as Murugan, is counted as the elder in the north and the younger in the south.

His marital status depends on region. In northern and western India he is often shown with two consorts, Buddhi and Siddhi (or Riddhi), intellect and attainment; in much of the south he is a bachelor. The Śiva Purāṇa gives him two sons, Kṣema and Lābha; northern tradition calls them Śubha and Lābha.

A daughter, Santoṣī Mā, became popular through a 1975 Hindi film, but has no Purāṇic basis.

ZAPPY: Why an elephant head?

PROF. BOLT: There’s a story, several in fact. Part 2 of this section tells them.

His image is one of the most recognisable in the world. He has an elephant’s head with one tusk broken, a large belly, and usually four arms, though forms with two to sixteen arms exist.

In the standard four-armed form he holds an axe or goad, a noose, his broken tusk and a modaka, which he tastes with his trunk. He is usually coloured red; some forms are white or blue.

The guide on his form and symbols explains each feature and the meanings tradition gives them.

Other mountsA lion, a peacock, the serpent Śeṣa in some forms

SizeA big god on a small mount: the joke is the lesson

ZAPPY: How does a mouse carry an elephant?

PROF. BOLT: Very carefully. The point is that the mind’s little gnawing desires have to carry him, not run him.

His mount is a mouse or shrew, called mūṣaka or ākhu. The earliest images show no mount at all; the mouse became standard in sculpture from about the seventh century and appears in the Purāṇas.

Interpretations vary. Some read the mouse as desire or restlessness held in check; others as pests and small obstacles that he subdues. Some forms ride a lion, a peacock or the serpent Śeṣa instead.

The contrast between the huge god and the tiny mount is itself part of the charm, and part of the lesson.

WHAT HE LOVES

Offerings

ZAPPY: Why modakas?

PROF. BOLT: Sweet inside a simple shell. Devotees read it as the sweetness of knowledge, hidden till you open it.

His favourite offering is the modaka, a sweet dumpling of rice flour filled with coconut and jaggery. Laḍḍus are also offered, and many images show him holding a bowl of sweets.

Dūrvā grass, offered in small bunches, is especially dear to him, as are red flowers and red sandal paste. Tradition says tulasī is not offered to him.

A coconut broken before his image at the start of a task is one of the simplest and most common acts of devotion.

LORD OF BEGINNINGS AND LEARNING

What he presides over
BeginningsWeddings, house-warmings, journeys, school
LearningTeacher of letters; children start writing in his name
ScribeIn a later legend, he wrote down the Mahābhārata for Vyāsa
ArtsMusic and dance invoke him first
BuddhiIntellect; one of his consorts in name
WisdomIn Gāṇapatya texts, he is the supreme reality itself

ZAPPY: A god of exams?

PROF. BOLT: Students in many homes would say so. A Gaṇeśa on the desk has seen many a board exam.

Gaṇeśa is lord of beginnings: weddings, house-warmings, journeys and the first day of school all begin with him. Children are often taught to write their first letters in his name.

A well-known legend makes him the scribe who wrote down the Mahābhārata as Vyāsa dictated it. Scholars regard it as a later addition to the epic, but it has made him the patron of writers and students.

In the Gāṇapatya tradition he is much more than this: the supreme reality, of which all other gods are forms.

ZAPPY: Even Buddhists?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, from Tibet to Japan. He travelled further than most gods.

See: Mahāgaṇapati in Śrīvidyā

Gaṇeśa is worshipped in every Hindu tradition, Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta and Smārta. In the Smārta pañcāyatana, the worship of five deities traditionally credited to Śaṅkara, he is one of the five.

The Gāṇapatya tradition holds him supreme. Jains and Buddhists also adopted him; Jain images survive from about the ninth century.

In Śrīvidyā, Mahāgaṇapati is worshipped at the start of the Śrīcakra worship. His guide in our Śrīvidyā series describes that form.

ZAPPY: Why the fourth day?

PROF. BOLT: Caturthī, the fourth, is his day. The stories behind it come in the Stories part.

His main festival is Gaṇeśa Caturthī, on the fourth day of the bright half of Bhādrapada, in August or September. In Karnataka, Gaurī is honoured the day before, and the two are celebrated together as Gaurī–Gaṇeśa.

In 1893 Lokmanya Tilak turned the family festival into a large public celebration in Pune, partly as a way of gathering people against British rule, and established the immersion of public images.

Saṅkaṣṭī Caturthī, the fourth day after each full moon, and Gaṇeśa Jayantī in Māgha are also his. A festivals part in this series covers them in detail.

ZAPPY: He’s in Japan?

PROF. BOLT: As Kangiten, often a pair of embracing elephant-headed figures. A long way from Kailāsa.

Gaṇeśa travelled with Indian culture across Asia. Images dated to the seventh and eighth centuries survive in Indonesia, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam.

In Thailand he is a patron of arts and learning; Sinhalese Buddhists in Sri Lanka revere him as Gaṇa deviyo; Nepal knows the five-headed Heramba; Tibetan Buddhism includes him in its canon.

In Japan he is Kangiten, whose cult is recorded from the early ninth century. The last part of this series takes up these journeys.

WHAT HE TEACHES

For the reader
Begin humblyEvery big thing starts with a bow
ObstaclesAre teachers, and can be removed
Odd is fineA god with a broken tusk and a pot belly is loved everywhere
LearningIs sacred
SweetnessKnowledge is a modaka
NearHe sits at every door

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Bow first, then begin. That’s Gaṇeśa in two words.

Gaṇeśa teaches that every undertaking begins with humility: a bow at the threshold.

He teaches that obstacles are not only problems but teachers, and that they can be removed. And his odd, lovable form, a broken tusk, a pot belly, a mouse for a mount, says that perfection is not the point.

Above all he is near. He sits at every door, on every desk, under every tree.

Pañcāyatana means “five abodes”: the worship of five deities, Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, Sūrya and Gaṇeśa, with one chosen as central and the other four around. It is characteristic of Smārta households.
