GAṆAPATI ATHARVAŚĪRṢA · PART 1 OF 3 · OPENING AND SECTIONS 1–5

HOW THIS WORKS: The opening and sections 1–5 in one go for chanting, then every section on two pages: the text, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive into the ideas behind it.

WHERE WE ARE

The Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa, also called the Gaṇapati Upaniṣad, is a short Sanskrit text that tradition attaches to the Atharvaveda. It declares Gaṇeśa to be Brahman, the one reality, and is the best-loved Gaṇeśa text of Maharashtra, chanted at every Caturthī and, by many families, every morning. Scholars date it late; Courtright and Thapan place it in the 16th or 17th century.

We give it in three parts: the opening peace-chant and sections 1–5 (Part 1); sections 6–10, with the meditation on his form (Part 2); and the fruits of recitation with the closing chant (Part 3). Editions number the sections differently; we follow sanskritdocuments.org.

Section 7 explains how Gaṇeśa’s seed-syllable is formed. In keeping with this site’s practice, we describe what it says but do not reproduce or teach it.

For who Gaṇeśa is, see our Foundations.

OPENING ŚĀNTI

MAY OUR EARS HEAR GOOD
oṃ bhadraṃ karṇebhiḥ śṛṇuyāma devāḥbhadraṃ paśyemākṣabhiryajatrāḥsthirairaṅgaistuṣṭuvāṃsastanūbhiḥvyaśema devahitaṃ yadāyuḥsvasti na indro vṛddhaśravāḥsvasti naḥ pūṣā viśvavedāḥsvasti nastārkṣyo ariṣṭanemiḥsvasti no bṛhaspatirdadhātuoṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ ‖śānti‖
O gods, may we hear with our ears what is good; O worshipful ones, may we see with our eyes what is good. With steady limbs and bodies, praising you, may we live out the span of life the gods have set. May Indra of wide fame bless us; may Pūṣan, who knows all, bless us; may Tārkṣya, whose wheel-rim is unbroken, bless us; may Bṛhaspati grant us well-being. Om, peace, peace, peace.

OPENING ŚĀNTI · DEEP DIVE

MAY OUR EARS HEAR GOOD

🔎 In context

The Atharvaśīrṣa, like many Upaniṣads attached to the Atharvaveda, opens with this peace-chant. Its verses come from the Ṛgveda (1.89.8 and 1.89.6), from a hymn to all the gods. Nothing in it names Gaṇeśa: it is the standard doorway through which a student enters any Atharvaveda Upaniṣad.

📜 Looking deeper

The prayer is modest and bodily. It asks for good things to hear and see, and for a full life in a healthy body, with the strength to keep praising. Then four gods are asked for svasti, well-being: Indra; Pūṣan, guardian of roads and travellers; Tārkṣya, usually taken as Garuḍa or a divine horse, whose wheel never breaks; and Bṛhaspati, lord of prayer. Some teachers point out that Bṛhaspati, also called Brahmaṇaspati, is the god the Ṛgveda calls gaṇapati in its famous verse 2.23.1, so the chant quietly leads into a Gaṇapati text.

🌱 For today

Before learning anything, ask for clear senses and a steady body. Study needs both.

SECTION 1

YOU ARE THAT
hariḥ oṃ namaste gaṇapatayetvameva pratyakṣaṃ tattvamasitvameva kevalaṃ kartā'sitvameva kevalaṃ dhartā'sitvameva kevalaṃ hartā'sitvameva sarvaṃ khalvidaṃ brahmāsitvaṃ sākṣādātmā'si nityam ‖1‖
Hari Om. Salutation to you, Gaṇapati. You alone are the visible form of “That thou art”. You alone are the maker, you alone the sustainer, you alone the destroyer. You alone are truly all this: Brahman. You are, plainly, the Self, for ever.
WordMeans

pratyakṣaṃ tattvamasi“That thou art”, made visible
kartā, dhartā, hartāmaker, sustainer, destroyer
sarvaṃ khalv idaṃ brahmaall this is truly Brahman
sākṣād ātmā nityamthe very Self, eternal
Gaṇapati is Brahman made visible.

SECTION 1 · DEEP DIVE

YOU ARE THAT

🔎 In context

This first section is the heart of the whole text. It takes two of the great sayings of the Upaniṣads, “tat tvam asi”, That thou art (Chāndogya 6.8.7), and “sarvaṃ khalv idaṃ brahma”, all this is Brahman (Chāndogya 3.14.1), and applies them to Gaṇeśa. He is not one god among many but the Absolute itself, seen.

📜 Looking deeper

Pratyakṣa means “before the eyes”, perceptible. The Upaniṣads teach that Brahman is beyond the senses; this text boldly says that in Gaṇeśa that truth has a face you can see. The three roles usually split between Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva are all given to him, with kevalam, “alone”, repeated each time. Scholars date the text late, perhaps to the 16th or 17th century, and its language deliberately borrows the authority of the old Upaniṣads; for the Gāṇapatya tradition it is the defining scripture.

🌱 For today

The form you love can be your door to the formless. You do not have to choose between them.

↑ SECTION 1 · Gaṇapati is Brahman made visible.

SECTION 2

I SPEAK TRUTH
ṛtaṃ vacmisatyaṃ vacmi ‖2‖

WordMeans

vacmiI speak

SECTION 2 · DEEP DIVE

I SPEAK TRUTH

🔎 In context

Two words, a solemn vow. The phrase echoes the Taittirīya Upaniṣad’s opening prayer, “ṛtaṃ vadiṣyāmi, satyaṃ vadiṣyāmi”, I will speak what is right, I will speak what is true. Having called Gaṇeśa Brahman, the speaker pauses to declare that he is not flattering: this is simply the truth.

📜 Looking deeper

Ṛta is the old Vedic word for cosmic order, the rightness by which the sun rises and seasons turn; satya is truth, what is. Some teachers read ṛta as truth in thought and satya as truth in speech and action. Either way the pair binds the reciter: if you say these words, you promise to mean them. It is the shortest section of the text and one of its most quoted.

🌱 For today

Say only what you mean. Then what you say has weight.

↑ SECTION 2 · Right and true.

SECTION 3

PROTECT ME
ava tvaṃ māmava vaktāramava śrotāramava dātāramava dhātāramavānūcānamava śiṣyamava paścāttātava purastātavottarāttātava dakṣiṇāttātava cordhvāttātavādharāttātsarvato māṃ pāhi pāhi samantāt ‖3‖
Protect me. Protect the one who speaks, protect the one who listens. Protect the one who gives and the one who upholds. Protect the learned reciter, protect the student. Protect me from behind, protect me in front; protect me from the north, protect me from the south; protect me from above, protect me from below. Protect, protect me from every side, all around.

SECTION 3 · DEEP DIVE

PROTECT ME

🔎 In context

Ava, “protect”, rings out thirteen times. First it covers people: the speaker and the listener, the one who gives the teaching and the one who holds it, the teacher who can recite and the student who learns. Then it covers space: the four directions, above and below.

📜 Looking deeper

This is the teacher-and-pupil setting of the old Upaniṣads, where a text was passed from mouth to ear. The prayer protects the chain of transmission itself, so that nothing breaks between the one who speaks and the one who hears. In the Vedic way of naming directions, paścāt, “behind”, is west and purastāt, “in front”, is east, for the reciter faces the sunrise. The section closes with pāhi pāhi, protect, protect: the only doubled word in the text, a cry rather than a formula.

🌱 For today

Pray for your teachers and your students too. Learning is a chain, and every link matters.

↑ SECTION 3 · Protect us on every side.

SECTION 4

MADE OF SPEECH AND BLISS
tvaṃ vāṅmayastvaṃ cinmayaḥtvamānandamayastvaṃ brahmamayaḥtvaṃ saccidānandādvitīyo'sitvaṃ pratyakṣaṃ brahmāsitvaṃ jñānamayo vijñānamayo'si ‖4‖
You are made of speech; you are made of consciousness. You are made of bliss; you are made of Brahman. You are being, consciousness and bliss, without a second. You are Brahman, visible. You are made of knowledge and of understanding.
WordMeans

SECTION 4 · DEEP DIVE

MADE OF SPEECH AND BLISS

🔎 In context

The fourth section is a string of -maya words, “made of”, “full of”. Speech, consciousness, bliss, knowledge: Gaṇeśa is the stuff of each. Vāṅmaya, made of speech, is especially apt for the god who wrote down the Mahābhārata and who is invoked at the start of every book.

📜 Looking deeper

Saccidānanda, being-consciousness-bliss, is the classic Vedānta description of Brahman, and advitīya, “without a second”, comes from the Chāndogya’s “ekam evādvitīyam”, one only, without a second. Jñāna and vijñāna are often paired: knowledge, and knowledge made one’s own by experience. The section repeats “you are visible Brahman” from section 1, keeping the text’s central claim before the reciter.

🌱 For today

Knowledge that is not lived is only half known.

↑ SECTION 4 · Speech, knowledge, bliss: all of it is him.

SECTION 5

THE WORLD COMES FROM YOU

All this world is born from you. All this world stands in you. All this world will dissolve in you. All this world returns to you. You are earth, water, fire, wind and sky. You are the four measures of speech.

SECTION 5 · DEEP DIVE

THE WORLD COMES FROM YOU

🔎 In context

Birth, standing, dissolution, return: the fifth section makes Gaṇeśa the origin and end of everything, in the language of the Taittirīya Upaniṣad, which defines Brahman as “that from which beings are born, by which they live, and into which they enter”. Then he is named as the five elements, the building blocks of the physical world.

📜 Looking deeper

The last phrase recalls a riddle of the Ṛgveda (1.164.45): speech has four measured quarters; the wise know them; three are hidden, and people speak only the fourth. Later grammarians and Tantric teachers named the four as parā, paśyantī, madhyamā and vaikharī, from the unspoken source to audible words; that naming is a later tradition, not in the Ṛgveda. For Gaṇeśa, lord of beginnings and of letters, the claim is natural: every word, from the silent thought to the spoken sound, is his.

🌱 For today

Everything you use, you borrowed from the world, and it will go back. Hold things lightly.

↑ SECTION 5 · Born from him, back to him.

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

Part 1 in five lines
Opening. May we hear and see what is good, and live a full life.
1. Gaṇapati is “That thou art” made visible: maker, keeper, destroyer, the Self.
2–3. I speak what is right and true; protect speaker, listener, teacher, student, on every side.
4. He is made of speech, consciousness, bliss: saccidānanda without a second.
5. The world is born from him and returns to him; he is the elements and every level of speech.
