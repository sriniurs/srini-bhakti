SIX SHORT PRAYERS FOR EVERY BEGINNING

HOW THIS WORKS: All the ślokas in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive.

WHERE WE ARE

Before anything begins, a lesson, a journey, a wedding, a pūjā, Indian families say a short verse to Gaṇeśa. These are the best known, the first Sanskrit many children ever learn.

Most are floating verses, anonymous ślokas passed from mouth to mouth and printed in every prayer-book; few have a reliable source, and we do not invent one. The twelve names are said to come from the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa.

For the longer hymns, see the Atharvaśīrṣa, the Pañcaratnam and the Saṅkaṭanāśana.

VAKRATUṆḌA

VAKRATUṆḌA MAHĀKĀYA
vakratuṇḍa mahākāya sūryakoṭisamaprabhanirvighnaṃ kuru me deva sarvakāryeṣu sarvadā ‖1‖
O curved-trunked, mighty-bodied one, radiant as ten million suns: O god, make all my work free of obstacles, always.
WordMeans
vakratuṇḍacurved trunk
mahākāyamighty body
sūryakoṭi-samaprabhabright as ten million suns
nirvighnaṃ kurumake free of obstacles
sarvakāryeṣu sarvadāin all my work, always
Clear the way, in everything, always.

VAKRATUṆḌA · DEEP DIVE

VAKRATUṆḌA MAHĀKĀYA

🔎 In context

This is probably the most widely known Sanskrit verse in India, the first prayer many children learn. It is said before exams, journeys, new jobs, weddings and the first page of a book. It is an anonymous floating verse; no reliable source names its author.

📜 Looking deeper

The first line describes; the second asks. Vakratuṇḍa, curved trunk, is also the first of the Mudgala Purāṇa’s eight avatāras and the first of the Saṅkaṭanāśana’s twelve names. Sūryakoṭi, ten million suns, is a stock image of brilliance. The request is precise: not success, but nirvighna, freedom from obstacles, in every task, sarvakāryeṣu, and at every time, sarvadā. The result is left to effort; the way is asked to be clear.

🌱 For today

Ask for a clear road, not a guaranteed result. The walking is yours.

↑ VAKRATUṆḌA · Clear the way, in everything, always.

ŚUKLĀMBARADHARAM

ŚUKLĀMBARADHARAM
śuklāmbaradharaṃ viṣṇuṃ śaśivarṇaṃ caturbhujamprasannavadanaṃ dhyāyet sarvavighnopaśāntaye ‖2‖
One should meditate on him who wears white, the all-pervading one, moon-coloured and four-armed, with a gracious face, for the calming of all obstacles.
WordMeans
śuklāmbaradharawearing white garments
viṣṇuall-pervading
śaśivarṇamoon-coloured
caturbhujafour-armed
prasannavadanawith a gracious, smiling face
sarvavighnopaśāntayefor the calming of all obstacles
White, moon-bright, smiling: obstacles calmed.

ŚUKLĀMBARADHARAM · DEEP DIVE

ŚUKLĀMBARADHARAM

🔎 In context

This verse opens nearly every ritual and recitation in South India, from the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma to a household pūjā. In South Indian practice it is addressed to Gaṇeśa, often with the reciter tapping the temples lightly with the knuckles.

📜 Looking deeper

Read literally, the verse could describe Viṣṇu: “viṣṇum” and four arms. In the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma tradition it is indeed recited as a meditation on Viṣṇu. Most reciters, though, take viṣṇu here in its root sense, “all-pervading”, and the verse as a dhyāna of Gaṇeśa, the remover of obstacles who must be remembered first. Both readings are traditional, and the verse is happy to bear both. Prasanna, gracious or clear, is the key: a calm, smiling face calms obstacles.

🌱 For today

Meet the day with a calm face. Half the obstacles shrink.

ZAPPY: Is it Viṣṇu or Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: Depends who you ask, Zappy. In South India, before almost anything, it’s Gaṇeśa.

↑ ŚUKLĀMBARADHARAM · White, moon-bright, smiling: obstacles calmed.

AGAJĀNANA

AGAJĀNANA PADMĀRKAM
agajānana padmārkaṃ gajānanamaharniśamanekadaṃ taṃ bhaktānāmekadantamupāsmahe ‖3‖
Sun to the lotus of the mountain-daughter’s face; elephant-faced; giver of many gifts to his devotees, day and night: the One-Tusked we worship.
WordMeans
agajāthe mountain’s daughter, Pārvatī
ānana-padma-arkasun to the lotus of her face
aharniśamday and night
anekadamgiver of many things
ekadantam upāsmahewe worship the One-Tusked
One tusk, many gifts.

AGAJĀNANA · DEEP DIVE

AGAJĀNANA PADMĀRKAM

🔎 In context

A small gem of wordplay. Agaja is the mountain-born, Pārvatī; her face (ānana) is a lotus (padma), and Gaṇeśa is the sun (arka) that makes it bloom: a mother brightening at the sight of her son. Then comes gajānana, elephant-faced, echoing agajānana.

📜 Looking deeper

The second line plays on numbers: aneka-da, “giving many”, against eka-danta, “one tusk”. He has one tusk but gives many gifts. Such puns are a favourite device of short ślokas meant for memorising: the sound helps the child remember, and the meaning rewards the adult who stops to look.

🌱 For today

One tusk, many gifts. Generosity has nothing to do with how much you have.

↑ AGAJĀNANA · One tusk, many gifts.

GAJĀNANAM

GAJĀNANAM BHŪTAGAṆĀDISEVITAM
gajānanaṃ bhūtagaṇādisevitaṃkapitthajambūphalasārabhakṣitamumāsutaṃ śokavināśakāraṇaṃnamāmi vighneśvarapādapaṅkajam ‖4‖
Elephant-faced, served by the bhūtas, gaṇas and others; who eats the best of wood-apple and rose-apple fruit; son of Umā, the cause of the end of sorrow: I bow to the lotus feet of Vighneśvara.
WordMeans
bhūtagaṇādi-sevitaserved by spirits, gaṇas and others
kapitthawood-apple
jambū-phalarose-apple, jamun
umāsutason of Umā
śokavināśa-kāraṇacause of the end of sorrow
vighneśvara-pāda-paṅkajathe lotus feet of the lord of obstacles
Wood-apple, jamun, and an end to sorrow.

GAJĀNANAM · DEEP DIVE

GAJĀNANAM BHŪTAGAṆĀDISEVITAM

🔎 In context

This verse is a favourite in South Indian homes and in Carnatic music. Gaṇeśa is pictured as a homely god: attended by Śiva’s wild bhūtas and gaṇas, and eating wood-apple and jamun, fruits of the Indian countryside.

📜 Looking deeper

Kapittha, the wood-apple, and jambū, the rose-apple or jamun, are among his favourite offerings; some read the line as eating “the essence” (sāra) of the fruit, others, in a variant, as eating them “prettily” (cāru). Umāsuta, son of Umā, ties him to his mother, and śoka-vināśa-kāraṇa, cause of the end of sorrow, says what he does for devotees. The verse ends at his feet, the usual place for a devotee’s bow.

🌱 For today

Simple fruit, a simple bow. The god of the countryside asks nothing elaborate.

↑ GAJĀNANAM · Wood-apple, jamun, and an end to sorrow.

MŪṢIKAVĀHANA

MŪṢIKAVĀHANA
mūṣikavāhana modakahastacāmarakarṇa vilambitasūtravāmanarūpa maheśvaraputravighnavināyaka pāda namaste ‖5‖
O you who ride the mouse, with a modaka in your hand; with ears like fans, the sacred thread hanging down; short of form, son of Maheśvara; O Vināyaka, remover of obstacles: salutation to your feet.
WordMeans
mūṣikavāhanarider of the mouse
modakahastamodaka in hand
cāmarakarṇaears like whisk-fans
vilambitasūtrawith the sacred thread hanging
vāmanarūpaof short stature
maheśvaraputrason of Maheśvara
Mouse, modaka, fan-ears: salutation.

MŪṢIKAVĀHANA · DEEP DIVE

MŪṢIKAVĀHANA

🔎 In context

This verse is all vocatives, one name after another, like a child calling out what it sees in a picture: the mouse, the modaka, the fan-like ears, the thread across the belly, the short figure. It is sung in many Gaṇeśa bhajans and taught in schools.

📜 Looking deeper

Its metre is different from the others, a light, dancing rhythm with words of four syllables. Cāmara is the yak-tail fan held over kings; his ears wave like it. Vāmanarūpa, “dwarf-shaped”, refers to his short, round figure, and echoes Viṣṇu’s dwarf avatāra. The vilambita sūtra, hanging thread, is the yajñopavīta, often shown as a snake in images. The verse ends “pāda namaste”, salutation to your feet.

🌱 For today

Look closely at what you love. Every detail can become a prayer.

↑ MŪṢIKAVĀHANA · Mouse, modaka, fan-ears: salutation.

Sumukha, Ekadanta, Kapila, Gajakarṇaka; Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Vighnanāśa, Gaṇādhipa; Dhūmraketu, Gaṇādhyakṣa, Bhālacandra, Gajānana.

TWELVE NAMES · DEEP DIVE

THE TWELVE NAMES

🔎 In context

A second list of twelve names, different from the Saṅkaṭanāśana’s. It is recited before beginning study, at weddings and at the start of pūjās. A longer form adds four more names, Vakratuṇḍa, Śūrpakarṇa, Heramba and Skandapūrvaja, to make sixteen, and says “ṣoḍaśaitāni”, these sixteen.

📜 Looking deeper

Many names overlap with the other lists: Ekadanta, Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Bhālacandra, Gajānana. New here are Sumukha, “fair-faced”; Kapila, tawny; Gajakarṇaka, elephant-eared; and Dhūmraketu, “smoke-bannered”, the name of his Kali-age avatāra in the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa. Some popular versions read vināyakaḥ for gaṇādhipaḥ in the second line. The sixteen-name form is said to come from the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa; we give the twelve-name form most often printed.

🌱 For today

Name what you rely on. It reminds you that you are not alone.

↑ TWELVE NAMES · Twelve names, one fair face.

PHALAŚRUTI

AT EVERY BEGINNING
dvādaśaitāni nāmāni yaḥ paṭhecchṛṇuyādapividyārambhe vivāhe ca praveśe nirgame tathāsaṅgrāme saṅkaṭe caiva vighnastasya na jāyate ‖7‖
Whoever reads or even hears these twelve names, at the start of study, at a wedding, on entering or leaving, in battle or in trouble: no obstacle arises for him.
WordMeans
yaḥ paṭhec chṛṇuyād apiwhoever reads, or even hears
vidyārambhabeginning of study
vivāhamarriage
praveśa, nirgamaentering, setting out
saṅgrāma, saṅkaṭabattle, crisis
vighnas tasya na jāyateno obstacle arises for him
At every threshold, no obstacle.

PHALAŚRUTI · DEEP DIVE

AT EVERY BEGINNING

🔎 In context

The phala-verse names the moments when Gaṇeśa is remembered: vidyārambha, the first lesson, still a ceremony on Vijayadaśamī in South India; vivāha, marriage, which begins with Gaṇeśa pūjā; praveśa, entering a new house; nirgama, setting out on a journey.

📜 Looking deeper

The last two, battle and crisis, widen the circle to any difficulty. “Śṛṇuyād api”, even hearing, is generous: you need not know Sanskrit to gain the blessing, only listen. That is why these names are recited aloud at ceremonies, so that everyone present hears them.

🌱 For today

Mark your beginnings. A moment’s pause at the threshold steadies the whole journey.

↑ PHALAŚRUTI · At every threshold, no obstacle.
