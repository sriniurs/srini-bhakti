HYMNS OF GAṆEŚA

GAṆAPATI IN THE VEDA

Gaṇānāṃ tvā · the oldest gaṇapati

ṚGVEDA 2.23.1 · ṚGVEDA 10.112.9 · MAHĀNĀRĀYAṆA UPANIṢAD

HOW THIS WORKS: The three verses in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive into what it first meant and how it is used.

WHERE WE ARE

The word gaṇapati, “lord of the hosts”, is as old as the Ṛgveda. Its most famous verse, gaṇānāṃ tvā gaṇapatiṃ havāmahe, opens countless Gaṇeśa pūjās today.

Scholars agree that the two Ṛgveda verses were addressed to Brahmaṇaspati and Indra, not to the elephant-headed god, who appears in texts and sculpture only many centuries later. A gāyatrī with the curved trunk and tusk appears in the Yajurveda tradition, though many think it a later addition.

We give all three, with what they first meant and how worship uses them. For the history, see Gaṇeśa in history.

ṚGVEDA 2.23.1 · DEEP DIVE

GAṆĀNĀṂ TVĀ

🔎 In context

This is Ṛgveda 2.23.1, the first verse of a hymn by the seer Gṛtsamada to Brahmaṇaspati, “lord of prayer”, also called Bṛhaspati. It contains the oldest use of the word gaṇapati. It is the verse with which countless Gaṇeśa pūjās begin, from homes to temples, and it is chanted at the start of Vedic recitation.

📜 Looking deeper

Did the Vedic poet mean Gaṇeśa? Scholars say no. In the hymn the gaṇapati is Brahmaṇaspati, lord of the hosts of priests or of sacred songs; the elephant-headed Gaṇeśa appears only centuries later. Ludo Rocher says it refers to Bṛhaspati and Bṛhaspati only, and notes that later Gāṇapatya writers quoted it to give Gaṇeśa Vedic standing. Tradition, meanwhile, simply uses it for Gaṇeśa: the words fit him well, the lord of gaṇas and the lord of speech and learning. Both are true at once: what the verse first meant, and what it has come to mean in worship.

🌱 For today

Old words can carry new devotion. Know both what they first meant and what they mean to you.

ṚGVEDA 10.112.9

SIT AMONG THE HOSTS

ṚGVEDA 10.112.9 · DEEP DIVE

SIT AMONG THE HOSTS

🔎 In context

Ṛgveda 10.112.9 is the second Vedic use of gaṇapati. Here the god addressed is Indra, “maghavan”, the bountiful, lord of the gaṇas of the Maruts, the storm-gods who ride with him. He is asked to sit among his troop and receive praise.

📜 Looking deeper

One line has become precious to Gaṇeśa’s devotees: “na ṛte tvat kriyate kiṃ cana”, nothing is done without you. It is exactly what later tradition says of Gaṇeśa, without whose blessing no work begins. In its own place the line praises Indra; in Gaṇeśa worship it is often quoted with RV 2.23.1. As with the first verse, scholars are clear that it is not about the elephant-headed god, and devotees are free to hear him in it.

🌱 For today

Nothing worthwhile is done alone. Count who sits with you.

MAHĀNĀRĀYAṆA

THE TUSKED ONE

WordMeans

Curved trunk, tusk: Gaṇeśa in the Veda’s margins.

MAHĀNĀRĀYAṆA · DEEP DIVE

THE TUSKED ONE

🔎 In context

This gāyatrī comes from the Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad, the tenth book of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka of the Black Yajurveda, in a series of gāyatrīs to different gods: Rudra, Gaṇeśa, Nandi, Garuḍa, Viṣṇu and others. A similar verse, with hastimukha, “elephant-faced”, is found in the Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā (2.9.1).

📜 Looking deeper

Here, at last, the words fit Gaṇeśa unmistakably: the curved trunk and the tusk. The 14th-century commentator Sāyaṇa identified the deity with Gaṇeśa, and the historian Heras accepted the identification. Many scholars, among them Thapan and Dhavalikar, consider these passages later additions to the Vedic texts. Compare the Gaṇeśa Gāyatrī of the Atharvaśīrṣa, “ekadantāya vidmahe”: the same shape, the same last line, with “one tusk” in place of “that Person”.

🌱 For today

Even the oldest-looking things have a history. Knowing it deepens, not weakens, the devotion.

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

Gaṇapati in the Veda in five lines
RV 2.23.1. “We call on you, lord of the hosts”: to Brahmaṇaspati, lord of prayer; now the opening of Gaṇeśa pūjā.
RV 10.112.9. “Nothing is done without you”: to Indra, lord of the storm-gods’ host.
Mahānārāyaṇa. “May the Tusked One inspire us”: curved trunk and tusk, Gaṇeśa unmistakable.
Scholars. The Ṛgveda verses are not about Gaṇeśa; the gāyatrī may be a later addition.
Worship. Tradition hears Gaṇeśa in all three, and chants them for him.
