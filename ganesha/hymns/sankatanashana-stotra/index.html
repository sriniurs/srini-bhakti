HYMNS OF GAṆEŚA

SAṄKAṬANĀŚANA STOTRA

Praṇamya śirasā devam · twelve names that end trouble

SAṄKAṬANĀŚANA GAṆEŚA STOTRAM · ASCRIBED TO THE NĀRADA PURĀṆA

HOW THIS WORKS: All eight verses in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive.

WHERE WE ARE

The Saṅkaṭanāśana Gaṇeśa Stotra, “the hymn that destroys trouble”, gives twelve names of Gaṇeśa and promises that whoever recites them at dawn, noon and dusk will lose all fear of obstacles. It is chanted daily in many homes, and especially on Saṅkaṣṭī Caturthī.

It is spoken by the sage Nārada and ascribed to the Nārada Purāṇa, but it is not easily found in printed editions of that Purāṇa; we treat the ascription as traditional.

For the names and their stories, see The Names of Gaṇeśa and the Mudgala avatāras.

VERSE 1

BOW TO GAURĪ’S SON
praṇamya śirasā devaṃ gaurīputraṃ vināyakambhaktāvāsaṃ smarennityamāyuṣkāmārthasiddhaye ‖1‖
Bowing the head to the god Vināyaka, son of Gaurī, the dwelling-place of his devotees, one should remember him every day for long life, for one’s wishes and for wealth.
WordMeans
praṇamya śirasābowing with the head
gaurīputrason of Gaurī (Pārvatī)
bhaktāvāsaabode of his devotees
smaren nityamone should remember daily
āyuṣ-kāma-artha-siddhayeto gain long life, wishes and wealth
Bow, then remember.

VERSE 1 · DEEP DIVE

BOW TO GAURĪ’S SON

🔎 In context

The Saṅkaṭanāśana Gaṇeśa Stotra, “the hymn that destroys trouble”, is one of the most chanted Gaṇeśa prayers in Maharashtra and beyond, recited daily in many homes and especially on Saṅkaṣṭī Caturthī. Printed copies open with “nārada uvāca”, Nārada said, and close by naming the Nārada Purāṇa; the hymn is not easily found in printed editions of that Purāṇa, so we treat the ascription as traditional.

📜 Looking deeper

The opening verse states the whole method: bow, then remember. Smaret, “one should remember”, is the key verb; the hymn is a tool for remembering Gaṇeśa by his names. Bhaktāvāsa, “where devotees dwell”, turns the usual idea round: not Gaṇeśa living in the devotee’s heart, but devotees living in him. The aims are frankly worldly: long life, wishes and wealth. Indian hymns do not apologise for asking for these.

🌱 For today

Remembering daily is the whole practice. The bow is how you start.

First, Vakratuṇḍa; second, Ekadanta; third, Kṛṣṇapiṅgākṣa; fourth, Gajavaktra.

VERSE 2 · DEEP DIVE

NAMES ONE TO FOUR

🔎 In context

Verses 2–4 give twelve names, counted off like beads. The first four describe his face: the curved trunk, the single tusk, the eyes, the elephant’s face.

📜 Looking deeper

Vakratuṇḍa is also the first of the Mudgala Purāṇa’s eight avatāras, who conquers Mātsarya, jealousy; Ekadanta, the second, conquers Mada, arrogance. Kṛṣṇapiṅgākṣa is unusual: kṛṣṇa, dark, and piṅga, tawny or reddish-brown, eyes; some read it as “dark-bodied and tawny-eyed”. Gajavaktra simply means elephant-faced. The ordinal words, prathamam, dvitīyakam, tṛtīyam, caturthakam, make the hymn easy to count and remember.

🌱 For today

Name things one at a time. Attention grows with each name.

↑ VERSE 2 · Curved trunk, one tusk, tawny eyes, elephant face.

lambodaraṃ pañcamaṃ ca ṣaṣṭhaṃ vikaṭameva casaptamaṃ vighnarājaṃ ca dhūmravarṇaṃ tathāṣṭamam ‖3‖
Fifth, Lambodara; sixth, Vikaṭa; seventh, Vighnarāja; and eighth, Dhūmravarṇa.

Big belly, king of obstacles.

VERSE 3 · DEEP DIVE

NAMES FIVE TO EIGHT

🔎 In context

The next four names move from face to body and power. Lambodara is the famous belly; Vikaṭa means huge or strange-looking; Vighnarāja, king of obstacles; Dhūmravarṇa, smoke-coloured.

📜 Looking deeper

Seven of the twelve names are also names of the Mudgala Purāṇa’s eight avatāras (all except Mahodara): Vakratuṇḍa, Ekadanta, Gajānana, Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Vighnarāja and Dhūmravarṇa each have a story there, defeating a demon who stands for a human fault. Vighnarāja is a reminder that Gaṇeśa is lord of obstacles both ways: he removes them for the devotee and sets them for the proud. The Gaṇeśa Purāṇa’s avatāra for the Kali age, Dhūmraketu, is smoke-coloured too.

🌱 For today

The one who rules obstacles can place them as well as remove them. Approach with humility.

ZAPPY: Smoke-coloured? I thought he was red.

PROF. BOLT: Red in the Atharvaśīrṣa, Zappy. Each name catches a different form.

VERSE 4

NAMES NINE TO TWELVE
navamaṃ bhālacandraṃ ca daśamaṃ tu vināyakamekādaśaṃ gaṇapatiṃ dvādaśaṃ tu gajānanam ‖4‖
Ninth, Bhālacandra; tenth, Vināyaka; eleventh, Gaṇapati; and twelfth, Gajānana.
WordMeans
bhālacandrawith the moon on his forehead
vināyakathe leader, with none above him
gaṇapatilord of the gaṇas
gajānanaelephant-faced
The moon on his brow, lord of the gaṇas.

VERSE 4 · DEEP DIVE

NAMES NINE TO TWELVE

🔎 In context

The last four names include his best-known titles: Vināyaka, Gaṇapati and Gajānana. Bhālacandra, “moon on the forehead”, recalls the story in which Gaṇeśa, after cursing the moon, wears a crescent on his brow, as his father Śiva does.

📜 Looking deeper

Gajānana, the twelfth, repeats the meaning of the fourth, Gajavaktra: both mean elephant-faced. The repetition does not bother reciters; it frames the list with the feature everyone knows. Gajānana is also the name of the Mudgala Purāṇa avatāra who conquers greed. Vināyaka is the name used in the South and in Śaṅkara’s Pañcaratnam; Gaṇapati is the Vedic-sounding title of the Atharvaśīrṣa. In twelve names the hymn sums up the whole Gaṇeśa tradition.

🌱 For today

Twelve names, one god. Each name is a door; they all open on the same room.

↑ VERSE 4 · The moon on his brow, lord of the gaṇas.

VERSE 5

AT THE THREE JUNCTIONS
dvādaśaitāni nāmāni trisandhyaṃ yaḥ paṭhennaraḥna ca vighnabhayaṃ tasya sarvasiddhiśca jāyate ‖5‖
Whoever recites these twelve names at the three junctions of the day has no fear of obstacles, and every success comes to him.
WordMeans
dvādaśaitāni nāmānithese twelve names
trisandhyamat the three junctions: dawn, noon, dusk
vighnabhayafear of obstacles
sarvasiddhievery success
No more fear of obstacles.

VERSE 5 · DEEP DIVE

AT THE THREE JUNCTIONS

🔎 In context

The phalaśruti begins. The three sandhyās, junctions of the day, are dawn, noon and dusk, the traditional times of prayer. Reciting the twelve names at each removes the fear of obstacles.

📜 Looking deeper

The promise is carefully worded: not that obstacles vanish, but that fear of them does (na ca vighnabhayam). For a devotee, that is often the real gift: the obstacle may remain, but it no longer paralyses. The three junctions are times of change, from night to day, morning to afternoon, day to night, and Gaṇeśa is the god of thresholds, so the timing fits.

🌱 For today

Fear of the obstacle is often heavier than the obstacle.

↑ VERSE 5 · No more fear of obstacles.

VERSE 6

WHATEVER YOU SEEK
vidyārthī labhate vidyāṃ dhanārthī labhate dhanamputrārthī labhate putrānmokṣārthī labhate gatim ‖6‖
The seeker of learning gains learning; the seeker of wealth gains wealth; the seeker of children gains children; the seeker of liberation gains the goal.
WordMeans
vidyārthīone who seeks learning
dhanārthīone who seeks wealth
putrārthīone who seeks children
mokṣārthī … gatimone who seeks liberation … the goal
Whatever you seek, you find.

VERSE 6 · DEEP DIVE

WHATEVER YOU SEEK

🔎 In context

Verse 6 is the most quoted line of the hymn, a simple four-part promise: learning, wealth, children, liberation. It is printed on school notebooks and shop calendars across Maharashtra.

📜 Looking deeper

The four seekers cover the main aims of life: knowledge, prosperity, family and freedom. Vidyārthī, “seeker of learning”, is also the ordinary word for a student, so the line has a special place at exam time. Gati, “the goal” or “the way”, is the destination of the seeker of mokṣa. The hymn accepts every kind of seeker; nobody is told that their wish is too small.

🌱 For today

Know what you are really seeking. The hymn meets you there.

↑ VERSE 6 · Whatever you seek, you find.

VERSE 7

SIX MONTHS, ONE YEAR
japedgaṇapatistotraṃ ṣaḍbhirmāsaiḥ phalaṃ labhetsaṃvatsareṇa siddhiṃ ca labhate nātra saṃśayaḥ ‖7‖
Let one recite this Gaṇapati hymn: in six months one gains its fruit, and in a year, full success. There is no doubt of it.
WordMeans
japedlet one recite
ṣaḍbhir māsaiḥin six months
saṃvatsareṇain a year
nātra saṃśayaḥthere is no doubt here
Six months for fruit, a year for success.

VERSE 7 · DEEP DIVE

SIX MONTHS, ONE YEAR

🔎 In context

Verse 7 sets a timetable: fruit in six months, full siddhi, success, in a year. Like most phalaśrutis, it is meant to encourage steady practice.

📜 Looking deeper

Japa means repeating, usually quietly or under the breath; here it is applied to the whole hymn. The time frame matters: the stotra promises nothing overnight. Six months and a year are long enough to make recitation a habit, and a habit of daily remembrance is, by the hymn’s own logic, the real point. “Nātra saṃśayaḥ”, there is no doubt, is a stock phrase of Purāṇic phalaśrutis.

🌱 For today

Give a practice time before you judge it. Six months is a fair trial.

↑ VERSE 7 · Six months for fruit, a year for success.

VERSE 8

WRITE IT AND GIVE IT
aṣṭabhyo brāhmaṇebhyaśca likhitvā yaḥ samarpayettasya vidyā bhavetsarvā gaṇeśasya prasādataḥ ‖8‖
Whoever writes it out and gives it to eight brāhmaṇas: by Gaṇeśa’s grace, all learning becomes his.
WordMeans
aṣṭabhyo brāhmaṇebhyaḥto eight brāhmaṇas
likhitvāhaving written out
samarpayetshould give, offer
gaṇeśasya prasādataḥby Gaṇeśa’s grace
Give the hymn, gain learning.

VERSE 8 · DEEP DIVE

WRITE IT AND GIVE IT

🔎 In context

The last verse praises copying the hymn and giving it away, as the Atharvaśīrṣa praised teaching it to eight brāhmaṇas. Before printing, hand-copying was how texts survived, and giving a copy was an act of merit.

📜 Looking deeper

The fruit is fitting: the one who spreads learning gains learning. In our time the same spirit lives on in printed booklets of the stotra given away at temples and weddings. Printed versions end with a colophon naming the Nārada Purāṇa and calling the hymn “saṅkaṭanāśana”, the destroyer of trouble.

🌱 For today

Share what you have learned. It is the surest way to keep it.

↑ VERSE 8 · Give the hymn, gain learning.

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

The stotra in five lines
1. Bow to Gaurī’s son and remember him daily for long life, wishes and wealth.
2–4. Vakratuṇḍa, Ekadanta, Kṛṣṇapiṅgākṣa, Gajavaktra, Lambodara, Vikaṭa, Vighnarāja, Dhūmravarṇa, Bhālacandra, Vināyaka, Gaṇapati, Gajānana.
5. Recite them at dawn, noon and dusk: no fear of obstacles.
6. Learning, wealth, children, liberation: each seeker gains what he seeks.
7–8. Fruit in six months, success in a year; copy it and give it for learning.
