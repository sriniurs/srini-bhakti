THE BROKEN TUSK

Ekadanta, the one-tusked

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Why does Gaṇeśa have only one whole tusk? Tradition gives four answers: a pen that broke while writing the Mahābhārata, Paraśurāma’s axe, a laughing moon, and a demon no weapon could kill.

This guide tells each story in full, with its source and its lesson.

ZAPPY: Four stories for one tooth?

PROF. BOLT: An important tooth, Zappy. Each story gives it a different meaning.

Gaṇeśa is Ekadanta, “the one-tusked”, and his broken tusk is one of his oldest and most important features. Some of his earliest images already show him holding it.

Tradition gives at least four explanations. He broke it himself to keep writing the Mahābhārata; Paraśurāma’s axe struck it off; he threw it at the laughing moon; or, in a southern story, he hurled it at an unconquerable demon.

This guide tells each in turn, with its source and what it teaches.

VYĀSA NEEDS A SCRIBE

The Mahābhārata begins
VyāsaThe sage who composed the Mahābhārata
ProblemA poem too vast to write down as fast as he could compose it
AdviceIn the popular telling, Brahmā tells him to ask Gaṇeśa
GaṇeśaLord of learning and letters
AgreesOn one condition
Where toldIn the opening of some Mahābhārata manuscripts

ZAPPY: Why Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: Who else could keep up? The lord of letters was the only scribe fast enough.

The sage Vyāsa composed the Mahābhārata, the longest poem in the world. In the popular telling, he needed a scribe who could keep pace with his composition, and Brahmā advised him to ask Gaṇeśa, lord of letters and learning.

Gaṇeśa agreed, but on a condition.

This story appears in the opening of many Mahābhārata manuscripts, though not in the oldest layer of the text, as the scholars’ page explains.

THE TWO CONDITIONS

A bargain of minds
Gaṇeśa’sVyāsa must dictate without ever pausing
If he stopsGaṇeśa will put down the pen and leave
Vyāsa’sGaṇeśa must understand every verse before writing it
The trickWhen Vyāsa needed time, he composed a hard verse
Gaṇeśa pausesTo understand it; Vyāsa thinks ahead
Knotty versesTradition calls these the epic’s riddling verses

ZAPPY: So Vyāsa cheated?

PROF. BOLT: He was clever. Both kept their word, and the epic got written.

Gaṇeśa’s condition was that Vyāsa must dictate without pausing; if he stopped, Gaṇeśa would put down his pen and leave. Vyāsa agreed, and added a condition of his own: Gaṇeśa must understand each verse before writing it down.

Whenever Vyāsa needed time to compose, he would recite a particularly difficult verse. While Gaṇeśa paused to work out its meaning, Vyāsa composed ahead.

Tradition says the epic’s knottiest verses come from these moments.

THE PEN BREAKS

Sacrifice for the work
Mid-flowHis writing instrument breaks
No stoppingHe cannot pause, by his own condition
He actsSnaps off his own tusk
Writes onUsing the tusk as a pen
ResultThe Mahābhārata completed
MeaningGive up something of your own to finish the work

ZAPPY: He broke his own tooth to keep his word?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the story. A promise, a pen, and no time to look for another.

In the popular telling, Gaṇeśa’s writing instrument broke partway through. Bound by his own condition, he could not stop to find another.

So he snapped off one of his tusks and went on writing with it, until the Mahābhārata was complete.

Devotees read this as the lesson of sacrifice: give up something of your own to finish the work you have promised to do.

WHAT SCHOLARS SAY

ZAPPY: So it’s not in the ‘real’ Mahābhārata?

PROF. BOLT: Not in the oldest layer. But many manuscripts carry it, and it’s shaped how Indians see him for a thousand years.

The scribe passage is found in 37 of the 59 manuscripts consulted for the critical edition of the Mahābhārata, is absent from southern manuscripts, and is left out of the critical text as a later addition. Scholars date it to about the eighth or ninth century.

The detail of the broken tusk used as a pen appears to be a still later, popular development.

None of this has dimmed the story’s appeal: it has shaped Gaṇeśa’s image as patron of writers and students for more than a thousand years.

ZAPPY: A door again?

PROF. BOLT: Gaṇeśa and doors. He was a gatekeeper from the very first story.

See: Dattātreya and Paraśurāma

The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa tells a different story. Paraśurāma, a great devotee of Śiva, came to Kailāsa carrying the axe Śiva had given him, to pay his respects. Gaṇeśa, guarding the door while Śiva rested, would not let him in.

They quarrelled and fought. Gaṇeśa seized Paraśurāma in his trunk and whirled him round.

The Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa tells a similar version.

THE AXE

Respect for his father’s gift
Paraśurāma throwsThe axe that Śiva gave him
Gaṇeśa seesIt is his father’s weapon
He choosesNot to block it
The blowIt strikes his tusk, which falls
NameFrom that day, Ekadanta
MeaningHe honoured Śiva’s gift even against himself

ZAPPY: He could have stopped it?

PROF. BOLT: The story says yes. He let it fall rather than shame his father’s weapon.

Recovering, Paraśurāma threw his axe at Gaṇeśa. Gaṇeśa saw that it was his father’s own gift, and chose not to resist it.

The axe struck his tusk, which broke and fell. From that day he was Ekadanta.

The story makes the broken tusk a sign of respect and restraint: he honoured his father’s gift even when it was turned against him.

PĀRVATĪ’S GRIEF

After the blow
PārvatīGrieves and is angry at the injury to her son
SkandaTells her what happened
DestinyThe Brahmāṇḍa says it was fated: he becomes Ekadanta
ParaśurāmaRepents and praises Gaṇeśa
PeaceThe family is reconciled
PatternLike the birth story: injury, grief, honour

ZAPPY: Why does Pārvatī always get angry?

PROF. BOLT: She’s his mother. In both stories her grief is what turns injury into honour.

Pārvatī grieved and was angry at the injury to her son, and asked what had happened; Skanda told her. In the Brahmāṇḍa’s telling, it is said to have been destined: so he became Ekadanta.

Paraśurāma repented and praised Gaṇeśa, and the family was reconciled.

The pattern echoes the birth story: an injury, a mother’s grief, and an honour that follows.

ZAPPY: He threw his tooth at the moon?

PROF. BOLT: In this version. The next guide tells the whole tale, and why you shouldn’t look at the moon on Caturthī.

A third story connects the tusk to the moon. After a great feast of modakas, Gaṇeśa was riding home when his mouse stumbled and he tumbled to the ground. The moon, watching, laughed.

Angry, Gaṇeśa broke off his tusk and threw it at the moon. Some say the moon’s dark marks come from that blow.

The next guide tells the whole tale, including the curse that followed and why people avoid looking at the moon on Gaṇeśa Caturthī.

THE DEMON GAJAMUKHA

A southern story
GajamukhāsuraAn elephant-faced demon who could not be killed by weapons
ProblemEvery weapon failed against him
GaṇeśaBreaks off his own tusk, which is no weapon
Hurls itThe demon falls
ChangeHe becomes a mouse
MountAnd Gaṇeśa makes him his vehicle

ZAPPY: So the mouse used to be a demon?

PROF. BOLT: In this southern telling, yes. The enemy became his mount: defeated, then carried along.

A popular southern story tells of Gajamukhāsura, an elephant-faced demon who had won a boon that no weapon could kill him. The gods could not defeat him.

Gaṇeśa broke off his own tusk, which was no weapon, and hurled it at the demon, who fell. Defeated, the demon turned into a mouse, and Gaṇeśa made him his vehicle.

We give this as popular tradition; its exact textual source is uncertain.

ZAPPY: Which one should I believe?

PROF. BOLT: Keep them all. A god with one tusk has room for four stories.

Each story gives the tusk a different meaning. The pen speaks of sacrifice for knowledge and keeping one’s word; the axe, of respect and restraint; the moon, of pride punished and then forgiven; Gajamukha, of a power no armour can stop.

What they share is that Gaṇeśa gives up something of his own, and gains a name by it.

All are tradition, and none cancels the others.

ZAPPY: Which side is broken?

PROF. BOLT: Look at the next image you see. Images differ; most break his right tusk, which is on your left.

In the usual four-armed form, his lower right hand holds the broken tusk. Some of the earliest images already show it.

Images differ on which tusk is broken, though most show his right tusk broken. Some modern pictures show him writing with it.

In the Mudgala Purāṇa, Ekadanta is the second of his eight avatāras, the one who conquers pride.

WHAT THE TUSK TEACHES

For the reader
Finish the workEven if it costs you something
Keep promisesGaṇeśa did, and so did Vyāsa
RespectSome blows are better taken than returned
ImperfectA broken tusk did not make him less
LearningThe tusk became a pen
ForgiveThe moon was forgiven

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: What looks like loss can become your pen.

The broken tusk is a reminder that loss can serve a purpose. In the Mahābhārata story, the broken tusk became the pen that wrote the epic.

The stories also teach keeping promises, restraint when one could strike back, and forgiveness.

And they show that imperfection did not diminish him. The one-tusked god is loved precisely as he is.
