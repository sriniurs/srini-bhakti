RĀVAṆA AND GOKARṆA

How a cowherd boy outwitted the demon king

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Rāvaṇa won Śiva’s own ātmaliṅga, on one condition: never set it down on the way home. A false dusk, a cowherd boy, three calls, and the liṅga is rooted at Gokarṇa for ever.

This guide tells how Gaṇeśa outwitted the demon king, and visits Karnataka’s temple town by the sea.

AT A GLANCE

How a cowherd boy outwitted Rāvaṇa
RāvaṇaThe demon king of Laṅkā, a great devotee of Śiva
The prizeŚiva’s ātmaliṅga, “the liṅga of his very self”
The conditionWherever it is set down, there it stays
The trickViṣṇu brings a false dusk; Gaṇeśa appears as a boy
The resultThe liṅga is rooted at Gokarṇa, on Karnataka’s coast
NameMahābaleśvara, “lord of great strength”
GaṇeśaStill at Gokarṇa, worshipped first

ZAPPY: Rāvaṇa was a Śiva devotee?

PROF. BOLT: A famous one, Zappy. He even composed the Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra, by tradition.

Gokarṇa, on the coast of Uttara Kannada in Karnataka, is home to Mahābaleśvara, the ātmaliṅga of Śiva. Its story is one of Gaṇeśa’s cleverest.

Rāvaṇa, the demon king, won the ātmaliṅga from Śiva by penance, on condition that he never set it down on the way home. With Viṣṇu’s help, Gaṇeśa, disguised as a cowherd boy, made sure he did, and it has stayed at Gokarṇa ever since.

This guide tells the story step by step and visits the temple town today.

RĀVAṆA’S MOTHER

Why he wanted the liṅga
KaikasīHis mother, a devotee of Śiva
Her liṅgaWorshipped for her son’s prosperity
IndraJealous, steals it and throws it into the sea
Her griefShe stops eating
Rāvaṇa’s vowTo bring her Śiva’s own ātmaliṅga
OffTo Kailāsa

ZAPPY: He did it for his mother?

PROF. BOLT: In this telling, yes. Even the villain of the Rāmāyaṇa loved his mother.

In the temple’s legend, Rāvaṇa’s mother, a devotee of Śiva, worshipped a liṅga for her son’s prosperity. Indra, jealous, stole it and threw it into the sea, and she stopped eating in her grief.

Rāvaṇa vowed to bring her something greater: Śiva’s own ātmaliṅga, from Kailāsa itself.

Other tellings simply make it Rāvaṇa’s own ambition.

THE PENANCE

At Kailāsa
AusterityFierce penance to please Śiva

SacrificeHe cuts off his own head
The harpMade with strings from his own body, in the legend

ZAPPY: He made a harp out of himself?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the legend. Rāvaṇa never did anything by halves.

See: Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra

At Kailāsa Rāvaṇa performed fierce penance. Tradition links his song of praise to the Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra, which is ascribed to him.

The legend says he cut off his own head and made a harp with strings drawn from his own body.

Śiva, pleased, offered him a boon, and Rāvaṇa asked for the ātmaliṅga.

ZAPPY: Why would Śiva give it away?

PROF. BOLT: A god keeps his word to a devotee. The gods had to find another way.

The ātmaliṅga carried Śiva’s very power. With it, Laṅkā and its king would be invincible.

Śiva granted it with one condition: Rāvaṇa must carry it home without ever setting it on the ground; wherever it was put down, it would stay for ever.

Rāvaṇa agreed and set off south. The gods were alarmed.

THE FALSE DUSK

Viṣṇu’s part
PlanThe gods turn to Viṣṇu (and in many tellings to Gaṇeśa)
ViṣṇuHides the sun so it seems evening
RāvaṇaA strict Brahmin, must perform his evening rites
ProblemHe cannot do them holding the liṅga
PlaceNear Gokarṇa, on the western sea
NeedSomeone to hold it for him

ZAPPY: Rāvaṇa was strict about his prayers?

PROF. BOLT: Very. The gods used his piety against him.

The gods sought help. As Rāvaṇa approached Gokarṇa on the western coast, Viṣṇu hid the sun so that it seemed to be evening.

Rāvaṇa, strict in his religious duties, had to perform his twilight rites, but could not do so while holding the liṅga.

He needed someone to hold it for him.

THE COWHERD BOY

Gaṇeśa in disguise
AppearsA boy tending cows (a Brahmin boy in some tellings)
Rāvaṇa asksHold this for me; don’t put it down
The boy agreesOn one condition
ConditionI will call you three times; if you don’t come, I’ll set it down
RāvaṇaAgrees, and goes to bathe
The boyIs Gaṇeśa

ZAPPY: He agreed to such a condition?

PROF. BOLT: He was in a hurry. Gaṇeśa counted on it.

Just then a boy appeared, tending cows; some tellings make him a young Brahmin. It was Gaṇeśa in disguise.

Rāvaṇa asked him to hold the liṅga and not to put it down. The boy agreed, on condition that he would call Rāvaṇa three times, and if Rāvaṇa did not return, he would set it down.

Rāvaṇa, in a hurry, agreed, and went to the water.

THREE CALLS

The liṅga set down
First callRāvaṇa still at his rites
Second callStill not done
Third callToo late
GaṇeśaSets the liṅga on the ground
RootedIt stays there, as Śiva promised
The boyVanishes with his cows

ZAPPY: Did Gaṇeśa cheat?

PROF. BOLT: He kept to the letter of the deal. Rāvaṇa agreed to three calls.

The boy called once, twice, three times. Rāvaṇa, still at his rites, did not come in time.

Gaṇeśa set the liṅga on the ground, and it was rooted there for ever, as Śiva had said. Then he vanished with his cows.

Gaṇeśa kept exactly to the terms. Rāvaṇa had agreed to them.

THE COW’S EAR

How Gokarṇa got its name
Rāvaṇa returnsFurious
A cowSinking into the earth
He grabsIts ear, as the rest disappears
StoneThe ear becomes a rock
Go + karṇaCow + ear: Gokarṇa
Other talesThe Purāṇas give other origins of the name too

ZAPPY: The town is named after a cow’s ear?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the local story. Gokarṇa means exactly that.

Rāvaṇa rushed back. He saw one cow sinking into the earth and caught it by the ear just as it disappeared. The ear turned to stone.

So, the local story goes, the place is called Gokarṇa, “cow’s ear”.

Purāṇic texts give other origins for the name as well; this is the one told at the temple.

MAHĀBALEŚVARA

The liṅga that would not move
Rāvaṇa pullsWith all his strength
It won’t moveIt is rooted
He faintsExhausted
NameMahābala, “of great strength”: Mahābaleśvara
PiecesTradition says fragments flew to other shrines nearby
The fiveRemembered as the pañca-kṣetras of the coast

ZAPPY: Rāvaṇa, who lifted Kailāsa, couldn’t lift this?

PROF. BOLT: The story enjoys that. The mountain moved; the liṅga didn’t.

Rāvaṇa tried to pull the liṅga out of the ground with all his strength, the strength that had once shaken Kailāsa itself, but it would not move. He collapsed, exhausted.

Because of its great strength, the liṅga was called Mahābaleśvara, “lord of great strength”.

Tradition says pieces of its coverings flew to other places nearby, which became the five holy places of the coast.

RĀVAṆA’S BLOW

The dent on Gaṇeśa’s head
RāvaṇaStrikes the boy on the head in his anger
The markGokarṇa’s Gaṇeśa image shows a hollow on the head
The imageGranite, standing, two-armed, about five feet tall
The formA boy, short-legged, like a cowherd
TraditionThe dent is Rāvaṇa’s blow
GaṇeśaUnmoved, smiling

ZAPPY: Rāvaṇa hit Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: So the temple story goes, and the image carries the mark.

In his anger Rāvaṇa struck the boy on the head. The Gaṇeśa image at Gokarṇa, a granite figure about five feet tall, standing, with two arms, has a hollow on top of the head that tradition says is the mark of that blow.

He is shown as a boy, short-legged like a cowherd, a form found in this region.

He stands there, unmoved, to this day.

ZAPPY: Do people visit Gaṇeśa first?

PROF. BOLT: By custom, yes. The boy who saved the liṅga gets the first bow.

Today the ātmaliṅga is enshrined in the Mahābaleśvara temple, set in a square base with a small opening through which devotees see the top of the liṅga. Kālidāsa mentions the lord of Gokarṇa in the Raghuvaṃśa, and the Tamil Tēvāram praises it.

By custom pilgrims worship the Mahāgaṇapati shrine first, and the Śivarātri chariot procession starts from the Gaṇapati temple at the end of Car Street.

Gokarṇa will have its own guide in the Temples part of this series.

ZAPPY: Two Gaṇeśas on one coast?

PROF. BOLT: Uttara Kannada loves him. A whole pilgrimage can be planned along that shore.

Not far away, near Honnāvar, is Idagunji, one of Karnataka’s best-known Gaṇeśa temples, with a standing two-armed image.

Pilgrims along this coast often combine Gokarṇa, Idagunji and Murḍeśvara in one journey.

Idagunji’s own legend will be told in its temple guide.

WHAT THE STORY TEACHES

For the reader
ClevernessWit beats strength
PietyEven Rāvaṇa’s devotion was real
PowerShould not belong to one who would misuse it
HumilityThe mighty can’t lift what grace has set down
PlaceA story rooted in Karnataka’s own coast
FirstGaṇeśa at the gate again

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: A little boy with cows did what the gods couldn’t: he kept his word, and won.

The story celebrates cleverness over brute strength. A boy with a few cows did what the gods could not.

It also respects Rāvaṇa: his devotion is real, his love for his mother is real. The problem is the power he would have had.

And it ties a great Purāṇic theme to a real place on Karnataka’s coast, where Gaṇeśa still receives the first bow.
