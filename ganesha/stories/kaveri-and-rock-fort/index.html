THE KĀVERĪ AND THE ROCK FORT

A crow, a sage and a boy on a hill

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Two southern stories on one river. As a crow, Gaṇeśa tips over the sage Agastya’s pot in Kodagu, and the Kāverī flows. As a boy, he sets Raṅganātha down at Śrīraṅgam and flees up the Rock Fort at Tiruchi.

This guide tells both, visits Talakāveri and Ucchi Pillaiyār, and compares them with the Gokarṇa story.

ZAPPY: A crow and a boy?

PROF. BOLT: Gaṇeśa in two disguises, Zappy, on one river.

Two southern stories, both set on the Kāverī, show Gaṇeśa at his most playful. In the first, disguised as a crow, he tips over the sage Agastya’s water-pot in the hills of Kodagu, and the Kāverī flows out to water the south.

In the second, disguised as a boy, he sets down Vibhīṣaṇa’s image of Raṅganātha on the river’s bank at Śrīraṅgam, then flees up the great rock at Tiruchi, where he is caught and struck on the forehead.

Both follow the same pattern as the Gokarṇa story: something held too tightly is released for everyone.

AGASTYA GOES SOUTH

Setting the scene

VindhyaHe humbles the rising mountain and crosses it

In one tellingKāverī, daughter of the sage Kavera, travels in the pot
In anotherHis wife Lopāmudrā, as water
In anotherWater of the Gaṅgā, carried south

ZAPPY: Who was in the pot?

PROF. BOLT: Depends who tells it. A river-goddess, the sage’s wife, or Gaṅgā’s water. All roads lead to the Kāverī.

See: Agastya and Lopāmudrā

Agastya is the great sage of the south, who humbled the rising Vindhya mountain and crossed into the southern lands. Our Śrīvidyā series has a guide on him and his wife Lopāmudrā.

He carried a kamaṇḍalu, a water-pot. Who or what was in it depends on the telling: Kāverī, daughter of the sage Kavera, travelling with him in the form of water; or his wife Lopāmudrā; or simply sacred water of the Gaṅgā.

Every version ends in the same place: the Kāverī.

ZAPPY: So Gaṇeśa removed a drought?

PROF. BOLT: That’s exactly the obstacle he removed. A very practical god.

In many tellings the southern lands were suffering from drought, and the people prayed to Gaṇeśa, remover of obstacles.

The obstacle was clear: the water the land needed was shut up in a sage’s pot, and no one would dare take it from Agastya.

Gaṇeśa found a way.

THE CROW

ZAPPY: Agastya chased a crow?

PROF. BOLT: The story says he did, and found a boy, and then Gaṇeśa. He forgave him at once.

When Agastya set down his pot in the Brahmagiri hills, a crow landed on its rim and tipped it over. The water poured out and became a river.

Agastya, angry, chased the crow, which became a boy and then revealed himself as Gaṇeśa. The sage forgave him at once.

Wikipedia’s article on the Kāverī cites the Skanda Purāṇa for this story.

THE NAME KĀVERĪ

Folk etymology

Kāverī“Spread by a crow”, says one explanation
AnotherDaughter of the sage Kavera
SanketiAnother view links the name to a word for river
Our noteFolk etymologies; scholars debate the real origin

ZAPPY: Is that really where the name comes from?

PROF. BOLT: It’s one explanation. The river doesn’t mind which you believe.

One popular explanation derives the name Kāverī from Tamil kā, “crow”, and viri, “to spread”: the river spread by a crow.

Others say she is named as the daughter of the sage Kavera, or link the name to a word for river.

These are folk etymologies; scholars debate the real origin.

TALAKĀVERI

The source today
WhereBrahmagiri hills, Kodagu district, Karnataka

TempleAgastyeśvara, recalling the sage
Tulā SaṅkramaṇaIn mid-October the spring is said to rise; thousands gather

ZAPPY: Can I visit the source?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and in October, at Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa, you’ll have thousands of Kodavas for company.

The Kāverī rises at Talakāveri in the Brahmagiri hills of Kodagu district, Karnataka, at about 1,341 metres. A small tank, the kuṇḍike, marks the spring, and the temple there is dedicated to Agastyeśvara, recalling the sage.

Most of the year the water seems to flow underground before emerging below. At Tulā Saṅkramaṇa, in mid-October, the spring is said to rise, and thousands gather; this is Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa.

Bhāgamaṇḍala, a short distance below, is the river’s first sacred confluence.

ZAPPY: The river of Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: She flows right past it. Every Mysuru child grows up on Kāverī water.

See: The three Raṅgas on the Kāverī

The river is worshipped as Kāveramma, mother Kāverī, especially by the Kodavas of Kodagu. One local story says they begged her not to leave their land.

Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa at Talakāveri is the great day of her year.

From Kodagu she flows past Śrīraṅgapaṭṇa and Mysuru into Tamil Nadu. Our Triranga guides follow the three island temples of Raṅganātha along her course.

ZAPPY: The same condition as Gokarṇa?

PROF. BOLT: The same. And Gaṇeśa plays the same trick.

See: Antya Raṅga, Śrīraṅgam

The second story begins after the Rāmāyaṇa war. Rāma crowned Vibhīṣaṇa king of Laṅkā and gave him an image of Raṅganātha, Viṣṇu reclining on the serpent. The condition was the familiar one: once set down, it could not be moved.

Vibhīṣaṇa carried it south toward Laṅkā. The gods wanted it to remain in India.

The story is told in the Śrīraṅga Māhātmya and in temple tradition.

THE BOY BY THE RIVER

ZAPPY: Same trick, different demon?

PROF. BOLT: Vibhīṣaṇa wasn’t a villain; he was Rāma’s friend. But the gods wanted Raṅganātha on the Kāverī.

Reaching the Kāverī, Vibhīṣaṇa wished to bathe and perform his rites. A boy stood by the river, a cowherd in some tellings, a young Brahmin in others.

Vibhīṣaṇa asked him to hold the image without setting it down. As soon as Vibhīṣaṇa stepped into the water, the boy placed it on the ground, where it stayed: Śrīraṅgam, on its island between two arms of the Kāverī.

Then the boy ran.

THE ROCK FORT

CaughtAt the summit
BlowVibhīṣaṇa strikes him on the forehead

ExplainedRaṅganātha was destined to stay by the Kāverī

ZAPPY: Another dent?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The Ucchi Pillaiyār image has a mark on its forehead, devotees say from that blow.

Vibhīṣaṇa chased him to the great rock by the river and caught him at the summit, where he struck the boy on the forehead.

The boy revealed himself as Gaṇeśa, and explained that Raṅganātha was destined to remain by the Kāverī. Vibhīṣaṇa apologised.

Devotees point to a mark on the forehead of the Ucchi Pillaiyār image as the sign of that blow.

ZAPPY: Can you see Śrīraṅgam from the top?

PROF. BOLT: On a clear day, yes: the god Gaṇeśa set down, seen from the rock where he was caught.

The Ucchi Pillaiyār temple stands at the very top of the Rock Fort in Tiruchirāppaḷḷi; ucchi means “summit” in Tamil. It is a rock-cut temple usually dated to about the seventh century.

Pilgrims climb hundreds of steps cut into the rock, passing the Tāyumānavar Śiva temple and the Māṇikka Vināyakar shrine on the way. From the top, Śrīraṅgam’s towers can be seen across the river.

It was once the greatest temple of the Gāṇapatya tradition, as our Foundations guide describes.

PatternSomething held too tightly is released for everyone
DisguisesBoy, cowherd, crow
PlacesAll in the south: two in Karnataka, one in Tamil Nadu

ZAPPY: Why does he keep doing this?

PROF. BOLT: Because some treasures are meant for everyone. Gaṇeśa opens the hand that holds them.

Three stories share one pattern. At Gokarṇa, Rāvaṇa’s ātmaliṅga is set down; at Śrīraṅgam, Vibhīṣaṇa’s Raṅganātha; at Talakāveri, Agastya’s pot is tipped.

In each, something precious held tightly by one person is released for everyone, and in two of them Gaṇeśa takes a blow on the head for it.

His disguises change, boy, cowherd, crow, but the trick is the same.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Next time you drink Kāverī water, thank a crow.

These stories teach letting go: what is held tightly can bless everyone once released. A drought, too, is an obstacle for the remover of obstacles.

They also show the gods choosing their own homes, and quick forgiveness from both Agastya and Vibhīṣaṇa.

And for readers in Mysuru and along the Kāverī, they are home stories: our river, from Kodagu to Tiruchi.
