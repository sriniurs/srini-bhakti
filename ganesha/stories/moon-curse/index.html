THE MOON’S CURSE

Why we don’t look at the moon on Caturthī

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the night of Gaṇeśa Caturthī, many families keep their eyes down after moonrise. Why? Because of a feast, a fall, a laughing moon and a curse.

This guide tells the story, how the curse was softened, how even Kṛṣṇa was caught by it over the Syamantaka jewel, and what tradition says to do if you see the moon by mistake.

Śrī Bhālacandrāya namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is the moon really dangerous that night?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the tradition, Zappy. Let’s hear why.

On the night of Gaṇeśa Caturthī, many families keep their eyes down after moonrise. This guide tells the story behind the custom.

After a great feast, Gaṇeśa tumbled from his mouse; the moon laughed; Gaṇeśa cursed him. The curse was softened to a single night, and its penalty became mithyā-doṣa, false blame. Even Kṛṣṇa, tradition says, was caught by it, and accused of stealing the Syamantaka jewel.

We tell the story, its versions, the remedy, and the custom as it lives today.

THE FEAST

A happy, heavy evening
OccasionHis birthday, in many tellings
OfferingsModakas and sweets from everywhere
He eatsAnd eats, and eats
Ride homeOn his mouse, in the moonlight
A snakeCrosses the path, in some versions
The mouseStartled, it stumbles

ZAPPY: Wasn’t that too much for a mouse?

PROF. BOLT: That’s rather the point of the story.

The story usually begins on Gaṇeśa’s birthday, when devotees everywhere offered him modakas and sweets. He ate his fill, and more.

Late at night he set off home on his mouse, the moon shining overhead. In some tellings a snake slid across the path.

Startled, or simply overloaded, the mouse stumbled.

THE FALL

Belly and snake
He tumblesOff the mouse, onto the road
BellyBursts open, and the modakas spill out
CalmHe gathers them back in
SnakeTies his belly shut with the snake as a belt
That’s whyMany images show a snake round his belly

ZAPPY: He used a snake as a belt?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva’s son: snakes are family jewellery.

Gaṇeśa tumbled to the ground, and his belly burst open, spilling the modakas.

Unruffled, he gathered them up, put them back, caught the snake and tied it round his belly as a belt. Many images show him with a snake round his waist, and this story explains it.

Then he got up as if nothing had happened.

THE MOON LAUGHS

Pride in a beautiful face
CandraThe moon god, proud of his beauty
He seesThe pot-bellied god sprawled on the road
He laughsLoudly, mockingly
The insultMocking a body, mocking a fall
GaṇeśaNot amused

ZAPPY: Who laughs at a god falling?

PROF. BOLT: Someone very proud of their looks. The moon learned a lesson that night.

But someone had been watching. Candra, the moon god, proud of his own beauty, saw the pot-bellied god sprawled on the road and burst out laughing.

It was a cruel laugh: mocking a body and mocking a fall.

In some tellings Gaṇeśa broke off his tusk and threw it at the moon, linking this story to the broken tusk.

THE CURSE

Gaṇeśa’s anger
First formThe moon will lose its light and be invisible
OrWhoever looks at the moon will suffer disgrace
The godsAlarmed: the world needs the moon
CandraBegs forgiveness
SoftenedThe curse is limited
ResultTwo outcomes, as the next page shows

ZAPPY: Did he really curse the moon?

PROF. BOLT: He did, in his anger. And then, like Śiva, he softened.

Gaṇeśa cursed the moon. In some tellings the moon was to lose its light and vanish; in others, anyone who looked at the moon would suffer disgrace.

The gods were alarmed, for the world needs the moon. Candra, humbled, begged forgiveness.

Like his father Śiva, Gaṇeśa softened.

THE CURSE SOFTENED

Mercy after anger
LimitedOnly on his own day, the fourth of Bhādrapada’s bright half
PenaltyMithyā-doṣa: false accusation, unearned blame
Waxing, waningSome tellings: the moon will shrink and grow, never stay whole
BhālacandraIn some versions, he places the moon on his own forehead
ForgivenThe moon is humbled, not destroyed
LessonPride punished, then pardoned

ZAPPY: So the moon’s phases are a curse?

PROF. BOLT: In this telling. It’s a story, not astronomy, Zappy.

He limited the curse to a single night, his own day, the fourth of the bright half of Bhādrapada. Whoever saw the moon on that night would suffer mithyā-doṣa, false accusation.

Some tellings add that the moon would wax and wane forever rather than stay whole, a story-explanation of the phases. In others, Gaṇeśa placed the forgiven moon on his own forehead, and became Bhālacandra.

The moon was humbled, not destroyed.

MITHYĀ-DOṢA

What the curse means
MithyāFalse

MeaningBeing blamed for something you didn’t do
NotGeneral bad luck
DayBhādrapada śukla caturthī only
PreciseA specific harm for a specific day

ZAPPY: So it’s about reputation?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The moon mocked; whoever looks at him that night risks being mocked unjustly.

Mithyā-doṣa means “the blemish of falsehood”: being blamed for something you did not do. It is not general bad luck.

The precision is part of the story’s logic. The moon mocked someone undeservedly; whoever looks at him on that night risks undeserved mockery themselves.

The penalty applies only to that one night.

THE SYAMANTAKA JEWEL

Bhāgavata and Viṣṇu Purāṇas
SatrājitReceives a dazzling jewel from Sūrya
PrasenaHis brother wears it hunting, and is killed by a lion
JāmbavānKills the lion and takes the gem
Kṛṣṇa blamedDvārakā whispers that he killed Prasena for the jewel
The searchKṛṣṇa follows the trail to Jāmbavān’s cave
ReturnAfter a long fight, he wins the gem and Jāmbavatī’s hand, and returns the jewel

ZAPPY: Kṛṣṇa, falsely accused?

PROF. BOLT: Even him. The Purāṇas tell the jewel story; Caturthī tradition explains it by the moon.

The Bhāgavata and Viṣṇu Purāṇas tell the story of the Syamantaka jewel. Satrājit received it from Sūrya; his brother Prasena wore it hunting and was killed by a lion; the bear-king Jāmbavān killed the lion and took the gem.

In Dvārakā people whispered that Kṛṣṇa had killed Prasena to get the jewel. To clear his name, Kṛṣṇa followed the trail to Jāmbavān’s cave, fought him for many days, and won both the jewel and the hand of Jāmbavatī.

He returned the jewel to Satrājit, who gave him his daughter Satyabhāmā in marriage.

KṚṢṆA AND THE MOON

The Caturthī connection
TraditionKṛṣṇa had glimpsed the moon on Bhādrapada caturthī
NāradaExplains the false blame: it is Gaṇeśa’s curse
RemedyKṛṣṇa keeps the Caturthī vow and worships Gaṇeśa
Name clearedThe jewel returned, the slander ends
Where toldIn the Caturthī vrata kathā, recited at the festival
LessonNo one is above blame; humility clears it

ZAPPY: Even Kṛṣṇa had to worship Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: In this telling, yes. That’s how the Caturthī vow is said to have begun.

Caturthī tradition adds a reason for the accusation: Kṛṣṇa had glimpsed the moon on Bhādrapada caturthī. The sage Nārada explained that the false blame was Gaṇeśa’s curse at work.

To be free of it, Kṛṣṇa kept the Caturthī vow and worshipped Gaṇeśa. The slander ended with the jewel’s return.

This version is told in the Caturthī vrata kathā recited during the festival.

ZAPPY: What if I see it by accident?

PROF. BOLT: Then tradition says: hear the Syamantaka story, and laugh at yourself rather than at others.

If someone sees the moon by mistake on that night, tradition offers a remedy: hear or read the Syamantaka story. A short verse beginning “siṃhaḥ prasenam avadhīt” summarises it: the lion killed Prasena, Jāmbavān killed the lion, and the child is told not to cry, for the jewel is his.

Some also remember Gaṇeśa in prayer or keep the Caturthī fast.

The spirit of the remedy is humility, the opposite of the moon’s laughter.

ZAPPY: So on Saṅkaṣṭī we look for the moon?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, on Saṅkaṣṭī the fast is broken after moonrise. Different day, different custom.

Today, many families avoid looking at the moon from moonrise to moonset on Gaṇeśa Caturthī, and almanacs give the times for each place.

The custom is the reverse on Saṅkaṣṭī Caturthī, the fourth day of the dark fortnight each month, when devotees fast and break the fast after moonrise.

And in Mithila, Bihar, the festival of Chaurchan honours the moon on the same evening, with its own stories.

ZAPPY: Why so many versions?

PROF. BOLT: Grandmothers have told it for centuries. Every grandmother adds a twist.

Tellings differ on many points: whether a snake caused the fall, what the curse was, whether he threw his tusk, whether the moon ended up on his forehead.

The Kṛṣṇa link is Caturthī tradition rather than part of the Purāṇas’ own jewel story.

All these are living tradition, retold and varied by every generation.

WHAT THE STORY TEACHES

For the reader
Don’t mockEspecially someone who has fallen
BeautyIs no excuse for pride
AngerCan be softened by a real apology
ReputationBlame can fall on the innocent, even Kṛṣṇa
RemedyHumility clears what pride caused
LaughAt yourself, as Gaṇeśa did getting up

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Don’t laugh at someone who falls. Help them pick up the modakas.

The story warns against mocking anyone, above all someone who has fallen, and against pride in one’s own beauty.

It shows anger softened by a sincere apology. And through Kṛṣṇa’s story it shows that false blame can fall even on the innocent, and that humility is the way through.

Gaṇeśa himself sets the best example: falling, he simply got up and gathered the sweets.
