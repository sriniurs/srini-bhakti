THE RACE ROUND THE WORLD

First round the world wins

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Śiva and Pārvatī set their sons a race: first round the world wins. Skanda flew off on his peacock. Gaṇeśa walked round his parents.

This guide tells the Śiva Purāṇa version, with Gaṇeśa’s wedding and the birth of Mallikārjuna, and the Tamil version, with Nārada’s fruit and Palani.

ZAPPY: Isn’t that cheating?

PROF. BOLT: Gaṇeśa would say it’s thinking, Zappy. Let’s see both sides.

This is one of the best-loved Gaṇeśa stories. Śiva and Pārvatī set their two sons a race round the world. Skanda flew off on his peacock; Gaṇeśa simply walked round his parents, declaring that they were his whole world.

In the Śiva Purāṇa the prize is the right to marry first, and Skanda, hurt, leaves for Krauñca mountain, where the Mallikārjuna jyotirliṅga arises. In Tamil tradition the prize is a divine fruit, and Murugan goes to Palani.

This guide tells both versions and asks whether the race was fair.

SolutionA contest
RuleWhoever circles the whole earth and returns first

ZAPPY: Why a race?

PROF. BOLT: A fair test, the parents thought. They didn’t expect what Gaṇeśa would do.

In the Śiva Purāṇa’s telling, Gaṇeśa and Skanda had grown up and both wished to marry. Śiva and Pārvatī faced the question of who should marry first.

They proposed a contest: whoever went round the whole earth and returned first would be married first.

Skanda set off at once. Gaṇeśa stopped to think.

SKANDA FLIES

The peacock rider
SkandaKārttikeya, Kumāra, Murugan: the warrior son
MountThe swift peacock
OffWithout a moment’s delay
EarthForests, mountains, oceans, lands
A long wayEven for a god on a peacock
Gaṇeśa’s mountA mouse. No contest, it seems

ZAPPY: A mouse against a peacock?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. If it were about speed, Gaṇeśa had already lost.

Skanda, also called Kārttikeya, Kumāra and Murugan, is the warrior son of Śiva and Pārvatī, and his mount is the swift peacock. He flew off without a moment’s delay, over forests, mountains and oceans.

Gaṇeśa’s mount was a mouse. If the race were only about speed, he had already lost.

The story turns on what Gaṇeśa did instead.

GAṆEŚA THINKS

The circle that counts
He bathesAnd asks his parents to sit together
He worships themWith honour and offerings
He circles themSeven times, in the Śiva Purāṇa
His claimI have gone round the earth
His proofScripture: going round one’s parents equals going round the earth
WarningLeaving parents to go on pilgrimage is a grave fault, he quotes

ZAPPY: Where did he get that idea?

PROF. BOLT: From the Vedas and the śāstras, he says. He quotes them at his surprised parents.

Gaṇeśa bathed, asked his parents to sit together, and worshipped them. Then he walked round them, seven times in the Śiva Purāṇa’s telling, and bowed.

When they asked why he had not set out, he quoted scripture: one who worships and circles his parents gains the merit of circling the earth, and one who leaves his parents at home to go on pilgrimage commits a grave fault.

So, he said, he had already gone round the earth, and they should keep their word.

ZAPPY: He got married while his brother was still flying?

PROF. BOLT: And had two sons, in the Śiva Purāṇa’s telling. It was a long trip.

Delighted by his wisdom, Śiva and Pārvatī arranged his marriage to Siddhi and Buddhi, the daughters of Prajāpati Viśvarūpa. The wedding was celebrated with great joy.

In time Siddhi bore a son, Kṣema, and Buddhi a son, Lābha. The names mean welfare and gain; their mothers’ names mean attainment and intelligence.

In northern India the sons are often called Śubha and Lābha, words still written on shop doors at Dīpāvali.

ZAPPY: Was Nārada stirring trouble?

PROF. BOLT: He often does. But the story needs Skanda to go south.

Meanwhile, Skanda was still travelling. The sage Nārada met him and told him everything, suggesting that he had been sent away deliberately.

Skanda, feeling cheated, returned to Kailāsa, bowed to his parents, and left, despite their efforts to stop him.

He went south, to Krauñca mountain.

ZAPPY: So the race made a jyotirliṅga?

PROF. BOLT: In this telling, yes. Parents chasing a hurt son, and staying near him forever.

See: Mallikārjuna, Śrīśailam

The Koṭi Rudra Saṃhitā of the Śiva Purāṇa continues the story. Krauñca mountain is identified with Śrīśailam in Andhra Pradesh. Śiva and Pārvatī followed their son there; he moved away a further three yojanas.

His parents stayed on the mountain in the form of light, as the Mallikārjuna jyotirliṅga.

Our Mallikārjuna guide in the jyotirliṅga series tells the temple’s side of the story.

NĀRADA’S FRUIT

The Tamil telling
NāradaBrings a wonderful fruit to Śiva
Jñāna-paḻamThe fruit of wisdom; often a golden mango
ProblemIt must be eaten whole, by one person
ContestWhoever circles the world first wins it
Same resultMurugan flies, Gaṇeśa circles his parents and wins
MuruganAngry, leaves Kailāsa

ZAPPY: A mango again?

PROF. BOLT: It’s Tamil Nadu. Of course it’s a mango.

Tamil tradition tells it differently. The sage Nārada brought Śiva a wonderful fruit, the jñāna-paḻam, “fruit of wisdom”, often described as a golden mango. It had to be eaten whole, by one person.

Both sons wanted it. Śiva set the race: whoever circled the world first would have the fruit.

As before, Murugan flew off on his peacock, Gaṇeśa circled his parents, and Gaṇeśa won the fruit.

ComingIn the Murugan and temples series

ZAPPY: Is that really where the name comes from?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the folk explanation. Scholars have others. The story is lovely either way.

See: Skanda

Murugan, angry, left Kailāsa and went to a hill in the south, giving up his finery to stand as an ascetic boy with a staff, Daṇḍāyudhapāṇi.

His parents followed and comforted him: you yourself are the fruit. Folk etymology derives the hill’s name, Palani, from “paḻam nī”, “you are the fruit”.

Palani is one of the six sacred abodes of Murugan, which will be covered in a later series.

WAS IT FAIR?

Gaṇeśa’s viewHe understood what was asked
DevoteesBoth are right: one saw the world, one saw its source
Tamil readingMurugan showed the universe is Śiva’s body
ParentsLove both; follow the hurt one
No villainOnly two different kinds of wisdom

ZAPPY: So who really won?

PROF. BOLT: Gaṇeśa won the race. Skanda won the south. Ask anyone in Palani.

Was the race fair? Skanda did exactly what was asked and lost. Gaṇeśa understood the question differently and won.

Devotees often say both were right. In one Tamil reading, Murugan’s journey showed that the universe is Śiva’s body, while Gaṇeśa saw that Śiva and Pārvatī are its source.

And the parents’ response is telling: they follow the son who was hurt.

ZAPPY: Should I walk round my parents?

PROF. BOLT: Some families still do it at festivals. It’s a lovely custom, and they’ll be pleased.

The race is often told to explain why Gaṇeśa is worshipped first. In the Śiva Purāṇa, that honour is given at his birth; the race shows the wisdom behind it.

Pradakṣiṇā, walking round something holy, is still part of every temple visit.

Some families keep the custom of children circling their parents on festival days.

PRADAKṢIṆĀ

Walking round with reverence
MeaningWalking clockwise round something holy
Right sideThe holy one stays on your right

GaṇeśaOften circled a set number of times
Inner meaningLife revolves round the sacred
The raceGaṇeśa’s seven circles made a whole world

ZAPPY: Why clockwise?

PROF. BOLT: So the holy one is always on your right, the side of respect.

Pradakṣiṇā means walking round something holy, keeping it on one’s right. Devotees circle the shrine, the temple, sometimes the whole town or hill.

Gaṇeśa shrines are often circled a fixed number of times.

The inner meaning is that life should revolve round what is sacred. Gaṇeśa’s seven circles round his parents are the story’s picture of that.

WHAT THE STORY TEACHES

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: The whole world might be sitting right in front of you.

The story teaches thinking before rushing, and honouring one’s parents as a whole world.

It also takes hurt feelings seriously: Śiva and Pārvatī follow their wounded son to the mountain, and stay.

Both sons are loved and both are worshipped. The race had a winner, but no loser.
