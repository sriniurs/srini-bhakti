THE AṢṬAVINĀYAKA

Eight Gaṇeśas round Pune

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

The Aṣṭavināyaka, “eight Vināyakas”, are eight Gaṇeśa temples around Pune, visited in a fixed order that begins and ends at Morgaon.

This overview gives the circuit, the eight forms and their stories in brief, their Gāṇapatya and Peshwa history, and links to a full guide for each temple.

ZAPPY: Why go in order?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition, Zappy. Start with Mayūreśvara, end with him, and the circle is complete.

The Aṣṭavināyaka are eight Gaṇeśa temples in Maharashtra, five in Pune district, two in Raigad and one in Ahilyanagar, visited in a set order that starts and ends at Morgaon. The whole circuit takes about three days.

Each image is believed to be svayambhū, self-manifested rather than carved, and each temple has its own legend. Most took their present form under the Peshwas in the eighteenth century; several are still managed by the Chinchwad Devasthān Trust.

This overview introduces the eight; each has its own guide.

THE EIGHT IN ORDER

The pilgrimage sequence
1. MorgaonMayūreśvara; Pune district; river Karhā
2. SiddhaṭekSiddhivināyaka; Ahilyanagar district; river Bhīmā
3. PālīBallāḷeśvara; Raigad district
4. MahaḍVaradavināyaka; Raigad district
5. TheūrCintāmaṇi; Pune district; river Mula–Mutha–Bhīmā
6. LeṇyādriGirijātmaja; Pune district; river Kukaḍī
7. OjharVighneśvara; Pune district; river Kukaḍī
8. RāñjaṇgāvMahāgaṇapati; Pune district

ZAPPY: Is the order fixed?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition fixes it. Many pilgrims follow it; some adjust for roads. Morgaon first and last is the rule most keep.

The traditional order is Morgaon (Mayūreśvara), Siddhaṭek (Siddhivināyaka), Pālī (Ballāḷeśvara), Mahaḍ (Varadavināyaka), Theūr (Cintāmaṇi), Leṇyādri (Girijātmaja), Ojhar (Vighneśvara) and Rāñjaṇgāv (Mahāgaṇapati), returning finally to Morgaon.

The route crosses the Deccan plateau and the Sahyādri ghats down to the Konkan for Pālī and Mahaḍ.

SVAYAMBHŪ

Self-manifested images
BeliefEach image appeared by itself, not carved
FormOften rough, with the shape of Gaṇeśa revealed in the stone
SindūrMany are thickly coated in orange sindūr
ExceptionMahaḍ’s image was found in a lake in 1690, the story says
TrunksMost turn left; Siddhaṭek’s turns right
JewelsSeveral have diamonds or rubies in eyes and navel

ZAPPY: Nobody carved them?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the belief. The stone itself took his form, and devotees found it.

All eight images are believed to be svayambhū: not carved by human hands but manifested by themselves. Many are rough stones coated thickly in orange sindūr, with eyes and navel set with jewels.

Most have the trunk turned to the left; Siddhaṭek’s Siddhivināyaka is the one with a right-turned trunk. The Wikipedia account notes that Mahaḍ’s image, said to have been found in a lake, sits awkwardly with the svayambhū claim.

ZAPPY: Every temple has a story?

PROF. BOLT: Every one. Together they’re a small Gaṇeśa Purāṇa you can drive round.

Each temple has its own legend. At Morgaon Gaṇeśa slays the demon Sindhu riding a peacock; at Siddhaṭek Viṣṇu gains siddhi to defeat Madhu and Kaiṭabha; at Pālī Gaṇeśa frees the boy Ballāḷa; at Mahaḍ the sage Gṛtsamada worships him.

At Theūr he recovers the wishing-jewel for the sage Kapila; Leṇyādri is where Pārvatī did penance for a son; at Ojhar he subdues Vighnāsura; at Rāñjaṇgāv Śiva worships him before defeating Tripura.

ZAPPY: Did the Peshwas build them?

PROF. BOLT: They rebuilt and enriched most. The worship was older; the stone halls are largely theirs.

See: The Gāṇapatya tradition

No exact founding dates are known for most of the temples. The Gāṇapatya saint Moraya Gosāvī is linked with Morgaon, Theūr and Siddhaṭek.

The current temples were mostly built or rebuilt under the Peshwas in the eighteenth century, as part of the royal patronage of Gaṇeśa that made Pune a Gaṇeśa city.

PLANNING THE CIRCUIT

Practical notes
TimeAbout three days by road
Start, endMorgaon
Nearest to PuneTheūr, about 22 km
Konkan legPālī and Mahaḍ, down the ghats
ClimbLeṇyādri: about 300 steps up a cave hill
Busy daysSaṅkaṣṭī, Aṅgārakī, Caturthī, Jayantī

ZAPPY: Is it hard?

PROF. BOLT: Easy by car, apart from Leṇyādri’s steps. Avoid Aṅgārakī unless you like crowds.

The circuit takes about three days by road. Theūr is the nearest temple to Pune; Pālī and Mahaḍ require a descent to the Konkan; Leṇyādri means climbing a few hundred steps to a cave.

Temples are busiest on Saṅkaṣṭī, especially Aṅgārakī, and at Gaṇeśa Caturthī and Jayantī.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Start at Morgaon, end at Morgaon. The circle is the point.

The Aṣṭavināyaka make a circle: the pilgrim returns to the first temple, as if to say that the end of every journey is where it began.

They also gather eight stories into a single journey through Maharashtra’s plains, ghats and coast.
