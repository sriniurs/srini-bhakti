FOREWORD

Above Junnar, in a hillside of Buddhist caves nearly two thousand years old, Gaṇeśa sits in Cave 7 as Girijātmaja, “son of the mountain’s daughter”.

This guide covers the caves, the legend of Pārvatī’s penance, the 283 steps and the image.

ZAPPY: Why ‘son of the mountain’s daughter’?

PROF. BOLT: Pārvatī is Girijā, daughter of the mountain Himālaya. Her son: Girijātmaja.

Leṇyādri, near Junnar in Pune district, is a hill with about thirty Buddhist rock-cut caves of the 1st to 3rd centuries CE. Cave 7, the largest excavation near Junnar, houses Gaṇeśa as Girijātmaja, son of Pārvatī.

It is the only Aṣṭavināyaka on a mountain and within Buddhist caves, reached by 283 steps. The caves are protected by the Archaeological Survey of India.

Tradition says Pārvatī did penance here for a son, and Gaṇeśa spent his childhood here.

ZAPPY: Monks built a Gaṇeśa temple?

PROF. BOLT: They built a monastery. Centuries later, Hindus made one of its halls Gaṇeśa’s home.

The Leṇyādri caves, about thirty in all, face south and were cut by Buddhist monks between the 1st and 3rd centuries CE. Caves 6 and 14 are chapels with stūpas; the rest are vihāras, dwellings for monks, with rock-cut cisterns.

Inscriptions in several caves record gifts by individuals and guilds; that in Cave 6 dates to the 2nd century.

ZAPPY: No electric lights?

PROF. BOLT: Just daylight through the doorway. Come in the morning.

Cave 7 was a vihāra, the largest excavation near Junnar, with a hall about 17 by 15 metres and twenty cells. Two of its central cells were merged to make Gaṇeśa’s shrine.

Its veranda pillars have octagonal shafts topped with animal figures. The temple relies on daylight; there is no electric lighting.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa grew up in a cave?

PROF. BOLT: In this legend, yes. A good place for a boy: steps, caves and a view.

See: Morgaon

Tradition says Pārvatī performed penance here for twelve years to obtain a son, and that Gaṇeśa was born here and grew up here for fifteen years. In the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, this is the Tretā-age avatāra, Mayūreśvara, who later defeated Sindhu.

Leṇyādri and Morgaon thus tell two halves of one story.

ZAPPY: Worshipped from behind?

PROF. BOLT: That’s what the sources say: the face is turned, and devotees see his back. A Leṇyādri mystery.

The image faces north with the trunk turned left; sources say devotees see it from the rear, and that it is less finely carved than the other seven.

The priests are Yajurvedī Brahmins. Gaṇeśa Jayantī and Caturthī draw large crowds.

ZAPPY: Is it a hard climb?

PROF. BOLT: Steady. Take water, take your time, and enjoy the view.

See: The Aṣṭavināyaka overview

The cave is reached by 283 stone steps in eight flights, built by devotees; tradition says they stand for the worldly pleasures Gaṇeśa overcame.

Ojhar, the next temple in the circuit, is close by.
