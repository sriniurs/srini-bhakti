MADHŪR

The Gaṇeśa a boy drew on a wall

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

On the Madhuvāhinī river near Kasaragod, the Madhūr temple is a Śiva temple whose Gaṇeśa outgrew it in fame: a figure drawn by a boy on the sanctum wall that, the story says, grew and grew.

This guide covers the legend, the Tipu Sultan story, and the great Mūḍappa sevā.

ZAPPY: Boḍḍajja?

PROF. BOLT: An affectionate nickname the boy gave him as the figure grew bigger and fatter. Devotees still use it fondly.

The Madhūr temple stands on the Madhuvāhinī (Mogral) river about 8 km from Kasaragod in north Kerala. Its main deity is Śiva as Madanantēśvara, but its fame comes from Siddhivināyaka, the Gaṇeśa on the southern wall of the sanctum.

Legend says a boy serving at the temple drew Gaṇeśa there in play, and the figure grew and grew until it became a great image; the boy called him “Boḍḍajja”.

It is counted among the six Gaṇeśa temples of ancient Tuḷunāḍu, and blends Kannada and Kerala cultures.

ZAPPY: Did Śiva mind?

PROF. BOLT: Not in the story. Father and son share the house.

Temple legend says a young boy serving at the temple playfully drew a picture of Gaṇeśa on the southern wall of the sanctum. Day by day it grew larger, becoming a living image; the boy called him “Boḍḍajja”, Bodda Gaṇeśa.

Gaṇeśa’s fame spread far beyond the region, though Śiva as Madanantēśvara remains the presiding deity.

ZAPPY: Did Tipu really spare it?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the local story, recorded by Kerala Tourism and the panchayat. The mark is there; the story is tradition.

Local lore says that during his 18th-century Malabar campaign Tipu Sultan came to destroy the temple, but after drinking water from its well changed his mind, leaving only a sword or dagger mark on the well-house, which is still shown.

The temple was long under the Kumbla rājas and is now administered by the Devaswom board.

ZAPPY: When is the next one?

PROF. BOLT: Whenever the temple and devotees can afford it. It’s a once-in-a-generation sight.

The temple’s most famous offering is the Mūḍappa sevā, in which the great Gaṇeśa image is entirely covered with appams made of rice and ghee. Because of its cost it is held only occasionally; Kerala Tourism notes one in 1992.

Locals say that all he needs is an appam to bless his devotees.

ZAPPY: Kerala or Karnataka?

PROF. BOLT: Both. Kasaragod sits between them, and Madhūr shows it.

The temple follows Kerala architecture, with a tiered roof and wooden carvings including scenes from the Rāmāyaṇa, while its worship mixes Kannada and Malayalam traditions.

It is counted among the six Gaṇeśa temples of ancient Tuḷunāḍu.
