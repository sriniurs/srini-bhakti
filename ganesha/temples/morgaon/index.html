AṢṬAVINĀYAKA 1 OF 8 · THE PEACOCK · FIRST AND LAST

Morgaon

Mayūreśvara, lord of the peacock · explained and illustrated

Lord of the peacock

HOW THIS WORKS: The village of peacocks, the image, the legend of Sindhu, the walled temple with four gates, Moraya Gosāvī, the Peshwas, and the festivals.

WHERE WE ARE

Morgaon, a village on the Karhā river south-east of Pune, is where the Aṣṭavināyaka begins and ends. Here Gaṇeśa is Mayūreśvara, Moreśvar in Marathi, lord of the peacock, who rode a peacock to defeat the demon Sindhu.

The Gāṇapatyas call it Gaṇeśa’s foremost seat on earth. This guide covers the place, the legend, the image, the fortress-like temple with its four gates, the saint Moraya Gosāvī, the Peshwas and the festivals. Start with the Aṣṭavināyaka overview for the whole circuit.

WHERE IT IS

The place and the road

The village. Morgaon lies about 55 km south-east of Pune in Baramati taluka, on the small river Karhā. “Mor” is Marathi for peacock: a village of peacocks.

Scripture. Both great Gāṇapatya texts praise it. The Mudgala Purāṇa gives it 22 chapters, and the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa counts it among Gaṇeśa’s three holiest places.

First and last. Pilgrims begin the Aṣṭavināyaka here and return here at the end. Many say a pilgrimage that does not close at Morgaon is not complete.

THE LEGEND OF SINDHU

Gaṇeśa on a peacock

The story

Gaṇeśa Purāṇa (Krīḍā Khaṇḍa) and temple tradition; tellings differ.

The demon Sindhu swallowed a bowl of amṛta, the nectar of immortality, and became impossible to kill. He oppressed the gods.
In the Tretā age Gaṇeśa was born to Pārvatī after her penance at Leṇyādri and grew up there.
To fight Sindhu he rode a peacock, a gift in the stories from his brother Skanda’s side, and so was called Mayūreśvara.
At Morgaon he cut the demon open and let the nectar flow out. Sindhu, without his nectar, fell.
Brahmā is said to have built the first shrine, and the Karhā river to have flowed from water spilled from his pot. Read the four yuga avatāras.

ZAPPY: Gaṇeśa on a peacock, not a mouse?

PROF. BOLT: In this age, Zappy. Each yuga gives him a different mount.

The peacock rider.

THE IMAGE

Mayūreśvara facing north

Posture. Seated, facing north, with four arms, three eyes and the trunk turned to the left.

Jewels. Diamonds are set in the eyes and the navel; a cobra’s hood rises above the head. Siddhi and Buddhi stand on either side.

Sindūr. Believed svayambhū, the image is thickly coated in sindūr, which is said to fall away roughly once a century, most recently in 1882 and before that in 1788.

The āratī. Tradition says Samartha Rāmadāsa composed “Sukhakartā Duḥkhahartā” after seeing this image.

ZAPPY: The coating falls off by itself?

PROF. BOLT: So the records say, Zappy. Once in a hundred years.

Jewelled eyes, a cobra’s hood.

THE TEMPLE

A walled shrine with four gates

Walls. The shrine stands inside tall stone walls with minaret-like towers at the four corners, looking almost like a fort.

Four gates. Each faces a direction and holds a Gaṇeśa form tied to an age and an aim of life: east, Ballāḷa and dharma; south, Vighneśa and artha; west, Cintāmaṇi and kāma; north, Mahāgaṇapati and mokṣa.

Courtyard. Two lamp towers, a six-foot mouse, śamī and bilva trees, and, unusually, a great Nandi. Legend says the Nandi was being carried to a Śiva temple and refused to go past Gaṇeśa.

Many gods. Smaller shrines round the courtyard hold many forms of Gaṇeśa and other deities.

ZAPPY: A Nandi at a Gaṇeśa temple?

PROF. BOLT: He stopped and wouldn’t move, Zappy. Family loyalty.

Four gates, four aims of life.

HISTORY

Saint and Peshwas

Date. Unknown. The temple is usually placed between the 14th and 17th centuries; the scholar Anne Feldhaus argues for the 17th, when Moraya Gosāvī made it famous.

Moraya Gosāvī. The Gāṇapatya saint worshipped here before settling at Chinchwad, and walked back to Morgaon regularly. Read the Gāṇapatya tradition.

The Peshwas. In the 18th century they gave land, cash and buildings; Moraya’s descendants, the Devs of Chinchwad, long managed the temple.

Today. The Chinchwad Devasthān Trust administers it.

ZAPPY: So Morgaon made Moraya famous, or Moraya made Morgaon?

PROF. BOLT: Both, Zappy. Saint and shrine grew together.

Saint first, kings after.

FESTIVALS

The year at Morgaon

Gaṇeśa Caturthī in Bhādrapada and Māghī Gaṇeśa Jayantī are the great festivals. The palanquin of Moraya Gosāvī comes from Chinchwad for the Bhādrapada festival.

Saṅkaṣṭī and Aṅgārakī bring crowds of devotees who fast until moonrise. Read our Saṅkaṣṭī series.

Vijayadaśamī and the lamp-lit nights of Kārtika are also kept.

Visiting. Timings and rules are set by the temple trust; check before you go.

ZAPPY: The palanquin walks all the way from Chinchwad?

PROF. BOLT: Carried by devotees, Zappy. The saint visits his lord every year.

The saint comes home.

WHY THE PEACOCK?

Pride carried and tamed

A proud bird. The peacock is beauty and pride. Gaṇeśa rides it, calm. Many read this as pride carried, not crushed: put to work for good. (Interpretation.)

Sindhu’s nectar. The demon wanted immortality only for himself. Gaṇeśa lets it flow out again: what is hoarded is released.

First and last. Starting and ending here makes the pilgrimage a circle. Every journey begins and ends with Gaṇeśa.

ZAPPY: So I start my exams with Gaṇeśa too?

PROF. BOLT: And end them with thanks, Zappy. That’s Morgaon in a sentence.

Begin and end here.

WORDS TO KNOW

The temple vocabulary of this guide

🛕 Svayambhū

Self-manifested: an image held to have appeared by itself.

🟠 Sindūr

The orange-red paste that coats many Gaṇeśa images.

🪔 Dīpamāḷā

A lamp tower of stone, lit with many lamps at festivals.

🌿 Dūrvā

The three-bladed grass dear to Gaṇeśa.

🙏 Āratī

Waving of lamps, with the song “Sukhakartā Duḥkhahartā”.

🌗 Caturthī · Saṅkaṣṭī

The bright and dark fourth days, sacred to Gaṇeśa.
