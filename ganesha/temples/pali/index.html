PĀLĪ

The Gaṇeśa named after a child

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

At Pālī in Raigad, Gaṇeśa is named after a child: Ballāḷeśvara, “Ballāḷa’s lord”, the only Aṣṭavināyaka known by a devotee’s name.

This guide tells Ballāḷa’s story, and describes Ḍhuṇḍhi Vināyaka, the temple shaped like the letter Śrī, and its bell from a Portuguese church.

ZAPPY: Why visit Ḍhuṇḍhi first?

PROF. BOLT: He’s the stone Ballāḷa’s father threw away. Tradition says you greet him before Ballāḷeśvara.

Pālī, in Raigad district between the Sarasgaḍ fort and the Ambā river, is the third Aṣṭavināyaka. Its Gaṇeśa is the only one known by a devotee’s name: Ballāḷeśvara, “Ballāḷa’s lord”.

Pilgrims first visit Ḍhuṇḍhi Vināyaka, the stone Ballāḷa’s father threw away, then the main temple, a stone building of 1760 shaped like the letter Śrī.

The image is dressed as a Brahmin, the form in which Gaṇeśa appeared to Ballāḷa.

ZAPPY: His own father?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, yes. And Gaṇeśa chose the boy’s side. That’s why the god has the child’s name.

In Pallīpur lived a merchant, Kalyāṇ Śeṭh, and his wife Indumatī. Their son Ballāḷa and his friends played at worship in the forest, using a stone as Gaṇeśa. When the children stayed out too long, the parents blamed Ballāḷa.

Kalyāṇ threw away the stone, beat his son and tied him to a tree. Gaṇeśa appeared as a Brahmin, freed and healed the boy, and at Ballāḷa’s request agreed to stay at Pālī for ever, as Ballāḷeśvara. The story is told in the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa’s Upāsanā Khaṇḍa.

ZAPPY: The thrown stone is worshipped too?

PROF. BOLT: First, in fact. Thrown away by an angry father, honoured by everyone since.

The stone that Kalyāṇ threw away is worshipped as Ḍhuṇḍhi Vināyaka in a west-facing shrine behind the main temple, unusual for a Gaṇeśa.

By custom, pilgrims worship Ḍhuṇḍhi Vināyaka before Ballāḷeśvara.

ZAPPY: Why diamonds in the eyes?

PROF. BOLT: Many Aṣṭavināyaka images have jewelled eyes. They catch the lamplight.

The image faces east with the trunk turned left, diamonds in its eyes and navel, and a mouse in front holding a modaka. It is said to resemble the shape of the mountain behind the temple.

It is dressed as a Brahmin, the form in which Gaṇeśa appeared to Ballāḷa. Unusually, the prasāda here is besan laḍḍu rather than modaka.

ZAPPY: A temple shaped like a letter?

PROF. BOLT: Śrī, the auspicious syllable. You’d see it best from the sky.

The original wooden temple was rebuilt in stone in 1760 under the Phaḍnavīs family, with lead mixed into the mortar. Its plan is shaped like the Devanāgarī letter Śrī, and it has two sanctums, 15 and 12 feet high.

The temple faces east, and after the winter solstice the rising sun falls on the image. Two lakes adjoin it, one reserved for the deity’s worship.

ZAPPY: A church bell in a Gaṇeśa temple?

PROF. BOLT: A trophy of the 1739 war. History hangs in the courtyard.

See: The Aṣṭavināyaka overview

The temple’s great bell was brought by Chimājī Appā, brother of Peshwa Bājīrāo I, after the Maratha victory over the Portuguese at Vasai in 1739; bells from Portuguese churches were offered at several Aṣṭavināyaka temples.

The same campaign is linked with the golden dome at Ojhar.
