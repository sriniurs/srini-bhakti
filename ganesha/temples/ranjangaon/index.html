RĀÑJAṆGĀV

The great Gaṇapati whom Śiva worshipped

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

The eighth Aṣṭavināyaka, Rāñjaṇgāv’s Mahāgaṇapati, is where Śiva himself is said to have worshipped Gaṇeśa before destroying the demon of the three cities.

This guide tells the legend, describes the image and the temple, and notes the story of a hidden twenty-armed image.

ZAPPY: Is this the last one?

PROF. BOLT: The eighth. Then you go back to Morgaon to finish.

Rāñjaṇgāv, about 50 km from Pune, is the eighth Aṣṭavināyaka. Its Gaṇeśa is Mahāgaṇapati, the great Gaṇapati, seated cross-legged and flanked by Riddhi and Siddhi.

Its legend says Śiva worshipped Gaṇeśa here before destroying Tripurāsura, the demon of the three cities. The town was once called Maṇipur.

After Rāñjaṇgāv, pilgrims return to Morgaon to complete the circuit.

ZAPPY: Even Śiva forgot?

PROF. BOLT: In this story. And his chariot broke. Lesson learned: Gaṇeśa first.

The temple’s legend tells that Śiva, setting out to destroy Tripurāsura and his three flying cities, worshipped Gaṇeśa here first. In a variant, Gaṇeśa broke the axle of Śiva’s chariot because Śiva had not honoured him first.

Having worshipped Gaṇeśa, Śiva destroyed the three cities. The town was then called Maṇipur.

ZAPPY: A hidden twenty-armed Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the local belief. Wikipedia calls it unsubstantiated. A good story, though.

The image is seated cross-legged, facing east, with a broad forehead and the trunk turned left, flanked by Riddhi and Siddhi.

Local belief holds that the true image, with ten trunks and twenty arms, lies hidden in a vault below; Wikipedia’s account calls this unsubstantiated.

ZAPPY: Jaya and Vijaya?

PROF. BOLT: Viṣṇu’s doorkeepers, guarding Gaṇeśa’s gate here.

The temple faces east, its main gate guarded by statues of Jaya and Vijaya, and is designed so that the sun’s rays fall on the deity during the sun’s southward course.

Peshwa Mādhavrāo built the stone sanctum, and in 1790 Anyābā Dev was authorised to worship the image. Its architecture is said to resemble that of the 9th–10th centuries, though this is unverified.

ZAPPY: Then home?

PROF. BOLT: Then Morgaon, then home. The circle has to close.

See: The Aṣṭavināyaka overview

Rāñjaṇgāv lies on the Pune–Ahilyanagar road, about 50 km from Pune, and Gaṇeśa Caturthī draws great crowds.

From here pilgrims return to Morgaon to complete the Aṣṭavināyaka circuit.
