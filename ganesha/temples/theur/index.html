THEŪR

The wish-jewel Gaṇeśa

GAṆEŚA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Closest of the eight to Pune, Theūr is the home of Cintāmaṇi, the wish-jewel Gaṇeśa, and the family deity of the Peshwa Mādhavrāo I.

This guide tells the jewel legend, the Peshwa story, and Moraya Gosāvī’s link with the temple.

Śrī Cintāmaṇaye namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why ‘Cintāmaṇi’?

PROF. BOLT: The wish-granting jewel, Zappy. Gaṇeśa won it back for a sage, and became the jewel himself.

ZAPPY: A Peshwa died here?

PROF. BOLT: Mādhavrāo I, young and ill, came to Theūr to be near his Gaṇeśa. His wife Ramābāī followed him in death.

Theūr, about 22 km from Pune near the meeting of the Mula–Mutha and Bhīmā rivers, is the fifth Aṣṭavināyaka and the closest to the city. Its Gaṇeśa is Cintāmaṇi, named after the wish-granting jewel.

It was the family deity of the Peshwa Mādhavrāo I, who renovated the temple and died here in 1772. Moraya Gosāvī often stopped here on his way between Chinchwad and Morgaon.

The place was once called Kadambanagar; a lake, Kadamba-tīrtha, lies behind the temple.

ZAPPY: What did Kapila do with the jewel?

PROF. BOLT: Gave it straight back to Gaṇeśa. Who needs a wishing jewel when you have the one who grants wishes?

Temple legend tells that the sage Kapila owned the Cintāmaṇi, a wish-granting jewel, which a greedy prince named Guṇa took from him. Gaṇeśa recovered it, and Kapila placed it round Gaṇeśa’s neck.

So Gaṇeśa here is Cintāmaṇi. The place was once called Kadambanagar.

ZAPPY: Why does the door face north if he faces east?

PROF. BOLT: An Aṣṭavināyaka puzzle. Several temples have such quirks.

The image faces east with the trunk turned left, its eyes set with a carbuncle and diamonds. The temple entrance faces north.

The main temple is attributed to Dharaṇīdhar Mahārāj Dev of Chinchwad; the wooden outer hall was built by the Peshwas.

ZAPPY: Why did he come here to die?

PROF. BOLT: To be with his Gaṇeśa. Theūr was his family’s god’s home.

The Peshwa Mādhavrāo I (1745–1772), whose family deity was Cintāmaṇi, renovated and extended the temple. Gravely ill, he came to Theūr and died here in 1772, still young.

His wife Ramābāī performed satī on 18 November 1772. The episode is remembered at the temple.

ZAPPY: Did Moraya Gosāvī stop here?

PROF. BOLT: Often, on his walks to Morgaon. Theūr was his halfway house.

See: The Gāṇapatya tradition

Moraya Gosāvī often visited Theūr on his journeys between Chinchwad and Morgaon, and the main temple is attributed to Dharaṇīdhar Mahārāj Dev of his line.

Devotees still make the Chinchwad–Morgaon pilgrimage by way of Theūr.

ZAPPY: If I only have a morning in Pune?

PROF. BOLT: Theūr. It’s the closest, and one of the loveliest.

See: The Aṣṭavināyaka overview

Theūr is the easiest Aṣṭavināyaka to reach from Pune, a short half-day trip. It is busiest on Saṅkaṣṭī and at Caturthī.

In the traditional order it is the fifth temple, after Mahaḍ.
