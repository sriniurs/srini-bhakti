HARIHARAPUTRA · DHARMA ŚĀSTĀ · SWAMIYE ŚARAṆAM

Ayyappa

The Lord of the Forest Hill · explained and illustrated

The lord of the forest hill

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Ayyappa is: son of Śiva and Mohinī, the foundling prince of Pandaḷam, the slayer of Mahiṣī, the friend of Vāvar, the seated yogī of Śabarimala and the married Śāstā of other shrines, and how his devotees keep their vow.

WHERE WE ARE

Every winter, from Mysuru to Malaysia, men in black or blue clothes, barefoot and bearded, greet each other as “Swāmi”: they are keeping the vow of Ayyappa, the god of the forest hill of Śabarimala. Few deities draw such crowds or ask such discipline.

This guide is about the god himself: his birth from Śiva and Mohinī, the Pandaḷam story, Mahiṣī, his Muslim friend Vāvar, how to read his image, his other forms as Dharma Śāstā, and the meaning of the vow. The temple and the pilgrimage route are in our Śabarimala guide.

SON OF HARI AND HARA

Śiva and Mohinī

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 8 (Mohinī and Śiva); the child’s birth as Ayyappa is Kerala and Tamil tradition.

When the gods and demons churned the ocean for nectar, Viṣṇu took the form of Mohinī, an enchantress, to divide it so that the gods received it.
Śiva asked to see this form. Seeing Mohinī, even the great ascetic was overwhelmed, the Bhāgavata says, and followed her.
The Purāṇa stops there. Kerala and Tamil tradition continue: from the union of Śiva and Viṣṇu-as-Mohinī was born a son, Hariharaputra, “son of Hari (Viṣṇu) and Hara (Śiva)”.
He was born to destroy a demoness who could be killed only by such a son, and was left by the Pampā river with a bell at his neck.
For devotees, his birth unites the two great streams of Hindu worship, Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava, in one child.

ZAPPY: Two fathers, in a way?

PROF. BOLT: Two great gods meeting, Zappy. The tradition chose to see harmony where others saw rivalry.

Hari and Hara in one child.

THE PRINCE OF PANDAḶAM

The foundling and the tiger’s milk

The story

The Pandaḷam legend, as told in Kerala; versions differ.

The childless king of Pandaḷam, hunting by the Pampā, heard a baby crying and found a radiant boy with a bell at his neck. A sage told him to raise the child; he named him Maṇikaṇṭha.
The queen then bore a son of her own. When the king chose Maṇikaṇṭha, now twelve, as heir, she and a scheming minister plotted against him: she pretended a fatal illness that only tiger’s milk could cure.
Maṇikaṇṭha went alone into the forest. There he met and killed the buffalo-demoness Mahiṣī (next page).
He came back riding a tigress, followed by tigers, the gods themselves in tiger form, to bring the milk. The plotters fell at his feet.
The boy revealed who he was and refused the throne. He asked the king to build him a temple where his arrow fell, on the hill of Śabarimala, and went there to sit in yoga.

ZAPPY: He brought the milk with the tiger attached!

PROF. BOLT: Exactly, Zappy. The tradition loves that detail.

He came back riding the tiger.

THE END OF MAHIṢĪ

Why only Hari and Hara’s son could do it

The story

Kerala tradition; the curse back-story is a later addition. Mahiṣī’s brother is Mahiṣa, slain by the Devī in the Devī Māhātmya.

After the Devī killed the buffalo-demon Mahiṣa, his sister Mahiṣī did tapas to avenge him and won a boon: only a son born of Śiva and Viṣṇu could kill her.
Sure that no such son could ever exist, she terrorised the gods.
Maṇikaṇṭha met her in the forest on the way to fetch the tiger’s milk. They fought near the river Aḻuta; he threw her down and danced on her.
Released from her demon body, she appeared as a beautiful woman (in a later story, a cursed celestial) and asked him to marry her. Vowed to celibacy, he refused.
He promised she would be honoured near him: in tradition she is Mālikappurattamma, whose shrine stands beside his at Śabarimala, waiting for the year no new pilgrim comes.

ZAPPY: She’s still waiting?

PROF. BOLT: The story says so, Zappy: he will marry her the year no first-time pilgrim arrives. That year never comes.

A promise never quite due.

VĀVAR, THE FRIEND

A shrine to a Muslim companion

Who he was. Vāvar (Vāvaru) is remembered in Kerala tradition as a Muslim warrior, or in another telling a saint from Arabia, who fought Ayyappa and then became his loyal companion and lieutenant.

The mosque at Erumēli. Pilgrims on the old route stop at the Vāvar mosque at Erumēli before the Śāstā temple there, and there is a Vāvar shrine near the eighteen steps on the hill.

Peṭṭa tuḷḷal. At Erumēli pilgrims dance with painted faces and wooden weapons, recalling the hunt against Mahiṣī, moving between the mosque and the temple.

History or legend? Scholars differ on whether Vāvar was a historical person; some see the legends as assembled from several sources. Devotees honour him regardless. (Sources differ.)

ZAPPY: A Muslim friend in a Hindu pilgrimage?

PROF. BOLT: Since long before anyone thought it unusual, Zappy. The pilgrims greet both.

Friendship at the foot of the hill.

READING HIS IMAGE

The yogī with the band round his knees

The seat. At Śabarimala he sits with knees raised, held by a cloth band (yogapaṭṭa), the posture of an ascetic in long meditation.

The hand. His right hand forms the cin-mudrā, thumb and forefinger joined: the gesture of teaching, the individual self joined to the universal. The left rests on his knee.

The bell. A bell at his neck recalls Maṇikaṇṭha, the baby found by the river.

Other forms. Riding a tiger with bow and arrow (the Pandaḷam story); on an elephant or a horse in other regions; as a young hunter. His whole form is youth and restraint, a naiṣṭhika brahmacārī, lifelong celibate, at Śabarimala.

ZAPPY: Why the band on his knees?

PROF. BOLT: To hold a meditation posture for a very long time, Zappy. He is still sitting.

Youth held in yoga.

DHARMA ŚĀSTĀ

One god, many shrines and stages

👶 Five stages

A Kerala tradition links five Śāstā shrines to five stages of life: the child at Kuḷattūppuḻa, the youth at Āryaṅkāvu, the householder at Accaṅkōvil, the yogī at Śabarimala and the forest-dweller at Kāntamala. (Tradition.)

💍 The married Śāstā

At Accaṅkōvil he sits with two consorts, Pūrṇā and Puṣkalā, and a son, Satyaka. The celibate of Śabarimala and the householder of Accaṅkōvil are both Śāstā.

🐎 Aiyanār

In Tamil villages Aiyanār, a guardian god with Pūrṇā and Puṣkalā and great clay horses, is often identified with Śāstā; this is one reason for Ayyappa’s huge Tamil following.

📜 Older roots

“Śāstā”, “teacher, ruler”, is an old title; some historians link it to the Buddha, called Śāstā in Pali texts, and the Kerala government once suggested Śabarimala may have been a Buddhist site. (Scholarly views.)

ZAPPY: Married at one temple, celibate at another?

PROF. BOLT: The same god at different stages of life, Zappy. The tradition saw no contradiction.

One Śāstā, every stage of life.

THE VOW

Black clothes, a mālā and a bundle

Taking the mālā. The vow (vratam) begins when a guru-swāmi places a tulasī or rudrākṣa mālā round the devotee’s neck. Traditionally it lasts a maṇḍala, 41 days.

The life. Black or blue clothes, bare feet, no shaving, vegetarian food once or twice a day, celibacy, no alcohol or tobacco, prayer morning and evening, and courtesy to all: every person is addressed as Swāmi, a woman as Māḷikappuram.

The irumuḍi. Before the journey the irumuḍi, a two-part cloth bundle, is packed: one side holds offerings, including a coconut filled with ghee for the Lord’s abhiṣeka; the other the pilgrim’s food. It is carried on the head.

The call. All the way the pilgrims chant “Swāmiye Śaraṇam Ayyappa”, “Lord, you are my refuge”. Only those carrying the irumuḍi may climb the eighteen holy steps. The route and the temple are in our Śabarimala guide.

ZAPPY: Forty-one days without shaving!

PROF. BOLT: And without anger, Zappy, which is harder. The beard is easy.

Discipline for the whole season.

WHO MAY CLIMB

The tradition and the debate

The custom. At Śabarimala, by long custom, girls and women of child-bearing age (traditionally 10 to 50) do not climb to the shrine; younger girls and older women do. Devotees explain this by the deity’s vow of celibacy at this particular shrine.

Elsewhere. The restriction applies to Śabarimala only. At Accaṅkōvil, at the other Śāstā temples, and at Ayyappa temples across India, women worship freely, and many women keep a vow of their own.

The court case. In September 2018 the Supreme Court of India ruled that women of all ages may enter. Large protests followed, and in 2019 the court referred the wider questions to a larger bench.

Our stance. This is a living dispute about tradition, faith and equality. We record the custom, the reasoning given for it and the legal history, and take no side. Traditions and opinions differ.

ZAPPY: What do you think, Professor?

MAKARAVILAKKU

The light on the hill

When. The season’s climax falls at Makara Saṅkrānti, mid-January, when the sun enters Capricorn.

The procession. The tiruvābharaṇam, sacred ornaments kept by the Pandaḷam royal family, is carried in procession over the hills to adorn the deity on that evening.

The light. At dusk, after the ornamented worship, a flame appears on the hill of Ponnambalamēḍu across the valley: the Makaravilakku. The temple board has acknowledged that the flame is lit by people as a ritual; devotees also watch for a bright star, the Makara Jyoti.

The lullaby. Each night the temple closes with Harivarāsanam, a gentle hymn sung as the god’s lullaby, after which the doors shut.

ZAPPY: People light it?

PROF. BOLT: As a rite, Zappy, the way priests light a lamp. Knowing that doesn’t make the moment smaller for those who watch.

A flame across the valley.

WHAT AYYAPPA TEACHES

Discipline, unity, equality on the path

Discipline. The vow asks for weeks of self-control before a single step is climbed. The pilgrimage is the vow, not only the destination. (Interpretation.)

Equality on the path. Rich and poor wear the same black, walk barefoot and call each other Swāmi; caste and status are set aside for the season.

Unity. Son of Śiva and Viṣṇu, friend of Vāvar, identified with Tamil Aiyanār: his worship gathers what elsewhere is kept apart.

Refuge. “Swāmiye Śaraṇam” is a whole theology in two words: you are my refuge. The hardship of the trek is meant to make that real.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Ayyappa?

PROF. BOLT: Keep one small vow for forty-one days, Zappy, and call everyone you meet “Swāmi” in your heart.

Every pilgrim is Swāmi.
