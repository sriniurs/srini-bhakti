Brahmā

The Lotus-Born Creator · explained and illustrated

The lotus-born

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Brahmā is and is not: his birth from the lotus, his four faces and his time, the stories of why he has so few temples, his role as giver of boons, his family of sages, and where he is worshipped.

WHERE WE ARE

In the trio of great gods, Brahmā creates, Viṣṇu preserves and Śiva dissolves. Yet the creator has hardly any temples, no great festival and no sect of his own. Who is he, and why does India honour him so quietly?

This guide separates Brahmā from Brahman, tells his birth and his famous mistakes, reads his image, measures his staggering day, and shows the places where he is still worshipped. It is one of our guides to the gods; Viṣṇu and Śiva have their own in Who Is Viṣṇu and Who Is Śiva.

BRAHMĀ IS NOT BRAHMAN

Two words that are easy to mix up

Brahmā (with a long final ā, masculine) is a god: a person with a body, a consort, a vehicle and a lifespan, who shapes the forms of the world.

Brahman (neuter, short a) is the Absolute of the Upaniṣads: not a person, not born, the reality behind all gods and all things.

A third word, brāhmaṇa, means a priest or a class of Vedic prose texts; Brahmā also names the chief priest who silently oversees a Vedic sacrifice. In every case the root is bṛh, “to grow, expand”.

The Purāṇas say Brahmā himself is born and dies: when his hundred years end, the universe dissolves and a new Brahmā arises. Brahman never comes or goes.

ZAPPY: So Brahmā is not the biggest God?

PROF. BOLT: He is a great god, Zappy, but a creature too. The Upaniṣads point past him to Brahman.

A god who is born and dies.

THE LOTUS FROM VIṢṆU’S NAVEL

How Brahmā was born

The story

Viṣṇu-centred Purāṇas (Bhāgavata, book 3) and the Mahābhārata; other texts give other origins (next panel).

Between creations there is only the dark ocean. On it, Viṣṇu sleeps on the coiled serpent Śeṣa, with all worlds folded inside him.
When it is time, a lotus grows from his navel, on a long stalk, and opens. Seated inside is Brahmā, alone, not knowing where he came from.
He climbs down the stalk looking for its root and cannot find it; he returns and sits in meditation, and hears the word “tapa”, austerity.
After long tapas the Lord shows himself, and Brahmā receives the knowledge to create. Turning to see in every direction, he grows four faces.
From his four mouths come the four Vedas, and he sets about making the worlds: the lotus becomes the map of the universe.

ZAPPY: He didn’t know where he came from?

PROF. BOLT: Even the creator had to begin with a question, Zappy, and an answer that came only after silence.

The creator began with a question.

OTHER ORIGINS

Prajāpati and the golden embryo

Prajāpati. In the Vedic Brāhmaṇas the creator is Prajāpati, “lord of creatures”, who creates by tapas and by sacrificing himself. Later texts identify Brahmā with him.

Hiraṇyagarbha. The Ṛgveda (10.121) praises the “golden embryo” that arose in the beginning, and asks again and again, “to which god shall we offer worship?” Purāṇas say Brahmā was born from this golden egg floating on the waters, and split it to make heaven and earth.

From Śiva or Devī. Śaiva texts say Brahmā and Viṣṇu came from Śiva; Śākta texts from the Devī. Each tradition puts its own supreme god at the root of the lotus.

A secondary creator. Almost all agree he makes the forms of the universe rather than its ultimate stuff. Traditions differ about who stands behind him.

ZAPPY: Everyone claims to be his parent!

PROF. BOLT: That tells you he matters, Zappy. Every school wanted the creator in its family tree.

Many tellings, one creator.

FOUR FACES, FOUR VEDAS

Reading his image

Four faces. Looking north, south, east and west, he sees all of creation at once. The faces are matched with the four Vedas, the four directions, the four ages and the four classes of beings. (Traditional readings.)

The fifth head. Many stories say he once had a fifth face, looking upward, which spoke in pride and was cut off by Śiva in the form of Bhairava; that story belongs to our Kāla Bhairava guide.

Four hands. The Vedas (book), a rosary (time and prayer), a water-pot (the waters from which all arises) and a sacrificial ladle (ritual). He is usually bearded, an elder, red or golden.

The swan. His mount is the haṃsa, said to separate milk from water: the power to tell the real from the unreal, needed by anyone who creates. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: Why a beard?

PROF. BOLT: He is Pitāmaha, the grandfather of the worlds, Zappy. Grandfathers get beards.

The grandfather who sees all four ways.

THE DAY OF BRAHMĀ

A clock for the universe

The yugas. Four ages, Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara and Kali, in the ratio 4:3:2:1, together make a mahāyuga of 4,320,000 human years.

One day. A thousand mahāyugas make one day of Brahmā, a kalpa: about 4.32 billion years. His night is as long; during it the worlds rest.

His life. Brahmā lives a hundred of his own years, after which the universe is dissolved entirely; then another Brahmā is born. Tradition says we are in the first day of the second half of his life.

Strangely modern. Readers often note that a day of Brahmā is close to the age of the earth estimated by geologists, about 4.5 billion years. The match is a coincidence of scale, not a scientific claim of the Purāṇas. (Note.)

ZAPPY: We live in Brahmā’s afternoon?

PROF. BOLT: His fifty-first year, morning of the first day, by the tradition, Zappy. Plenty of time left.

A day of 4.32 billion years.

THE COLUMN OF LIGHT

Why the ketakī flower is not offered to Śiva

The story

Śiva Purāṇa, Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā; the story of Mahāśivarātri’s liṅgodbhava (see Mahāśivarātri).

Brahmā and Viṣṇu once argued over which of them was supreme. Between them appeared a blazing column of light with no visible beginning or end.
They agreed that whoever found its end would be greater. Brahmā became a swan and flew upward; Viṣṇu became a boar and dug downward.
Viṣṇu searched for ages, found no bottom, and returned to admit it. Brahmā found no top either, but on the way met a ketakī flower drifting down, and persuaded it to say he had reached the summit.
Śiva emerged from the column, angry at the lie. He declared that Brahmā would have no worship of his own on earth, and that the ketakī would never be offered to Śiva.
Viṣṇu, who told the truth, was honoured. Even today ketakī (screw-pine) flowers are kept away from Śiva’s worship.

ZAPPY: One lie cost him all his temples?

PROF. BOLT: In this telling, Zappy. The story is really about the endless God and a creator’s pride.

The truth-teller is honoured.

SĀVITRĪ’S CURSE AND PUSHKAR

A sacrifice that could not wait

The story

Padma Purāṇa, Sṛṣṭi Khaṇḍa (Pushkar tradition); details differ.

Brahmā wished to perform a great sacrifice on earth. He let a lotus fall, and where its petals landed, lakes appeared: the place became Puṣkara, “lotus”.
The sacrifice had to begin at the auspicious moment, and a sacrificer must sit with his wife. His wife Sāvitrī was late, still adorning herself.
The priests urged him on, and Brahmā married a local maiden, Gāyatrī, so that the rite could start on time.
When Sāvitrī arrived and saw another woman in her place, she cursed Brahmā: he would be worshipped nowhere on earth except Pushkar.
She then went up to the hill above the lake, where her own temple stands; Gāyatrī’s is on the facing hill. Pushkar’s Brahmā temple is the most famous in India.

ZAPPY: Two curses for one god!

PROF. BOLT: Two stories with one lesson, Zappy: even a creator must keep his word and his promises at home.

Pushkar keeps his worship alive.

THE GIVER OF BOONS

Why demons prayed to him

🦁 Hiraṇyakaśipu

Asked to die neither by man nor beast, by day nor night, inside nor outside. Viṣṇu came as Narasiṃha at dusk, on the threshold, and found the gap.

👑 Rāvaṇa

Asked to be unkillable by gods, demons and spirits, not bothering to name humans: so Viṣṇu came as Rāma, a man.

🐃 Mahiṣa

Asked that no man should kill him; the gods’ combined power became the Devī who did. See Devī Māhātmya.

⚖️ Why he gives

Brahmā rewards tapas fairly, whoever performs it. The stories teach that every boon has a limit, and that pride hides the gap.

ZAPPY: Why give boons to demons?

PROF. BOLT: Because they earned them by effort, Zappy. Brahmā is fair; he isn’t foolish. Every boon had a loophole for dharma.

Effort is rewarded; pride finds the gap.

HIS FAMILY

Sarasvatī and the mind-born sons

📖 Sarasvatī

Speech and knowledge are the creator’s power: without the word, nothing can be named into being. See Sarasvatī.

🧠 Mind-born sons

From his mind came the great sages: Marīci, Atri, Aṅgiras, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, Vasiṣṭha, Bhṛgu and others, ancestors of the gotras (family lines).

🎵 Nārada and Dakṣa

Nārada, the wandering sage with the vīṇā, carries news and devotion between worlds; Dakṣa fathered many of the gods’ wives, Satī among them.

🧒 The Kumāras

Sanaka and his brothers refused to create and remained boy-ascetics forever: the creator’s children who chose renunciation.

ZAPPY: Atri is Datta’s father!

PROF. BOLT: And Brahmā is Datta’s grandfather, Zappy. See Datta Jayantī. Most Hindu family trees climb back to his sons.

Every gotra climbs back to him.

WHERE HE IS WORSHIPPED

Pushkar, Kumbakōṇam and every temple wall

Pushkar. On the sacred lake in Rajasthan, the Brahmā temple with its red spire and swan emblem is the best-known; Kārtika Pūrṇimā brings the Pushkar fair and a great bath.

Other temples. Khedbrahma in Gujarat, Asotra in Rajasthan, and the Brahmā shrine at Kumbakōṇam in Tamil Nadu (see our Kumbakōṇam guide); Thailand’s Erawan shrine in Bangkok honours him as Phra Phrom.

On temple walls. In South Indian Śiva temples Brahmā usually stands in a niche on the north wall of the sanctum, one of the guardians of the directions; Hoysaḷa temples carve him often.

In daily life. The hour before sunrise is brāhma muhūrta, prized for study and prayer; marriages and sacrifices call on him as a witness; the gotra one names at every ritual traces back to his sons.

ZAPPY: So Brahmā is everywhere after all?

PROF. BOLT: In niches, in mornings and in family names, Zappy. Quiet, but everywhere.

Quiet, but everywhere.

WHAT BRAHMĀ TEACHES

Creation, humility and the next morning

Creation is work. Brahmā creates by tapas, effort and attention, not by magic. Every maker, from a potter to a parent, shares his task. (Interpretation.)

Even the creator is humbled. He begins not knowing his origin; he loses a head to pride and his temples to a lie. The tradition does not flatter its gods.

Creation renews. Each night of Brahmā the worlds rest; each morning they begin again. The cycle is a comfort as much as a cosmology.

Why so few temples? Beyond the curses, historians point to the rise of devotion to Viṣṇu, Śiva and the Devī, who each took over his role in their own theology. Both explanations can stand. (Sources differ.)

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Brahmā?

PROF. BOLT: Wake once in brāhma muhūrta, Zappy, and make something with your own hands that day.

Make with care and humility.
