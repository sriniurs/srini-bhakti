THE EAGLE AND THE BULL · THE GODS’ VEHICLES

Garuḍa and Nandi

The Eagle and the Bull Who Carry the Gods · explained and illustrated

Facing their Lords

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Garuḍa and Nandi are: how each came to serve, their stories, why each sits facing the sanctum, their great images from Tirumala to Chāmuṇḍi Hill, and what a vāhana teaches about devotion.

WHERE WE ARE

Walk into almost any Viṣṇu temple and you pass Garuḍa, the eagle-man, kneeling with folded hands. Walk into any Śiva temple and you pass Nandi, the bull, lying calmly with his gaze fixed on the liṅga. They are the two most famous vāhanas, the vehicles of the gods, and each is a god and a devotee in his own right.

This guide tells how each came to serve, their best-known stories, why they face their Lords, the great images people travel to see, including Mysuru’s own Nandi on Chāmuṇḍi Hill, and what they teach about devotion. It is one of our guides to the gods.

WHAT IS A VĀHANA?

More than transport

The word. Vāhana means “that which carries”. Each deity has one, and it often appears on the deity’s banner (dhvaja) too: Viṣṇu is Garuḍadhvaja, Śiva Vṛṣabhadhvaja, “bull-bannered”.

A character, not furniture. The great vāhanas have their own births, stories and temples. Garuḍa and Nandi are worshipped before the main deity in many temples.

A meaning. Many read the vāhana as the force the god masters, or the devotee’s mind made steady enough to carry the divine. The bull is strength and dharma; the eagle is speed and the Vedas. (Interpretations.)

In procession. At temple festivals the processional image rides a Garuḍa or a Nandi vāhana through the streets on set nights: Garuḍa Sevā at Tirumala, Nandi vāhana at Nañjanagūḍu.

ZAPPY: So Nandi isn’t just a bull?

PROF. BOLT: He is a sage, a gatekeeper and a teacher who chose to be a bull, Zappy. Wait till you read his story.

The carrier is a devotee.

GARUḌA STEALS THE AMṚTA

To free his mother

The story

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan (Āstīka section). How Vinatā lost a wager to Kadru is told in our Nāga Pañcamī guide; Garuḍa’s elder brother Aruṇa in Ratha Saptamī.

Garuḍa hatched blazing like fire and found his mother Vinatā a slave to her sister Kadru and Kadru’s serpent sons.
The serpents named their price for her freedom: bring us the amṛta, the nectar of immortality, from heaven.
Garuḍa flew to heaven, scattered the gods, passed through a ring of fire, a spinning wheel of blades and two guardian serpents, and seized the pot of amṛta without tasting a drop.
He set it on a bed of darbha grass before the serpents, freeing his mother. While they went to bathe before drinking, Indra snatched it back. The serpents licked the grass where it had stood and split their tongues on its sharp blades.
That, says the story, is why snakes have forked tongues, why darbha grass is holy, and why Garuḍa and the serpents remain enemies.

ZAPPY: He carried the nectar and never sipped it?

PROF. BOLT: Not a drop, Zappy. That restraint is what Viṣṇu noticed next.

He held immortality and didn’t drink.

THE BARGAIN WITH VIṢṆU

How the eagle became a vehicle

The story

Mahābhārata, Ādi Parvan.

On his way back with the amṛta, Garuḍa met Viṣṇu, who was pleased that he had not drunk it and offered him a boon.
Garuḍa asked to sit above Viṣṇu and to be immortal without the nectar. Viṣṇu granted both, and asked a boon in return: be my vehicle. Garuḍa agreed, and to keep his first wish Viṣṇu placed him on his banner, above himself.
Indra then struck Garuḍa with his thunderbolt. Garuḍa, unhurt, let fall a single feather out of respect for the weapon, and Indra offered friendship.
Garuḍa promised the nectar would not reach the serpents; Indra granted that serpents would be his food. The bird who freed his mother became the eternal carrier of the Lord.
In the Vaiṣṇava tradition he is honoured as the first servant, Periya Tiruvaḍi, “the great holy feet”, in Tamil.

ZAPPY: He sits above Viṣṇu and below him?

PROF. BOLT: On the flag above, under his feet below, Zappy. Love finds a way to keep both promises.

The servant above and below.

HONOURING GARUḌA

Pillar, sevā and protection

Facing the Lord. In Vaiṣṇava temples a shrine or pillar with Garuḍa, kneeling and folding his hands, faces the sanctum; the temple flag-pole (dhvajastambha) flies the Garuḍa flag at festivals.

Garuḍa Sevā. On the fifth night of the Tirumala Brahmotsava, Veṅkaṭeśvara rides the golden Garuḍa vāhana, the most crowded procession of the festival; Kāñcīpuram and Mēlukōṭe have famous Garuḍa Sevās too.

Against snakes and poison. As the enemy of serpents, Garuḍa is invoked against snakebite and poison; Garuḍa Pañcamī in Śrāvaṇa is kept by some, especially in the South, on or near Nāga Pañcamī.

His Purāṇa. The Garuḍa Purāṇa is Viṣṇu’s teaching to Garuḍa, on cosmology, ritual and what happens after death.

ZAPPY: He fights snakes and is worshipped on Nāga Pañcamī?

PROF. BOLT: A day to make peace with both, Zappy. Hindu calendars like balance.

The Lord’s first servant.

NANDI, SON OF ŚILĀDA

The boy who would not die

The story

Śiva Purāṇa and Liṅga Purāṇa; tellings differ in detail.

The sage Śilāda prayed to Śiva for a son who would never die. Śiva granted it.
As Śilāda prepared a sacrificial ground, a shining boy appeared from it, already marked with Śiva’s signs. Śilāda named him Nandi, “joy”.
When the boy was seven, two visiting sages remarked that he would not live past his eighth year. Undismayed, Nandi went to the banks of a river (the Narmadā in many tellings) and began intense tapas.
Śiva appeared, embraced him, and made him free of death, the chief of his attendants (gaṇas) and the gatekeeper of Kailāsa.
Nandi asked only one thing more: to be near Śiva always. Śiva made him his vehicle, his banner and his doorkeeper.

ZAPPY: He was supposed to die at eight?

PROF. BOLT: And chose tapas instead of fear, Zappy. Śiva answered with a whole new life.

Fear turned into devotion.

NANDI AND RĀVAṆA

The gatekeeper’s curse

The story

Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa; tellings differ.

Rāvaṇa, flying north in his chariot Puṣpaka, found it could go no further over Kailāsa. At the gate stood Nandi, telling him Śiva and Pārvatī were within and no one could pass.
Rāvaṇa laughed at the gatekeeper’s bull-like face and behaved restlessly, like a monkey, as he waited.
Nandi, offended for his Lord’s home, cursed him: beings with faces like monkeys would one day destroy him and his kingdom.
Rāvaṇa went on to try to lift Kailāsa itself, and was pinned beneath it until he sang Śiva’s praise; that is another story.
The curse came true when Hanumān set Laṅkā ablaze and the monkey army crossed the sea. See Hanumān in Sundara.

ZAPPY: Never insult a gatekeeper!

PROF. BOLT: Or anyone, Zappy. But especially not the one who guards what you love.

Mock the gate, lose the city.

NANDI IN THE TEMPLE

Facing the liṅga, listening at the ear

His gaze. Nandi lies in a pavilion (Nandi maṇḍapa) on the axis of the sanctum, looking straight at the liṅga. He is read as the devoted soul whose whole attention rests on God.

Do not cross. Devotees avoid walking between Nandi and the liṅga; many look at the liṅga first between Nandi’s horns.

The whisper. Many devotees whisper their prayer into Nandi’s ear, trusting the gatekeeper to pass it on; some first ask his permission to enter.

Pradoṣa. At every Pradoṣa, Nandi receives his own abhiṣeka first, because Śiva danced between his horns that evening. In Kannada he is Basava, and a decorated bull is still called a basava in villages.

ZAPPY: Why look through his horns?

PROF. BOLT: To see the Lord the way his best devotee sees him, Zappy.

See God through a devotee’s eyes.

THE GREAT NANDIS

From Chāmuṇḍi Hill to Thañjāvūr

Sources differ on the Chāmuṇḍi Nandi’s date: most credit Dodda Devarāja Woḍeyar in the seventeenth century; one encyclopaedia caption calls it far older.

⛰️ Chāmuṇḍi Hill, Mysuru

Half-way up the 1,000-step stairway, a monolith about 16 feet high and 24–25 feet long, dark with oil from daily worship. Commonly credited to Dodda Devarāja Woḍeyar (r. 1659–73), who also laid the steps. See Chāmuṇḍeśvarī.

🏙️ Basavanagudi, Bengaluru

The Doḍḍa Basavana Guḍi, built in 1537 by Kempe Gowḍa, founder of Bengaluru, around a huge granite Nandi; a groundnut fair (Kaḍalekāyi Parishe) is held there each Kārtika.

🪨 Lepākṣi

A giant Nandi carved from a single rock near the Vīrabhadra temple, among the largest in India. See Lepākṣi.

🛕 Thañjāvūr and Hampi

The great Nandi of the Bṛhadīśvara temple and Hampi’s monolithic bull on the bazaar street. See Thañjāvūr and Hampi.

ZAPPY: Our Nandi is one of the biggest?

PROF. BOLT: One of the great three, Zappy, and the most climbed-to.

Mysuru’s Nandi on the stairway.

NANDI THE TEACHER

Nandikeśvara, dance and the Siddhas

🎭 Dance and gesture

A treatise on gesture, the Abhinaya Darpaṇa, is attributed to Nandikeśvara and still taught in Bharatanāṭyam; tradition says he learnt dance by watching Śiva’s.

🧘 Teacher of yogīs

In the Tamil Śaiva Siddhānta tradition Nandi is the first guru of the Siddhas; his pupils included Tirumūlar and Patañjali.

🥁 Drummer of the dance

In images of Śiva’s dance Nandi often plays the drum; at Pradoṣa he keeps the rhythm.

🚩 The Nandi flag

A seated bull on a flag has become the emblem of Śaivism in Sri Lanka and beyond, first hoisted in 1998.

ZAPPY: A bull wrote a dance book?

PROF. BOLT: Nandikeśvara did, by tradition, Zappy. When a bull devotes himself to Śiva, he learns to dance.

The bull who taught dance.

WHAT THEY TEACH

Strength and speed in service

Two kinds of devotion. Garuḍa is speed and action, ready to go anywhere at once; Nandi is stillness, waiting at the door for ages. Devotion needs both. (Interpretation.)

Greatness in service. Both could have been gods of their own; both chose to carry. The Gītā’s ideal of action without pride fits them well.

Restraint. Garuḍa held the nectar and did not drink; Nandi guarded the door and did not let even Rāvaṇa through. Power that holds back is the theme of both stories.

Facing the Lord. Their images ask every visitor: what is your attention turned towards?
