GODS · THE MOTHERS WHO GUARD THE VILLAGE

Grāma Devatās

Mothers of the Village · explained and illustrated

Mother of the village

HOW THIS WORKS: The grāma devatās of Mysuru and Karnataka: Mārammā, Durgamma, Ellamma, Kariyamma and the countless village mothers; their stone and pot forms, the tambiṭṭu lamp, the jātre with fire-walking and processions, their power over rain and epidemics, the guardian gods at the boundary, and how they meet the great gods of the Purāṇas.

WHERE WE ARE

Drive through any village around Mysuru and before the big temple, before the school, often at the very entrance, you will find a small shrine, sometimes just a stone smeared with turmeric and kuṅkuma under a neem tree. Inside is the village’s own mother: Mārammā, Durgamma, Kariyamma, Ellamma.

These are the grāma devatās, the village deities. For most of India’s history, and for millions today, they are the gods people turn to first. This guide explains who they are and how they are worshipped. For the great goddess of Mysuru, see Cāmuṇḍeśvarī.

WHO ARE THE GRĀMA DEVATĀS?

Guardians of one place

Local. A grāma devatā belongs to one village or town. She protects its people, fields and cattle; people from other places may not know her name.

Mother and guardian. In South India she is usually a goddess, called amma (“mother”). She is gentle to devotees and fierce to wrongdoers and to disease.

Two faces. If honoured, she guards against epidemics, drought and harm; if neglected, it is believed, she can let them in.

First and closest. Families who visit great temples on pilgrimage still go to the village Amma for daily needs: a sick child, a failed rain, a wedding.

ZAPPY: So she’s like the village’s own mother?

PROF. BOLT: That’s exactly what amma means, Zappy.

The village’s own mother.

THE AMMAS OF KARNATAKA

Many names

🌧️ Mārammā

Probably the most widespread village goddess of old Mysore. In Tamil country she is Mariyamman, goddess of rain and of fevers; Sirsi’s famous Mārikāmbā is a great form of her.

🛡️ Durgamma

The village Durgā, guardian against danger.

🌿 Ellamma

“Mother of all”, worshipped widely in North Karnataka; her great temple is at Savadatti.

🐯 Others

Kariyamma, Huliyamma (“tiger mother”), Cauḍamma, Kālamma, Annamma, Pattaladamma and many more, often named for a place or story.

ZAPPY: Huliyamma, tiger mother!

PROF. BOLT: She protected the village from tigers, Zappy, in the old days.

Every village, her own name.

HOW SHE IS SHOWN

Stone, pot and face

Unshaped stone. Often she is a simple stone or termite mound, covered in turmeric and kuṅkuma, with eyes added in silver or paint.

The pot. A decorated pot (kalaśa or karaga) can carry her presence, especially in festivals when she is taken through the streets.

Face and mask. A metal face (mukha) or a head alone is common; in some stories only her head is worshipped.

Open shrines. Many shrines are small, under a tree, with no roof at all; some have grown into large temples.

ZAPPY: Why no full statue?

PROF. BOLT: She’s older than temple sculpture, Zappy. The stone was always enough.

Simple form, great power.

WORSHIPPING THE AMMA

Turmeric, neem and lamps

🟡 Turmeric and kuṅkuma

The Amma’s colours; she is bathed in turmeric water and marked with kuṅkuma.

🌿 Neem

Neem leaves are her plant, used to cool fevers; in the hot months devotees carry pots of turmeric water with neem.

🪔 Tambiṭṭu ārati

Lamps made of rice flour and jaggery, carried on the head by women in procession, then shared as prasāda.

🍋 Lemons, bangles, saris

Lemons, green bangles, saris and a cool drink are offered, as to a beloved married woman of the house.

ZAPPY: A lamp you can eat afterwards!

PROF. BOLT: Tambiṭṭu, Zappy. Best prasāda of the jātre.

A mother’s gifts.

THE JĀTRE

The village festival

Once a year or more. Each Amma has her jātre, often after harvest or before the rains, lasting days. Relatives come home, fairs spring up, the whole village eats together.

Procession. The goddess, in her pot or image, is carried through every street and to the boundaries, to protect the whole village.

Fire-walking. At many jātres devotees walk over a bed of glowing coals (koṇḍa) in fulfilment of vows.

Vows. Devotees carry lamps or pots, roll around the temple, offer hair, or take part in dances, to thank her for a cure or a child.

ZAPPY: Walking on fire!

PROF. BOLT: Only after vows and preparation, Zappy, and never as a game.

The village comes home.

RAIN AND EPIDEMICS

Mother against disease

Mārammā and the fevers. Mārammā, like Tamil Mariyamman, is linked with rain and with diseases such as smallpox, measles and chickenpox; a fever with rash was said to be her presence.

Cooling her. So she was “cooled” with neem, turmeric water and cooling foods; patients were kept on neem leaves.

Plague and cholera. In the epidemics of the 19th and early 20th centuries, many Karnataka villages held special jātres for the Amma and built new shrines.

Today. Vaccines and medicine have changed this; but the cool drink and neem for the goddess in summer remain.

ZAPPY: People thought the disease was the goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Her presence, Zappy, and also her protection against it. A way to face fear.

Mother in hard times.

OFFERINGS, OLD AND NEW

A changing tradition

Blood offerings. Village goddesses were traditionally offered animals: chickens, goats and, at some great jātres, a male buffalo.

The law. Karnataka’s Prevention of Animal Sacrifices Act (1959) bans animal sacrifice in places of worship; enforcement and practice vary, and the issue is still debated.

Substitutes. Many shrines now offer a pumpkin (kūṣmāṇḍa) split and marked with kuṅkuma, lemons or coconuts in place of an animal.

Our approach. We describe practice as it is and has been; readers should follow the law and their own tradition.

ZAPPY: A pumpkin instead of a goat!

PROF. BOLT: Red kuṅkuma on white flesh, Zappy. A change many welcomed.

Old ways, new offerings.

GUARDIANS AT THE EDGE

Male protectors and the boundary

Male guardians. Alongside the Ammas, many villages have male protectors: Munēśvara (common around Bengaluru and Mysuru), Bhairava, Vīrabhadra and hero-spirits.

Boundary stones. Stones at the village edge mark where her protection begins; processions visit them.

Hero stones. Karnataka has thousands of vīragallu, memorial stones for heroes who died defending villages; some are worshipped.

Further south and west. Kodagu and Tuḷunāḍu have their own village gods and spirits; see Kodagu village deities and Tuḷu daivas.

ZAPPY: Even heroes become gods?

PROF. BOLT: Remembered and honoured, Zappy, at least.

Guarded at every edge.

THE AMMA AND THE GREAT GODDESS

Village and Purāṇa

Forms of the one Goddess. Most devotees see each Amma as a form of Pārvatī, Durgā or Kālī: the great Devī living close to home. See Pārvatī and Durgā.

Nāḍa devatā. Some local goddesses grow to rule a whole region: Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is the nāḍa devatā, goddess of the Mysuru land, worshipped at Dasarā.

Seven sisters. Village goddesses are often counted as groups of seven sisters, echoing the Sapta Mātṛkās.

Stories. Ellamma’s story is linked with Reṇukā, mother of Paraśurāma; many Ammas have local tales of a wronged woman who became a goddess. (Traditions vary.)

ZAPPY: So the village Amma and Cāmuṇḍi are related?

PROF. BOLT: Sisters, mothers, forms of one Mother, Zappy, depending whom you ask.

Near and great, one Mother.
