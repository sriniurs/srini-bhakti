LORD OF TIME · GUARDIAN OF KĀŚĪ · THE DOG

Kāla Bhairava

The Guardian Who Is Time · explained and illustrated

The guardian of the city of light

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Kāla Bhairava is: the fierce form born from Śiva’s anger, the beheading of Brahmā’s fifth head and the skull that would not leave his hand, his release at Kāśī, the eight Bhairavas, his dog, Bhairava Aṣṭamī, and his shrines from Ujjain to Ādicuñcanagiri.

WHERE WE ARE

In Kāśī, pilgrims say, you must greet Kāla Bhairava before you greet Viśvanātha, and you may not leave the city without his leave. He is Śiva in his most terrifying form: dark, fanged, carrying a skull, followed by a dog, and yet the protector to whom a whole city entrusts itself.

This guide tells how he was born and why he carries a skull, what “Kāla” means, how he came to guard Kāśī and every temple’s boundary, his eight forms, his days of worship, and his great shrines, including Ādicuñcanagiri in Maṇḍya. The hymn Śaṅkara wrote to him is in our Kālabhairavāṣṭakam guide.

THE NAME

Bhairava and Kāla

Bhairava. “The terrible”, “the one who inspires fear” (bhīru, fear). Tradition also reads it as the one who protects the fearful from fear. (Interpretation.)

Kāla. Both “black” and “time”. Kāla Bhairava is Śiva as Time that devours all things, before which even Brahmā, the creator, must bow. The Gītā’s Kṛṣṇa says the same of himself: “I am Time” (11.32).

A fierce form. Śiva has calm forms (saumya) like Dakṣiṇāmūrti the teacher, and fierce ones (ugra) like Vīrabhadra and Bhairava. The fierce forms deal with pride, evil and danger.

Many Bhairavas. Bhairava is a class of forms as well as one god: Kāla Bhairava, Baṭuka (the boy) Bhairava, the eight Bhairavas, and a Bhairava guarding each Śakti Pīṭha.

ZAPPY: So he is Time?

PROF. BOLT: Time that no one escapes, Zappy. Which is why everyone, even the creator, learns humility before him.

Time in a fierce form.

THE FIFTH HEAD

Brahmā’s pride is cut

The story

Śiva Purāṇa (Śatarudra Saṃhitā) and Kūrma Purāṇa; tellings differ.

In those days Brahmā had five heads, one more than now, the fifth looking upward.
Sages asked who was the supreme reality. Brahmā declared it was himself, the creator; in one telling he spoke with contempt of Śiva, in another he had lied about the column of light (see our Brahmā guide).
From Śiva’s anger, from the space between his brows, sprang a dark, terrible figure: Bhairava. Śiva told him to punish the creator’s pride.
With the nail of his left thumb, Bhairava cut off the fifth head, the one that had spoken. Brahmā, humbled, praised Śiva; Viṣṇu interceded for him.
Brahmā has had four heads ever since. But the severed skull stuck fast to Bhairava’s palm, and would not fall.

ZAPPY: Wasn’t that too harsh?

PROF. BOLT: It cut only the head that boasted, Zappy. The creator kept four heads and lost his pride.

Only the boasting head falls.

THE SKULL THAT WOULD NOT FALL

From wandering beggar to guardian of Kāśī

The story

Śiva Purāṇa; Kāśī Khaṇḍa of the Skanda Purāṇa; Kūrma Purāṇa.

Cutting a head of Brahmā was a grave sin, brahmahatyā. The sin took the form of a terrible woman and followed Bhairava everywhere.
Śiva told him to wander as a naked beggar, using the skull as his begging bowl, until he was free. Bhairava roamed all the worlds, followed by the sin.
At Vaikuṇṭha Viṣṇu honoured him and filled the bowl, in some tellings with his own blood; still the skull clung.
At last Bhairava entered Kāśī. The moment he crossed into the city, the sin could not follow, and the skull fell from his hand. The place is Kapālamocana, “release of the skull”.
Śiva made him the guardian of Kāśī: the city’s kotwāl, its chief of police, who decides who may live and die in the city and who punishes wrongs done there.

ZAPPY: Even Bhairava needed Kāśī to be freed?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the point of the story, Zappy: Kāśī frees everyone. Its guardian learned it first.

Kāśī releases even the releaser.

READING HIS IMAGE

Dark, fierce and kind

Colour and face. Dark blue or black, with bulging eyes, fangs, flaming hair, a third eye, serpents as ornaments and sacred thread; often naked (digambara, “clothed in the directions”), sometimes in a tiger skin.

In his hands. The trident and ḍamaru of Śiva; a sword and a noose for punishment and binding; and Brahmā’s skull, the kapāla, as his bowl. Many images add a garland of skulls.

The dog. His vehicle is a black dog: Bhairava is Śvāśva, “whose horse is a dog”. Dogs guard boundaries and follow faithfully, as Bhairava guards the sacred ground. Feeding dogs is a common way to honour him.

Fierce but kind. Devotees see the terror as aimed outward, at fear itself. His face is fierce; his hand, in many images, also shows the gesture of protection.

ZAPPY: Dogs again! Like Datta’s.

PROF. BOLT: Well noticed, Zappy. Both the teacher and the guardian keep company with the creature everyone else keeps at a distance.

The faithful dog guards the gate.

THE EIGHT BHAIRAVAS

Guardians of the eight directions

🧭 Aṣṭa Bhairava

Asitāṅga, Ruru, Caṇḍa, Krodha, Unmatta, Kapāla, Bhīṣaṇa and Saṃhāra: eight Bhairavas guarding the eight directions, each with his own consort among the Mothers. Kāśī has a shrine for each around the city.

👦 Baṭuka Bhairava

The boy-form, gentle and child-like, worshipped especially in Śākta households; the Baṭuka Bhairava stotra is recited for protection.

🛡️ Pīṭha guardians

Each Śakti Pīṭha has its Bhairava, Śiva’s form beside that body-part of Satī; see the Śakti Pīṭhas.

🏯 Beyond India

Bhairava is central to the Newar tradition of Nepal, whose priests keep many Bhairava temples in the Kathmandu valley; Buddhism took him in as a dharma-protector.

ZAPPY: Eight Bhairavas guard the eight directions?

PROF. BOLT: So no way is left unguarded, Zappy. Fear can’t sneak in from the corner.

Every direction guarded.

THE KṢETRAPĀLA

Guardian of every temple ground

Guardian of the ground. In many South Indian Śiva temples a Bhairava shrine stands in the north-east of the circuit. At night the priests ritually hand over the temple keys to him and take them back at dawn.

Guardian of the town. In villages of Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra, Bhairava (Bhairoba, Bhairavanātha) is the grāmadevatā who protects the boundary, often with a dog beside his shrine.

Ādicuñcanagiri. On the hill in Nāgamaṅgala taluk, Maṇḍya, Śrī Kālabhairaveśvara is the kṣetrapāla of the Ādicuñcanagiri Mahāsaṃsthāna Maṭha, the Nātha monastery and spiritual seat of the Vokkaliga community; Gaṅgādhareśvara is the presiding liṅga among five.

Guru of Śani. Kāla Bhairava is called the teacher and lord of Śani (Saturn); people facing Śani’s hard periods pray to him, often on Saturdays and at Kālāṣṭamī.

ZAPPY: The temple gives him the keys at night?

PROF. BOLT: Every night, Zappy. The guardian takes the night shift.

He takes the night shift.

HIS DAYS

Bhairava Aṣṭamī and Kālāṣṭamī

Bhairava Aṣṭamī. His appearance is celebrated on the eighth day of the dark fortnight that falls in Kārtika by the South’s month reckoning (Mārgaśīrṣa in the northern reckoning): about a week before the Kārtika–Mārgaśīrṣa new moon. Also called Kālabhairava Jayantī.

Kālāṣṭamī. Every dark-fortnight aṣṭamī is his monthly day: a fast, worship at night, and the Kālabhairavāṣṭakam recited eight times by some.

Offerings. Lamps of mustard or sesame oil, black sesame, vaḍe (lentil fritters) in South India, garlands of lemons in some temples, and food for dogs. At Ujjain’s Kāl Bhairav temple liquor is famously offered and accepted by the image, a practice rooted in old Tantric worship. (Practices vary.)

Sundays and nights. Many keep Sunday for Bhairava; his worship is traditionally a night-time worship.

ZAPPY: Lemon garlands?

PROF. BOLT: For protection against the evil eye, Zappy, in many temples. Lemons and fierce gods go together.

Worship in the dark half.

HIS SHRINES

Kāśī, Ujjain and the Jyotirliṅgas

Kāśī. The Kāla Bhairava temple in the old city is visited first by pilgrims; the image has a silver face and wears a garland, with a dog at his side. Black threads tied there are worn for protection. See Kāśī Viśvanātha.

Ujjain. The Kāl Bhairav temple near the Kṣiprā, guardian of Mahākāleśvara’s city.

Near every Jyotirliṅga. Shrines to Bhairava stand near most of the twelve Jyotirliṅgas, as kotwāl of each holy town.

In Karnataka. Ādicuñcanagiri in Maṇḍya, where Kālabhairaveśvara guards the Nātha maṭha on the hill, and Kālabhairava shrines in the circuits of many Śiva temples and at village boundaries.

ZAPPY: A silver face?

PROF. BOLT: On festival days, Zappy. Underneath, the black stone that has guarded Kāśī for centuries.

Every holy town has its guardian.

ŚAṄKARA’S HYMN

The Kālabhairavāṣṭakam

The hymn. Ādi Śaṅkara is said to have composed the Kālabhairavāṣṭakam in Kāśī: eight verses, each ending “kāśikāpuradhinātha kālabhairavaṃ bhaje”, “I worship Kāla Bhairava, lord of the city of Kāśī”.

What it praises. His dark form and garland of skulls, his dog, his role as punisher of wrong-doers and liberator of devotees, and above all his lordship of Kāśī.

When it is chanted. At Kālāṣṭamī and Bhairava Aṣṭamī, before travelling, and by those who feel afraid; many chant one verse daily.

Read it. The full text with verse-by-verse meanings is in our Kālabhairavāṣṭakam guide.

ZAPPY: A gentle monk wrote a hymn to a fierce god?

PROF. BOLT: The gentlest people often love the fiercest protectors, Zappy. They know what needs guarding.

Eight verses for the guardian.

WHAT HE TEACHES

Fear, pride and protection

Pride is cut. His first act was to cut the boasting head of the creator. No status protects pride. (Interpretation.)

Wrong follows the wrong-doer. Even Bhairava, acting on Śiva’s order, had to carry his deed until it was released. Actions have consequences that must be worked through.

Time is fierce and fair. As Kāla, he devours all; as guardian, he protects those who live rightly within his city. Both are true of time. (Interpretation.)

Courage. Devotees face the fierce form so that fear itself is faced. The dark night is less frightening with a guard at the door.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Bhairava?

PROF. BOLT: Feed a stray dog, Zappy, and let go of one thing you’re proud of.

Face fear; drop pride.

Skull, used as a begging bowl.

“Release of the skull”, the place in Kāśī.

Guardian of sacred ground.
