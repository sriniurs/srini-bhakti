GODS · THE SUN

Sūrya

The Eye of the World · explained and illustrated

The eye of the world

HOW THIS WORKS: Sūrya, the Sun god: the Vedic hymns and the Gāyatrī, the chariot of seven horses and the charioteer Aruṇa, his wife Saṃjñā and her Shadow, his famous children (Yama, Yamunā, Śani, Karṇa, Sugrīva), the twelve Ādityas, morning arghya, Sūrya namaskāra, and the great Sun temples from Konārk to Sūryanār Kōvil.

WHERE WE ARE

Every morning, all over India, people stand facing the rising sun and pour out water from cupped hands. They are offering arghya to Sūrya, the one god everyone can see.

Sūrya is among the oldest gods of the Vedas, the head of the nine planets, one of the five deities of the Smārta household shrine, and the father of some of India’s most famous heroes. This guide tells his stories, his worship and his temples. For his festivals, see Ratha Saptamī and Makara Saṅkrānti.

IN THE VERSE

Ṛgveda 1.115.1

The face of the gods
citraṃ devānām ud agād anīkaṃcakṣur mitrasya varuṇasyāgneḥāprā dyāvāpṛthivī antarikṣaṃsūrya ātmā jagatas tasthuṣaś ca
The bright face of the gods has risen, the eye of Mitra, Varuṇa and Agni. He has filled heaven and earth and the space between. Sūrya is the soul of all that moves and all that stands still.

SŪRYA IN THE VEDAS

Sūrya, Savitṛ and the Gāyatrī

Many names, one sun. The Vedas praise the sun as Sūrya (the visible sun), Savitṛ (the impeller, who sets things moving), and among the Ādityas, sons of Aditi.

Uṣas. The dawn goddess goes before him, opening the doors of the sky.

The Gāyatrī. The most sacred Vedic mantra is addressed to Savitṛ: “we meditate on the glorious light of Savitṛ; may he inspire our minds”. See Gāyatrī mantra.

Sandhyā. The twice- or thrice-daily sandhyāvandana is offered to the sun at the junctions of the day.

PROF. BOLT: To Savitṛ, the sun as inspirer, Zappy. Light for the mind.

SAṂJÑĀ AND HER SHADOW

A family story

The story

Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa, with variations.

Saṃjñā, daughter of the divine craftsman Viśvakarmā, married Sūrya, but could not bear his blazing heat. She bore him Manu, Yama and Yamunā.
Exhausted, she created a double, Chāyā (“Shadow”), left her in her place, and went to the forest as a mare to practise austerity.
Chāyā bore Sūrya Śani (Saturn) and Tapatī. When she favoured her own children over Saṃjñā’s, Yama protested, and the secret came out.
Sūrya found Saṃjñā; as horses they had the twin Aśvins, physicians of the gods. Viśvakarmā trimmed Sūrya’s brightness on his lathe so she could live with him; the shavings became divine weapons, including Viṣṇu’s discus, in some tellings.
So Death (Yama), the river Yamunā, Saturn and the healers are all the Sun’s children.

ZAPPY: Śani and Yama are brothers?

PROF. BOLT: Half-brothers, Zappy, both children of the Sun.

THE SUN’S FAMOUS CHILDREN

Heroes, gods and rivers

⚖️ Yama and Yamunā

The god of death and dharma, and his twin sister the river; their bond is celebrated on Yama Dvitīyā (Bhāī Dūj).

🪐 Śani

Saturn, the slow, stern planet; see Nakṣatras and Rāśis.

🏹 Karṇa

The tragic hero of the Mahābhārata, born to Kuntī by Sūrya, with divine armour and earrings.

🐒 Sugrīva

The monkey king of the Rāmāyaṇa, Hanumān’s friend; Hanumān himself learned the scriptures from Sūrya. See Kiṣkindhā.

ZAPPY: Hanumān’s teacher was the Sun?

PROF. BOLT: He kept pace with the chariot to learn, Zappy, walking backwards across the sky.

HOW HE IS SHOWN

Chariot, lotuses and boots

🪷 Two lotuses

He stands holding a lotus in each hand: the lotus opens at sunrise and closes at sunset.

🐎 Seven horses

Seven horses, read as the seven days, seven colours of light, or the seven Vedic metres. Aruṇa, the legless dawn, drives.

👢 Boots

Some early North Indian images show him in a long coat and high boots, a sign of Central Asian influence.

☸️ The wheel

His chariot has one wheel; at Konārk the whole temple is a stone chariot with 24 carved wheels.

ZAPPY: A god in boots!

PROF. BOLT: Only in some old sculptures, Zappy. Look for them in museums.

Lotus in each hand.

WORSHIPPING THE SUN

Arghya, namaskāra and Sunday

Arghya. Water poured from cupped hands or a copper vessel towards the rising sun, often with the Gāyatrī; part of sandhyāvandana.

Sūrya namaskāra. A flowing sequence of postures greeting the sun, each with one of his twelve names (“oṃ mitrāya namaḥ…”). The modern sequence took shape in the 20th century.

Sunday. Ravivāra is his day; some fast or avoid salt on Sundays.

Hymns. The Vedic hymns, the Āditya Hṛdayam taught by Agastya to Rāma before battle, and the Sūrya Aṣṭakam.

ZAPPY: Twelve names, twelve postures?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the pattern, Zappy: name, bow, breathe.

SŪRYA AMONG THE GODS

Graha, Āditya and pañcāyatana

Head of the Navagrahas. In every Navagraha shrine, Sūrya stands at the centre, the other eight planets around him.

Twelve Ādityas. Purāṇas count twelve sons of Aditi (Mitra, Varuṇa, Aryaman, Bhaga, Indra, Viṣṇu and others), often taken as the sun in the twelve months.

Pañcāyatana. In Smārta home worship, Sūrya is one of five deities with Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī and Gaṇeśa; see Sampradāyas.

Saura tradition. Some communities once worshipped Sūrya as the supreme god; this declined from around the 13th century, and he was often absorbed as Sūrya-Nārāyaṇa.

ZAPPY: He used to have his own tradition?

PROF. BOLT: The Sauras, Zappy. A few traces remain, especially in the East.

At the centre.

SUN TEMPLES

From Konārk to Kumbakonam

🛞 Konārk, Odisha

A 13th-century temple built as the Sun’s stone chariot, with 24 wheels and 7 horses; a World Heritage Site.

💧 Modhera, Gujarat

An 11th-century Sun temple with a great stepped tank before it.

⛰️ Mārtaṇḍ, Kashmir

An 8th-century Sun temple, now in ruins, on a plateau near Anantnag.

🛕 The South

Arasavalli (Andhra Pradesh) and Sūryanār Kōvil near Kumbakonam, the Sun’s temple among the nine Navagraha temples; see Kumbakonam.

ZAPPY: A whole temple shaped like a chariot!

PROF. BOLT: Konārk, Zappy. Its wheels are also sundials, people say.

Temples of light.

WHAT SŪRYA TEACHES

Light, life and duty

Life itself. Every grain of food is stored sunlight; Indian tradition worshipped the sun as the giver of life long before science explained why.

Faithfulness. The sun rises every day without fail and gives light to all equally. Teachers hold this up as a model of duty without favour. (Interpretation.)

Health. Morning sunlight and Sūrya namaskāra are linked in tradition with health; the Aśvins, the healers, are his sons.

The inner sun. The Upaniṣads speak of the person “in the sun” and in the eye as the same Self: outer light and inner light are one.
