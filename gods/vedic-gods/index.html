INDRA · AGNI · VĀYU · VARUṆA · THE GODS OF THE VEDA

The Vedic Gods

Indra, Agni, Vāyu and Varuṇa · explained and illustrated

The gods of the fire altar

HOW THIS WORKS: Who the gods of the Ṛgveda were: Indra the thunderer who freed the waters, Agni the fire who carries offerings, Vāyu the wind and breath, Varuṇa the guardian of cosmic order, their famous hymns, why their worship changed, and where they live on in Hindu practice today.

WHERE WE ARE

Long before temples to Viṣṇu and Śiva, the oldest Hindu hymns praised a different family of gods: Indra, wielder of the thunderbolt; Agni, the fire on the altar; Vāyu, the wind; Varuṇa, who sees every wrong and upholds the order of the world. The Ṛgveda, composed more than three thousand years ago, gives them most of its thousand hymns.

This guide introduces the four, tells their great stories, quotes two famous verses, explains how their place changed as Purāṇic Hinduism grew, and shows where they still live: in every fire ritual, in the guardians of the directions, and, for Karnataka’s Mādhvas, in Vāyu above all.

THE WORLD OF THE ṚGVEDA

Hymns, fire and soma

The hymns. The Ṛgveda is a collection of 1,028 hymns (sūktas) in ten books (maṇḍalas), composed by families of poet-seers and preserved orally with astonishing accuracy. Most hymns praise particular gods.

The ritual. The gods were invited to a sacrifice (yajña) around a fire altar: offerings of ghee, grain and the pressed drink soma were poured into the fire, and the hymns were sung.

No images. Vedic worship needed no temple and no image; the gods came to the fire and the sacred grass.

Many gods, one truth. Even so, the poets already wondered what lay behind all the gods (next page). The scriptures door explains the Veda’s place among Hindu texts.

ZAPPY: No temples at all?

PROF. BOLT: A patch of ground, a fire and the right words, Zappy. That was enough for the gods to come.

A fire, a hymn, a god.

INDRA AND VṚTRA

Freeing the waters

The story

Ṛgveda 1.32 and many other hymns.

The serpent Vṛtra, “the obstructor”, lay coiled across the mountains, holding back the waters of the world. Nothing could grow.
Indra, strengthened by soma, took up the vajra, the thunderbolt made for him by the craftsman Tvaṣṭṛ.
He struck Vṛtra and split him open. The serpent fell, and the waters rushed out “like lowing cows” to the sea.
Rivers ran, rain fell, the sun shone: life was possible. Indra’s great deed is told again and again; his title is Vṛtrahan, “Vṛtra-slayer”.
Many read the myth as the monsoon breaking the drought, or as light overcoming obstruction. (Interpretations.)

ZAPPY: Like the monsoon arriving!

PROF. BOLT: Exactly how many readers see it, Zappy. Anyone who has waited for June rain understands Indra.

The obstruction is split.

IN THE VERSE

Ṛgveda 1.1.1

The first verse of the Veda
agnim īḷe purohitaṃyajñasya devam ṛtvijamhotāraṃ ratnadhātamam
I praise Agni, the one placed in front, the god of the sacrifice, its priest, the invoker, the greatest giver of treasure.
WordMeaning

The Veda begins with fire. The very first word of the Ṛgveda is agnim. Agni is both the god and the fire itself: he is the priest who calls the gods and the mouth through which they eat. Every Hindu rite with a fire, from a wedding to a homa, still invites him first.

Ṛgveda 1.1.1; text as in shlokam.org (Agni Sūktam) and sanskritdocuments.org’s Ṛgveda (unaccented).
The Veda opens with fire.

AGNI

The fire that carries

Messenger. Agni carries offerings from humans to the gods and brings the gods to the rite. He is present in three worlds: as fire on earth, lightning in the sky and the sun in heaven.

Priest and guest. He is the household fire, the guest in every home; the cooking fire and the funeral fire are both Agni.

Witness. A Hindu marriage is solemnised by walking round the fire: Agni is the witness to the vows. Many traditions also keep a sacred fire through life (agnihotra).

His image. When shown in art: red, with two faces and flames for hair, riding a ram; he guards the south-east.

ZAPPY: So the fire at a wedding is a god?

PROF. BOLT: The witness to your promise, Zappy. Hard to break a vow made in front of Agni.

The witness to every vow.

VARUṆA AND MITRA

Guardians of ṛta

Ṛta. The Veda’s great idea of cosmic order: the regular course of sun, seasons and rivers, and the truthful order of human life. Ṛta is the ancestor of the later word dharma.

Varuṇa. The sovereign who guards ṛta. He sees everything with his spies and the stars; he binds wrongdoers with his noose; and he forgives the repentant. The Ṛgveda’s hymns to him are among its most personal confessions.

Mitra. “Friend” and “contract”: Varuṇa’s partner, the god of agreements and friendship, linked with the sun.

Later. In the Purāṇas Varuṇa becomes lord of the oceans and guardian of the west, riding a makara; Mitra fades from worship.

ZAPPY: Varuṇa sees everything?

PROF. BOLT: He was the Veda’s conscience, Zappy: the god who knew when you broke your word.

The order behind the world.

VĀYU

Wind and breath

Wind. Vāyu is the wind, swift and invisible, often paired with Indra; in the soma rite he is honoured as first to drink.

Breath. In the Upaniṣads the wind outside is prāṇa, the breath within: when the other senses argued about who was greatest, only breath, by leaving, could make the body fail. (Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya Upaniṣads.)

Father of heroes. In the epics Vāyu is the father of Hanumān and of Bhīma, the strongest of the Pāṇḍavas.

In Karnataka. For the Mādhva tradition, Vāyu (Mukhyaprāṇa) is the highest of all souls and Viṣṇu’s chief servant, appearing as Hanumān, Bhīma and Madhvācārya himself. Mukhyaprāṇa shrines stand in Mādhva temples across the state.

ZAPPY: Hanumān, Bhīma and Madhva are the same?

PROF. BOLT: In Mādhva belief, Zappy: three appearances of Vāyu. It’s why Udupi’s tradition loves Hanumān so.

The breath in every body.

IN THE VERSE

Ṛgveda 1.164.46

“One truth, many names”
indraṃ mitraṃ varuṇam agnim āhuratho divyaḥ sa suparṇo garutmānekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadantyagniṃ yamaṃ mātariśvānam āhuḥ
They call it Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Agni; and it is the heavenly, fair-winged Garutmān. What is one, the inspired poets speak of in many ways: they call it Agni, Yama, Mātariśvan.
WordMeaning
āhuḥthey call
ekam satthat which is one
viprāḥinspired poets
bahudhāin many ways
vadantispeak
mātariśvana name of wind or fire

The most quoted line. “Ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti” is cited as the Veda’s own statement that the many gods are names of one reality. Scholars debate whether the poet meant God, the sun or the sacrifice; but the line set the direction for the Upaniṣads.

One with many names.

THE REST OF THE FAMILY

Soma, Uṣas, the Aśvins and more

🍶 Soma

The sacred drink pressed in the rite and the god within it; a whole book of the Ṛgveda (the ninth) praises him. Later Soma is the Moon.

🌅 Uṣas

The dawn, a radiant young woman who wakes all beings: some of the Veda’s most beautiful hymns are hers.

🐎 Aśvins and Maruts

The Aśvins, twin horsemen and healers who rescue the distressed; the Maruts, storm-gods who ride with Indra.

🏹 Rudra and Viṣṇu

Rudra, the fierce archer, and Viṣṇu of the three strides have only a few hymns here; in later Hinduism they become Śiva and Viṣṇu.

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu had only a few hymns?

PROF. BOLT: About six, Zappy. Small seeds, great trees.

Small seeds, great trees.

HOW THEIR PLACE CHANGED

From kings of heaven to guardians

Very old gods. A treaty of the Mitanni kingdom in northern Syria, of about the fourteenth century BCE, invokes Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa and the Nāsatyas (Aśvins): these gods were known far beyond India very early.

A shift. As devotion to Viṣṇu, Śiva and the Devī grew, the Vedic gods became their servants: in the Purāṇas Indra is a proud king of heaven who often needs rescuing, and Varuṇa, Agni and Vāyu serve the great gods.

Guardians of directions. They became the dikpālas: Indra (east), Agni (south-east), Yama (south), Nirṛti (south-west), Varuṇa (west), Vāyu (north-west), Kubera (north), Īśāna (north-east). Temples carve them on ceilings and walls.

Indra and Kṛṣṇa. The story of Govardhana, where Kṛṣṇa lifts a hill to shelter the cowherds from Indra’s storm, marks the shift: worship moves from Indra to Kṛṣṇa. See Kṛṣṇa.

ZAPPY: Indra got demoted?

PROF. BOLT: He kept his title, Zappy, but the stories turned. He still sends the rain.

From kings to guardians.

WHERE THEY LIVE TODAY

Fire, directions and breath

Every homa. Gṛhapraveśa, weddings, thread ceremonies and annual homas still pour offerings into Agni with Vedic verses; the Vedic gods are invoked by name.

Daily chant. Brāhmaṇa families chant the Gāyatrī (to Savitṛ, a sun god of the Veda) at dawn and dusk; see The Gāyatrī Mantra.

In the temple. Look up: the dikpālas often frame the ceiling of the hall. Ancient fire-temples aside, Indra and Varuṇa have few shrines of their own; Varuṇa is prayed to for rain.

In the body. Yoga’s prāṇāyāma and the Upaniṣads’ teaching of prāṇa carry Vāyu inward: every breath is the wind god.

ZAPPY: I meet Vāyu every time I breathe?

PROF. BOLT: By the Upaniṣads’ reckoning, Zappy. Try noticing it once a day.

In fire, sky and breath.

WHAT THEY TEACH

Order, gratitude, wonder

Wonder at nature. The Vedic poets saw the sacred in dawn, storm, wind, fire and flowing water. Their hymns remain some of the oldest nature poetry in the world.

Order and truth. Varuṇa’s ṛta asks that human life keep faith with the order of the world: honest speech, kept promises, seasons respected.

Gratitude through offering. The fire ritual returns a share of what we receive. The Gītā keeps this idea of yajña at its centre (3.10–16).

One behind many. “Ekaṃ sat”: the Veda itself points beyond its many gods to one reality.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for the Vedic gods?

PROF. BOLT: Watch one sunrise properly, Zappy, and say thank you to it. That’s how the Veda began.

Begin with wonder.
