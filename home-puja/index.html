A Simple Home Pūjā

Step by Step, and What Each Step Means · explained and illustrated

Step by step, and what each step means

HOW THIS WORKS: The steps of a home pūjā in order, and why: the tilaka, flowers, lamp, bell, naivedya, ārati, pradakṣiṇā and prasāda, with a five-minute version.

WHERE WE ARE

Pūjā is simply this: receiving God as an honoured guest in your home. You invite, offer a seat, water, fragrance, flowers, light and food, you circle and bow, and you take back what was offered as grace.

This article walks through a simple home pūjā step by step, and answers the questions people actually ask: why do we do ārati? why tilaka? why ring a bell? what is prasāda? It ends with a five-minute version for busy days.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Do I need a priest or Sanskrit for this?

PROF. BOLT: No. A clean place, a lamp, a flower and a sincere mind. The Gītā itself says a leaf or a little water offered with love is accepted.

WHAT IS PŪJĀ

God as the honoured guest

The idea. The steps of pūjā follow the way an honoured guest was received in an old Indian home: welcome, a seat, water for the feet and hands, a bath, fresh clothes, fragrance, flowers, light, a meal, betel, and a respectful farewell.

The word. Pūjā means worship or honouring; each act of service is an upacāra.

How many. There are short and long forms: five services (pañcopacāra), ten, sixteen (ṣoḍaśopacāra), and longer temple forms.

Why a form at all. The form gives the hands something to do while the heart attends. It is a discipline of attention.

ZAPPY: So I’m treating God like a guest?

PROF. BOLT: Like the most welcome guest you could ever have. That’s the whole emotional key.

Receive God as your guest.

BEFORE YOU BEGIN

What you need

🛁 Yourself

A bath and clean clothes; a calm few minutes.

🧹 The place

A clean corner, shelf or room; many face east or north.

🖼️ The image

A picture, image (mūrti), śālagrāma, liṅga or simply a lamp.

🪔 The things

Lamp, water, a flower, incense, a little food. A bell and kuṅkuma if you have them.

ZAPPY: What if I don’t have all this?

PROF. BOLT: Use what you have. A lamp and water are enough to begin; the rest grows with habit.

A clean place and a quiet mind.

STEP 1 · ĀCAMANA

Sipping water to begin

What. Three small sips of water from the right palm, each with a name of God (commonly Keśava, Nārāyaṇa, Mādhava in Vaiṣṇava homes).

Why. Inner purification: as a bath cleans the body, the sips are said to clean speech and mind before worship.

Prāṇāyāma. Many follow with a few slow breaths to settle the mind.

Simple version. Wash hands and feet, sip a little water, and take three calm breaths.

Clean inside before you begin.

STEP 2 · SAṄKALPA

Saying what you are doing

What. A short statement of intention: who is worshipping, where, when, which deity, and why. Formal saṅkalpas name the place and time in traditional reckoning.

How. Hold a little water, rice or a flower in the right hand, state the intention, and release it.

Why. It turns a routine into a deliberate act; you know what you are doing and for whom.

Simple version. In your own language: “Today I worship the Lord, for the good of my family and all beings.”

ZAPPY: Can I say it in Kannada or English?

PROF. BOLT: Of course. God understands every language. Sanskrit is lovely, not compulsory.

Know what you are doing.

IN THE VERSE

Gaṇeśa first

Why every pūjā begins with Gaṇeśa
śuklāmbaradharaṃ viṣṇuṃ śaśivarṇaṃ caturbhujamprasannavadanaṃ dhyāyet sarvavighnopaśāntaye
One should meditate on the one clad in white, all-pervading, moon-coloured, four-armed, with a gracious face, for the calming of all obstacles.
WordMeaning
śukla-ambara-dharamwearing white
viṣṇumthe all-pervading
śaśi-varṇammoon-coloured
catur-bhujamfour-armed
prasanna-vadanamwith a gracious face
sarva-vighna-upaśāntayefor the calming of all obstacles

Gaṇeśa is remembered first so that the rest of the worship goes without hindrance. This verse is recited in many homes as the very first line of any pūjā.

Text as on our Gaṇeśa daily ślokas page.
Obstacles calmed, then begin.

STEP 3 · DHYĀNA AND ĀVĀHANA

Remembering and inviting

Dhyāna. Close your eyes and picture the deity’s form, as described in a meditation verse or as you know it.

Āvāhana. Invite God to be present in the image or lamp before you. Many show an inviting gesture with joined palms.

Why. The image is not worshipped as stone or metal; it is a place where you ask the Lord to receive your service.

Āsana. Then offer a seat: a flower or a few grains of rice placed at the base.

ZAPPY: So the idol isn’t God?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition says God is everywhere and chooses to receive worship there. The image is the meeting point.

Picture, invite, offer a seat.

THE SIXTEEN SERVICES

Ṣoḍaśopacāra at a glance

Lists differ by tradition: some count dhyāna separately, some end with mantrapuṣpa or a prayer for pardon. This is a common arrangement.

1–3 Dhyāna, āvāhana, āsana

Remember, invite, offer a seat.

4–6 Pādya, arghya, ācamanīya

Water for the feet, the hands and to sip.

7–9 Snāna, vastra, yajñopavīta

A bath, clothing, the sacred thread or ornaments.

10–12 Gandha, puṣpa, dhūpa

Sandal paste, flowers, incense.

13–14 Dīpa, naivedya

A lamp and food.

15–16 Tāmbūla/nīrājana, namaskāra

Betel or ārati, then pradakṣiṇā and bowing.

Sixteen ways to serve a guest.

WATER OFFERINGS

Pādya, arghya, ācamanīya, snāna

Pādya. A little water at the feet, as a tired traveller’s feet are washed.

Arghya. Water for the hands, often with flowers and rice: the mark of respect to a guest.

Ācamanīya. Water to sip.

Snāna. A bath: plain water, or pañcāmṛta (milk, curd, ghee, honey, sugar) on special days. At home a few drops sprinkled on the image, or on a flower, is enough.

Water first, as for any guest.

WHY TILAKA?

Gandha, kuṅkuma and the mark on the forehead

To God. Sandal paste (gandha) and kuṅkuma are offered to the deity as fragrance and adornment.

To ourselves. After worship we wear it as prasāda on the forehead, between the brows, where many teachers place the seat of attention. (Interpretation.)

Different marks. Vaiṣṇavas wear the upright ūrdhva-puṇḍra, Śaivas the three horizontal lines of ash (tripuṇḍra), many wear a dot of kuṅkuma; each marks a tradition.

Why wear it. It says “I have worshipped today”; a reminder through the day, and a sign of belonging.

ZAPPY: Is the tilaka the same for everyone?

PROF. BOLT: No: it tells you the tradition. Upright for Viṣṇu, lines of ash for Śiva, red for Devī. All say: God first.

A mark that remembers worship.

WHY FLOWERS?

Puṣpa, akṣata and sacred leaves

Flowers. The most beautiful thing in nature, offered back to its source; fresh, unsmelled, and whole.

By deity. Tulasī for Viṣṇu and Kṛṣṇa, bilva for Śiva, dūrvā and red flowers for Gaṇeśa, red flowers for Devī. Each pairing has its stories.

Akṣata. Unbroken rice, often with turmeric or kuṅkuma: “unbroken”, standing for wholeness; used when an item is not at hand.

Puṣpāñjali. At the end, flowers offered with both hands together.

The best of nature, given back.

WHY INCENSE?

Dhūpa

Fragrance. Incense honours the guest with the sense of smell, as sandal paste does with touch.

Rising smoke. It rises and spreads; many see it as prayer going upward. (Interpretation.)

Cleansing. Traditionally incense and resin also cleared the air of insects and odours.

Care. One stick is enough; keep a window open and the ash contained.

Fragrance for the guest.

WHY LIGHT A LAMP?

Dīpa

Light. The lamp honours God with sight, and is itself an image of knowledge driving out ignorance.

Ghee or oil. A ghee lamp is traditional; sesame oil is common. The wick is lit facing east or north in many homes.

Morning and evening. Many homes light the lamp at dawn and at dusk even when there is no full pūjā.

The lamp as deity. In many homes the lamp itself is worshipped as a form of Devī or of light.

ZAPPY: Why does every pūjā have a lamp?

PROF. BOLT: Because nothing is seen without light, including God. Lighting a lamp is the shortest pūjā there is.

Light drives out darkness.

WHY RING A BELL?

Ghaṇṭā and śaṅkha

Announcing. The bell is rung at the start of worship, at the bath, while offering incense, lamp and food, and through ārati: an announcement that worship is under way.

Gathering the mind. Its clear sound draws scattered attention to the present. (Interpretation.)

Driving away the unwelcome. Traditional texts say the sound welcomes the divine and drives out the undivine.

The conch. Blown at the start or end in many homes and temples; Viṣṇu holds it as Pāñcajanya.

A sound that gathers the mind.

IN THE VERSE

What is naivedya?

Food offered first to God
patraṃ puṣpaṃ phalaṃ toyaṃ yo me bhaktyā prayacchatitadahaṃ bhaktyupahṛtamaśnāmi prayatātmanaḥ
Whoever offers me with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit, or water, that devotion-offering of the pure-hearted I accept. (Bhagavad Gītā 9.26)
WordMeaning
patrama leaf
puṣpama flower
phalama fruit
toyamwater
bhaktyāwith devotion
aśnāmiI accept, I partake

Naivedya is the food offered to God before anyone eats: cooked rice, fruit, milk, sweets, whatever the house eats. Water is sprinkled around it, it is offered with a prayer, and after a pause it becomes prasāda. Kṛṣṇa’s verse sets the standard: not the cost, but the devotion.

Text as on our Gītā, chapter 9 page.
A leaf given with love is enough.

WHY DO WE DO ĀRATI?

Nīrājana, the waving of lights

What. A lamp or camphor flame on a plate is waved in circles before the deity, usually with a bell and a song or verse.

Why. It shows every part of the form in light, as one would look lovingly at a beloved guest; it also marks the climax of the pūjā.

Why camphor. Camphor burns away completely, leaving no ash: an image of the ego burning away in God. (A common interpretation.)

Taking ārati. Afterwards everyone passes their palms over the flame and touches their eyes: receiving the light as grace.

ZAPPY: Why do we put our hands over the flame and then on our eyes?

PROF. BOLT: To take the blessing of the light into ourselves. Grace given, grace received.

Light shown, light received.

WHY PRADAKṢIṆĀ AND NAMASKĀRA?

Circling and bowing

Pradakṣiṇā. Walking clockwise around the deity, keeping God on your right, the side of honour. At home, turning around in place is the accepted substitute.

Why clockwise. As the planets circle the sun, the devotee’s life circles God as its centre. (Interpretation.)

Namaskāra. Bowing with joined palms; the full prostration (sāṣṭāṅga, “with eight limbs”) touches forehead, chest, hands, knees and feet to the ground.

Meaning. “Not I, but you”: the ego bows.

God at the centre, the ego bowed.

IN THE VERSE

The closing offering

Giving it all back
kāyena vācā manasendriyair vābuddhyātmanā vā prakṛteḥ svabhāvātkaromi yad yat sakalaṃ parasmainārāyaṇāyeti samarpayāmi
Whatever I do, by body, speech, mind or senses, by intellect or self, or by the natural bent of my nature, all of it I offer to the Supreme, to Nārāyaṇa.
WordMeaning
kāyenaby the body
vācāby speech
manasāby the mind
indriyaiḥby the senses
prakṛteḥ svabhāvātfrom my natural bent
samarpayāmiI offer

Many close every pūjā with this verse (from the Bhāgavata, 11.2.36), offering not just the pūjā but the whole day. Śaivas and Śāktas say the same with the name of their deity. Then many ask pardon for any mistakes in the worship.

Text as on our Viṣṇu Sahasranāma, part 10 page.
Offer the whole day, not just the pūjā.

IN THE VERSE

What is prasāda?

Grace you can eat
brahmārpaṇaṃ brahmahavirbrahmāgnau brahmaṇā hutambrahmaiva tena gantavyaṃ brahmakarmasamādhinā
The offering is Brahman, the oblation is Brahman, poured by Brahman into the fire that is Brahman; Brahman is reached by the one absorbed in action as Brahman. (Bhagavad Gītā 4.24)
WordMeaning
arpaṇamthe act of offering
haviḥthe oblation
agnauin the fire
hutamoffered
gantavyamto be reached
samādhināby absorption

Prasāda means grace, clarity, favour. Food offered to God and returned is no longer just food: it is received with the right hand, never wasted or refused, shared with others. Many recite this Gītā verse before every meal, so that eating itself becomes an offering.

Text as on our Gītā, chapter 4 page.
Food offered becomes grace returned.

A FIVE-MINUTE PŪJĀ

Pañcopacāra for busy days

1. Begin. Wash, sip water, a breath; remember Gaṇeśa.

2. Five offerings. Gandha (a dot of sandal or kuṅkuma), puṣpa (a flower), dhūpa (incense), dīpa (the lamp), naivedya (a fruit, milk or a sweet).

3. Ārati. Wave the lamp or camphor three times, with a bell if you have one.

4. Bow and take prasāda. A namaskāra, a word of thanks, and share the offered food.

ZAPPY: Five minutes counts?

PROF. BOLT: Every day for five minutes beats an hour once a month. The habit is the pūjā.

Five offerings, five minutes.

COMMON QUESTIONS

Short, honest answers

🖼️ No image?

A lamp, a picture or even the sun will do; many traditions worship the formless through a flame.

🗓️ Missed a day?

Resume the next day. Regularity matters more than perfection.

📿 No mantras?

The deity’s name, said with attention, is enough. Learn a few verses over time.

🍽️ What to offer?

What your home eats that day, made with care. Many avoid onion and garlic in offered food.

Sincerity over perfection.

PŪJĀ WITH CHILDREN

Bringing them in

Give them a job. Ring the bell, pick the flower, carry the water, hold the plate at ārati.

One verse. Teach one short verse, say the Gaṇeśa verse, and let them lead it.

Explain one step. One “why” a week: why the lamp, why the bell, why prasāda.

Make it theirs. Let them place their own offering. Children who belong to the pūjā keep it as adults.

Give every child a job.
