How to Read IAST

The dots and lines in every Sanskrit verse · explained and illustrated

A dozen marks, every verse opened

HOW THIS WORKS: First why we use IAST, then each special letter with examples you already know: long vowels, the vowel ṛ, the dotted letters, breath, the three hisses, the nasals, and a practice verse.

WHY THIS GUIDE

Every hymn and verse on this site is written in IAST, the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration: ordinary Roman letters with a few dots and lines added. We use it instead of Devanāgarī so that anyone can read the Sanskrit, whatever script they grew up with.

But the dots and lines matter. Śiva and siva, Kālī and kali are different words. This guide explains every special letter in plain English, with examples you already know, so you can chant from our pages with confidence.

Read it once, then keep it open beside any verse page.

LONG AND SHORT VOWELS

The line on top means “hold it”

The macron. A line over a vowel (ā, ī, ū) means it is long: held for about twice as long as the short one. That is all; the sound itself barely changes.

The sounds. a is short, like the “u” in “but”; ā is long, like “a” in “father”. i as in “bit”, ī as in “machine”. u as in “put”, ū as in “rule”.

Always long. e (as in “café”) and o (as in “go”) have no short form in Sanskrit, so they carry no mark. ai sounds as in “aisle”, au as in “loud”.

Why it matters. Length changes meaning and rhythm. Kālī (the Goddess) is not kali (the dark age). In a verse, long syllables carry the beat, so getting them right makes a chant flow.

ZAPPY: So Rāma is “Raa-ma”?

PROF. BOLT: Yes: a long first “a”, a short final one. And Śiva is “Shi-va”, both short.

A line on top means hold the vowel twice as long.

THE VOWEL Ṛ

Not a consonant, a vowel

A vowel. ṛ, with a dot under it, is a vowel, not a consonant: a short, rolled “r” that carries a syllable by itself, like the “r” some people say in “purr”.

Two common ways. In North India it is usually said like a short “ri” (Krishna, rishi); in much of South India like a short “ru” (Krushna, amrutha). Both are traditional; follow your teacher or family.

Long ṝ and ḷ. ṝ is the same held longer, and ḷ is a rare vowel “l” (like a short “lri”). You will meet them only occasionally.

Spot it. Kṛṣṇa, ṛṣi, amṛta, Bṛhaspati, hṛdaya (heart), Vṛṣabha (bull): every one has the vowel ṛ.

ZAPPY: So Kṛṣṇa has no “i” in it?

PROF. BOLT: Not written. The ṛ is the vowel. The “i” or “u” you hear is just how the vowel is voiced.

ṛ is a vowel: “ri” in the north, “ru” in the south.

WHERE THE SOUNDS ARE MADE

Five places in the mouth

A beautiful order. Sanskrit grammarians arranged the consonants by where the tongue or lips touch, from the throat forward: velar (back), palatal (palate), retroflex (roof, tongue curled), dental (teeth), labial (lips).

Five in each row. Each place has five: plain, breathy (aspirated), voiced, voiced breathy, and nasal. So k kh g gh ṅ, c ch j jh ñ, ṭ ṭh ḍ ḍh ṇ, t th d dh n, p ph b bh m.

Why learn it. Once you know the five places, every dotted letter makes sense: the dots mark the retroflex row, and the h’s mark breath.

Try it. Say ka, ca, ṭa, ta, pa slowly and feel the touch move forward from the back of your mouth to your lips.

ZAPPY: Grammarians drew a map of the mouth?

PROF. BOLT: Over two thousand years ago, and it is still the best way to learn the sounds.

Back, palate, roof, teeth, lips: the consonants in five rows.

DOTS UNDER T, D, N

Retroflex versus dental

Dotted = curled. ṭ ṭh ḍ ḍh ṇ (and ṣ) are retroflex: curl the tip of your tongue back and touch the roof of your mouth. They sound heavy, like the “t” in Indian English “time”.

Plain = teeth. t th d dh n are dental: the tongue tip touches the back of the upper teeth. They sound soft, like the “t” in Spanish or French.

English is in between. English “t” and “d” sit between the two, so English speakers need to push one way or the other.

Examples. Gaṇeśa, Kṛṣṇa, maṇḍapa, Naṭarāja (retroflex); deva, tat tvam asi, Nandi (dental).

ZAPPY: Does it really make a difference?

PROF. BOLT: To meaning, yes. And to a chant’s sound: the curled letters give Sanskrit its weight.

Dot underneath: curl the tongue back. No dot: tongue to the teeth.

THE H THAT IS BREATH

kh, gh, th, dh, ph, bh…

An h means a puff. In IAST, an h after a consonant is breath: the same sound with a puff of air. Hold your hand before your mouth: you should feel it on kh but hardly on k.

th is not “the”. th is a breathy “t” as in “hot tea” said quickly; it is never the English “th” of “thin” or “this”. So Ratha is “rat-ha”, and tathāgata is “tat-haa-ga-ta”.

ph is not “f”. ph is a breathy “p”, as in “top hat”, not “f”. Phala (fruit) is “p-hala”.

Voiced ones too. gh, jh, ḍh, dh, bh are voiced with breath: Bhagavad Gītā, dharma, Siddhidātrī.

ZAPPY: So “Bhagavad” starts with a b-h?

PROF. BOLT: A b with a breath behind it, all in one sound. Not “b” then “h”.

h after a consonant = a puff of breath; th ≠ English th, ph ≠ f.

C, Ś, Ṣ AND S

The “ch” and the three hisses

🅲 c is always “ch”

c is never “k” or “s”. Candra is “chandra”, Cāmuṇḍā “Chaamundaa”, and ch is the same with breath (chāyā, shadow).

🌙 ś: the soft sh

ś (acute accent) is a palatal “sh”, as in “ship” with the tongue near the palate: Śiva, Śrī, Śaṅkara.

🌀 ṣ: the hard sh

ṣ (dot under) is a retroflex “sh”, tongue curled back: Viṣṇu, Kṛṣṇa, Lakṣmī, ṛṣi. Many speakers make little difference between ś and ṣ; careful chanters do.

🐍 s: plain s

s is just “s”, as in “sun”: Sarasvatī, Sītā, sūrya.

ZAPPY: So Śiva and Viṣṇu both have “sh”, but different ones?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Śiva’s is soft, from the palate; Viṣṇu’s is hard, tongue curled. Now you can hear it.

c = ch; ś soft sh, ṣ hard sh, s plain s.

ṄA, ÑA, Ṃ AND Ḥ

Nasals, the hum and the breath at the end

ṅ and ñ. ṅ is the “ng” of “sing” (Gaṅgā, śaṅkha). ñ is the “ny” of Spanish “señor” (pañca, five; vāñchita). The cluster jñ, as in jñāna, is said “gnya” or “dnya” in different regions; traditions differ.

ṃ, the anusvāra. A dot under (or above) m marks a nasal hum that takes the colour of the next sound: saṃskṛta, śailaputrīṃ. At the end of a line it is a soft “m”. Older books may print it ṁ; we use ṃ.

ḥ, the visarga. ḥ at the end of a word is a soft breath that echoes the vowel before it: namaḥ is “namaha”, śāntiḥ “shaantihi”, said lightly.

Together. Oṃ namaḥ śivāya: a hum, a breath, a soft sh, a long ā. You can now read it exactly.

ZAPPY: So I should say “namaha”, not “namah”?

PROF. BOLT: A light echo: “nama-ha”, with the last “a” barely there. It shouldn’t sound like a new syllable shouted.

ṅ = ng, ñ = ny, ṃ = hum, ḥ = breath echo.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Conventions: IAST as standardised for Sanskrit (Geneva, 1894) and its successor ISO 15919; the five places of articulation as set out in the Sanskrit phonetic tradition (śikṣā) and in Pāṇinian grammar. Wikipedia, “International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration”, “Sanskrit phonology”.

Sources differ: regional pronunciations of ṛ (ri / ru), jñ (gya / dnya / jnya), the visarga echo, and how strongly ś and ṣ are distinguished. We give the common options; follow your teacher.

Study notes: Pronunciations are approximations through English and should be checked by listening to a good chanter. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
