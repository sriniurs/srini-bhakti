The Ainmane

The Ancestral House and Its Lamp · explained and illustrated

Before the lamp

HOW THIS WORKS: The ainmane, the ancestral house of a Kodava clan: its plan, the hanging lamp, the kaimaḍa of the founding ancestor, the paṭṭedāra and the festivals that bring the clan home.

WHERE WE ARE

Every Kodava clan has one house that is more than a house: the ainmane, the ancestral home of the okka. It may be a hundred years old or several hundred; most of the family may live in Bengaluru or Dubai; but at Puttari and Kailpoḷd, at weddings and deaths, they come back to it, and before its hanging lamp the family prays.

This part explains what the ainmane is, how it is built and arranged, the lamp and the prayer corner, the kaimaḍa for the ancestor, who heads the house, and why the clans are restoring their houses.

The clans and the guru kāraṇa were introduced in Part 1. Previous: the mand. Next: life rites.

ZAPPY: Why “ain”?

PROF. BOLT: Commonly explained as “ancestral” or “main” house. Spellings: ainmane, ain mane, aimane.

ZAPPY: Does anyone live there?

PROF. BOLT: Often a branch of the family does. Others come for festivals.

One house for a whole clan.

WHAT IT IS

The clan’s home

Ainmane. The ancestral home of an okka, where the clan began in a place.

Shared. It belongs to the whole clan, not to one heir.

Clan name. Every member carries the okka name, and the house is where that name lives.

Rights. A bride receives rights in her husband’s ainmane at the wedding (see Part 8).

Many communities. Most Kodava-speaking communities have ainmanes too.

ZAPPY: Like a family temple?

PROF. BOLT: House, temple and meeting hall in one.

House, temple and meeting hall.

INSIDE

How the house is arranged

Central hall. The heart is the main hall where the sacred lamp hangs; here rites are performed.

Prayer corner. A corner or room is kept for prayer, for the ancestors and for Kāverī.

Stores. Granaries and store rooms held the paddy of the estate.

Verandah. A long verandah opens to the courtyard where guests are received.

Names. Kodava names for the rooms vary by house and region; we keep to English here.

ZAPPY: All ainmanes look like this?

PROF. BOLT: No. This is only the idea. Old houses grew room by room.

Built around the lamp.

BUILT TO LAST

Wood, laterite and tile

For rain. Steep tiled roofs shed the monsoon.

Wood. Pillars, doors and ceilings are of local timber, often carved.

Walls. Thick walls of mud and laterite keep the house cool and dry.

Courtyard. Many houses are built around or before an open courtyard.

Repairs. Clans pool money to repair the roof and woodwork.

ZAPPY: How old are they?

PROF. BOLT: Some claim many centuries. Most standing houses were rebuilt in the 19th and 20th centuries.

A house for heavy rain.

THE LAMP

The light that hangs in the hall

The lamp. A brass lamp hangs in the central hall; it is lit for every rite.

Before it. Prayers to the ancestors are said before the lamp, not before an idol.

Weddings. At a Kodava wedding, elders bless the couple before the lamp; there is no fire ritual and no priest.

Puttari. The Puttari procession leaves from and returns to the lamp.

Kailpoḷd. Weapons are laid before it.

ZAPPY: No idol?

PROF. BOLT: In many ainmanes, no. The lamp and the ancestors are enough.

Pray before the light.

THE KAIMAḌA

The ancestor’s shrine

Guru kāraṇa. The founding ancestor of the family is the guru kāraṇa.

Kaimaḍa. He is honoured at a small shrine, the kaimaḍa, near the house.

Offerings. Annual offerings are made, with family food.

Kāraṇas. Ancestors are believed to protect the descendants who remember them.

At festivals. Every festival begins with remembering them.

ZAPPY: Do all houses have one?

PROF. BOLT: Many do; customs differ between families.

The founder has his own house.

THE PAṬṬEDĀRA

Who leads the house

Paṭṭedāra. The head of the house, usually the senior male, is the paṭṭedāra.

Duties. He leads rites: lighting the lamp, offerings, the Puttari procession.

Clan meetings. Family matters are discussed at the ainmane.

Elders. Family elders, not priests, guide weddings and deaths.

Today. Clan associations often manage the house and its trust.

ZAPPY: What if he lives abroad?

PROF. BOLT: Another elder leads, or he flies home for Puttari.

An elder, not a priest.

COMING HOME

Festivals at the ainmane

Puttari. At harvest the whole okka gathers at the ainmane.

Kailpoḷd. Weapons are worshipped in the hall.

Weddings. Rites of welcome and rights are performed here.

Deaths. Final rites link the dead to the ancestors.

Reunions. Clan days bring scattered families together.

ZAPPY: Do the young come?

PROF. BOLT: They come for Puttari. The food helps.

The clan comes home.

RESTORING

Saving the old houses

Decay. Many ainmanes fell into disrepair as families moved away.

Trusts. Clan trusts and associations raise funds for repair and rebuilding.

Choices. Some restore old woodwork; others rebuild in concrete with the old plan.

Records. Families record their histories and photographs.

Identity. Restoring the house is restoring the clan.

ZAPPY: Concrete or wood?

PROF. BOLT: A hot debate in every okka.

Save the house, save the clan.
