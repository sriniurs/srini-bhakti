Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa

The River Mother Rises at Talakāverī · explained and illustrated

The mother returns

HOW THIS WORKS: Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa at Talakāverī: the legend of Kāverī and Agastya, the tīrthōdbhava at the Brahma Kuṇḍike, the holy water taken home and the Kanni Pūje in every Kodava house.

WHERE WE ARE

On the first day of the solar month of Tulā, in mid-October, a small tank on the Brahmagiri hills becomes the centre of Kodagu. At a moment fixed by astrologers, water wells up in the Brahma Kuṇḍike at Talakāverī, and the river Kāverī is said to be born again. This is Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa.

This part tells the legend of Kāverī and Agastya, describes Talakāverī and Bhāgamaṇḍala, the tīrthōdbhava, the holy water taken home, the Kanni Pūje in every house, the food and the walk of the pilgrims, and the story of the Kodava sari.

The river continues to the Triraṅga downstream. Previous: Kailpoḷd. Next: Igguthappa.

With the blessings of the Kāverī Amma,

ZAPPY: Is the river really born every year?

PROF. BOLT: The spring flows all year. On this day the water rises visibly at the fixed moment. Faith calls that her birth.

ZAPPY: Why Tulā?

PROF. BOLT: The sun enters Tulā (Libra). That saṅkramaṇa is hers.

The river is born again.

THE LEGEND

Kāverī and Agastya

The story

A Purāṇic legend (Kāverī Purāṇa, Skanda Purāṇa) as told in Kodagu; versions differ.

The sage Kavera was given a daughter by Brahmā; she was named Kāverī.
She married the sage Agastya on one condition: he must never leave her alone. He kept her in his water pot.
When he left her once, she flowed out of the pot and rushed away as a river. The people tried to stop her; she promised to return each year at Saṅkramaṇa.

THE SARI LEGEND

Why the pleats are at the back

The story. When Kāverī rushed away, the Kodavas, especially the women, tried to hold her back.

The force. The force of the water swept their sari pleats round to the back.

Ever since. Kodava women wear the sari with the pleats at the back, in honour of that day.

A legend. This is a tradition, told with love; dress historians may explain it other ways.

See. The dress is described in Part 1.

ZAPPY: Is it true?

PROF. BOLT: It is the Kodava answer, and the one every grandmother gives.

A sari shaped by a river.

TALAKĀVERĪ

The source on the Brahmagiri

The place. Talakāverī (“head of the Kāverī”) lies on the Brahmagiri hills, about 47 km from Madikeri.

Brahma Kuṇḍike. The source is a small sacred tank, the Brahma Kuṇḍike; a larger bathing tank is in front.

Shrines. Small shrines nearby honour Agastya’s liṅga (Agastyēśvara) and Gaṇapati.

The hilltop. Steps lead up the hill to a view across the Ghats.

Underground. From the source the water goes underground and reappears lower down.

ZAPPY: Can you see the river at the source?

PROF. BOLT: Just a spring and a tank. The great river begins small.

The great river begins as a spring.

BHĀGAMAṆḌALA

The confluence below

Triveṇī. At Bhāgamaṇḍala the Kāverī meets the Kannike and a hidden third stream, the Sujyōti.

The temple. The Bhagaṇḍēśvara temple stands here in the Kerala style (see Part 10).

First stop. Pilgrims bathe at the confluence before climbing to Talakāverī.

Pathaya. Before the day, rice is offered to the temple granary at a fixed time, a ritual announced in the press.

Pinda. In the following month families offer rites for ancestors here. (As reported.)

ZAPPY: Three rivers?

PROF. BOLT: Two you see, one you believe in. Like Prayāga.

Where three waters meet.

TĪRTHŌDBHAVA

The moment the water rises

The hour. Astrologers fix the moment of Tulā Saṅkramaṇa; it may fall by day or at night.

The rise. At that moment water is seen to well up in the Brahma Kuṇḍike: the tīrthōdbhava, “the birth of holy water”.

The crowd. Thousands wait; priests offer worship; the water is sprinkled on the crowd.

A saying. Kodavas say: to see a sea of humanity go on Saṅkramaṇa; to see the goddess, go another day.

Bathing. Pilgrims bathe in the tank and at Bhāgamaṇḍala.

ZAPPY: How does it rise?

PROF. BOLT: Pilgrims see it, priests confirm it. We leave the explanation to faith.

The water wells up.

THE HOLY WATER

Tīrtha taken home

Collected. The tīrtha is collected in pots and bottles and carried home.

Shared. It is distributed to families who could not go.

Kept. It is preserved in every Kodava home until the next Saṅkramaṇa.

For the dying. It is believed that a dying person given this water attains salvation.

In rites. It is used in rituals through the year.

ZAPPY: Water kept for a year?

PROF. BOLT: In the prayer corner, beside the lamp. A sip of the mother for every need.

The mother comes home in a pot.

KANNI PŪJE

Kāverī on a tray

Next day. On the day after the tīrthōdbhava, Kāverī is worshipped in every Kodava home in a simple ritual, the Kanni Pūje.

The tray. A brass or silver tray is heaped with rice; on it a decorated coconut (or a cucumber, in some homes) is placed, dressed as the goddess.

Beside it. Fresh flowers, three betel leaves, areca nuts and a lit lamp.

Women lead. Married women of the house commonly perform it; the family prays and thanks Kāveramma for the year’s bounty.

Symbol. Kāverī is honoured not as an idol but through the decorated symbol.

ZAPPY: A coconut as the goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Dressed in silk and flowers, on a bed of rice. The river is a mother, not a statue.

The goddess on a bed of rice.

THE FOOD

A vegetarian day

For a pork-loving community, the choice of a vegetarian menu shows how sacred the day is.

🥞 Dosa

Vegetarian dosa is cooked for the day.

🎃 Pumpkin curry

A pumpkin curry is the typical partner.

🚫 No meat

The Saṅkramaṇa meal is vegetarian in many homes, unusual in Kodagu.

🍚 Rice

Rice from the Kanni tray may be cooked later.

🎪 Jātre

Fair stalls at Bhāgamaṇḍala.

🙏 First the goddess

The meal follows the Kanni Pūje.

A rare vegetarian feast.

THE PILGRIMS

Barefoot with the dudi

The walk. Many Kodavas walk barefoot from Bhāgamaṇḍala up to Talakāverī on the day.

Dress. Men in white or black kupya and chele, women in Kodava saris.

Drum. They beat the dudi, the hand-drum, and sing the old songs (bāḷō pāṭ).

Everyone. People of all communities, and pilgrims from far away, come too.

Fair. The Kāverī Jātre offers shopping, food and rides.

ZAPPY: Barefoot up a hill?

PROF. BOLT: Seven or eight kilometres. Grandmothers do it.

Up the hill to the mother.
