Kodava Life Rites

Birth, Marriage and Death · explained and illustrated

Joined by the clans

HOW THIS WORKS: Kodava life rites: birth, the mangala wedding with its mukkālis, sammanda and Gaṅgā pūje, and the rites of death that make the dead ancestors.

WHERE WE ARE

A Kodava wedding has no fire, no seven steps and no priest. The couple sit on three-legged stools, elders bless them before the lamp, the two clans speak an ancient dialogue to join them, and at the end the bride draws water from a well while the groom’s family dance around her. Life’s great rites in Kodagu belong to the family and the clan.

This part covers the rites of birth and childhood, the full Kodava wedding (mangala) in its main stages, and the rites of death, as they are described by Kodava writers and the press.

The ainmane, where many rites happen, is in Part 7. Next: village deities.

ZAPPY: Why three legs?

PROF. BOLT: A three-legged stool stands firm on uneven ground. Make of that what you will.

Family, clan, and the lamp.

BIRTH

Welcoming a child

Care. After childbirth the mother rests for a period of care at home.

Announcing a son. Old accounts say the birth of a boy was announced with a gunshot; some say a bow and arrow were given. (As described.)

Naming. A naming ceremony follows, with the clan name first.

Rights. Every rite ties the child to the okka and ainmane.

Change. Today most births are in hospitals, but the family rites remain.

ZAPPY: A gun for a baby?

PROF. BOLT: A salute, not a toy. An old custom, now rare.

A new name in the clan.

THE DAY BEFORE

Ūrkūḍuvō and the bangles

Ūrkūḍuvō. On the eve, the village and relatives gather to prepare: the ūrkūḍuvō.

Bāḷe iḍuva. The bride is given bangles in a ceremony.

Separately. Traditionally each side held its own rites at its own house first.

Single muhūrta. Bride and groom were first blessed separately: the “single muhūrta”.

Feast. Pork, rice and drink are part of the feast. (Some Samājas now restrict liquor.)

ZAPPY: Two separate weddings?

PROF. BOLT: In the old form, yes: first each at home, then together.

The village comes to help.

BĀḶE BIRUD

Cutting the banana stems

The stems. Banana stems, decorated with flowers, are set along the path.

The cut. The groom, or a family member, cuts through them with the oḍi katti.

Meaning. Commonly explained as cutting through obstacles. (Common explanation.)

Groom’s dress. He wears a white kupya, chele, turban, pīcce katti and a jasmine garland.

Welcome. The bride’s mother feeds him milk, rice and sugar.

ZAPPY: One stroke?

PROF. BOLT: That is the ideal. The crowd counts.

Through the stems, to the bride.

DAMPATI MUHŪRTA

The blessing

The dais. The couple sit on mukkālis on a decorated dais.

The groom. He sprinkles rice towards the lamp, walks three times round the stool and sits.

Blessing. Elders and guests come one by one, sprinkle rice and bless them, offering milk and gifts.

Kāveramma. The families seek the blessing of Kāverī.

Garlands. He helps her rise and they exchange garlands.

ZAPPY: Does everyone bless?

PROF. BOLT: Hundreds of guests. It takes a long time.

Rice, milk and many hands.

SAMMANDA

The clans speak

Sammanda koḍupo. The spokesmen (aruvas) of the two clans exchange a formal, ancient dialogue.

Rights. The bride is given rights in her husband’s house and clan.

The coin. A gold coin is given as a pledge to look after her; it is tied and kept. (As described.)

This is the wedding. The dialogue, not a fire rite, solemnises the union.

Records. In the old days the sammanda was the legal proof of marriage.

ZAPPY: Like a contract?

PROF. BOLT: A spoken contract between clans, witnessed by everyone.

The words that make it binding.

GAṄGĀ PŪJE

The bride fetches water

Nīr eḍpo. At the end of the day the bride draws water from the well at her new home: the Gaṅgā pūje or nīr eḍpo, “fetching water”.

The walk. She carries the pots on her head with a head-rest, taking small, careful steps.

The teasing. The groom’s family dance round her and try to delay her.

Meaning. She shows patience and strength, and takes up her place in the household. (Common explanation.)

All night. The dancing may go on to the early hours.

ZAPPY: Do they make it hard?

PROF. BOLT: Very. That is the fun.

The first water of a new home.

FEAST AND DANCE

Vālaga and pork

Vālaga. The band plays outside; anyone who wants to dance, dances.

Food. Pork curry, rice and kaḍambuṭṭ are central.

Drink. Alcohol was traditional; some Kodava associations now urge a ban at weddings.

Short. Kodava weddings are shorter than many Indian weddings.

Mixed weddings. Many couples combine Kodava rites with other customs.

ZAPPY: Is the band essential?

PROF. BOLT: No band, no Kodava wedding.

Dance till the pots are home.

DEATH

The last rites

The elders. Death rites (cāvu) are conducted by family elders.

News. Old accounts say a death was announced by gunshots. (As described.)

Holy water. A sip of Kāverī tīrtha is given to the dying.

Ancestors. After the rites the dead join the ancestors (kāraṇas) honoured at the ainmane.

Clan. Special rites also govern widows and children’s clan rights.

ZAPPY: Burial or cremation?

PROF. BOLT: Customs differ by family and period; we do not generalise.

From the clan to the ancestors.
