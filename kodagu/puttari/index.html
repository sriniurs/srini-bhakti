Puttari

The Festival of New Rice · explained and illustrated

The night of the new rice

HOW THIS WORKS: Puttari, Kodagu’s festival of new rice: the hour fixed at Igguthappa’s temple, the restraint, the night in the field with lamp, milk and gun, and the songs and dances after.

WHERE WE ARE

On a full-moon night in the Kodava month of Birchiyār, every ancestral house in Kodagu walks out into its paddy fields after dark. A girl carries a lamp, an elder carries the gun, and a man ties a bundle of leaves to the first ripe stalks, pours milk on them, and cuts a handful while everyone shouts “Poli, poli, dēva!”, “Grow, grow, O God!” A shot is fired into the night. The new rice is home.

This is Puttari (Puthari, Huttari), “new rice”, the greatest festival of the Kodava year. This guide follows it from the day astrologers fix its hour at the shrine of Igguthappa, through the fourteen days of restraint, the night at Pāḍi, the night in every home, the food, the house-songs and the stick-dance on the village green.

The Kodavas themselves are introduced in Part 1; the god whose temple opens the festival in Igguthappa. The coast’s own new-rice festival is Koral Parba.

ZAPPY: A gun at a harvest festival?

PROF. BOLT: Kodagu is a land of farmers who were also soldiers. The gun is fired to the sky, not at anyone: a salute to the new crop.

ZAPPY: Is it the same day everywhere?

PROF. BOLT: Pāḍi goes first, the rest of Kodagu the next night. The hour is the same for everyone, fixed by the astrologers.

New rice brought home by lamplight.

NEW RICE

The name and the season

Puttari. In Kodava takk, the Kodava language, put is “new” and ari is “rice”: Puttari is the festival of the new rice. In Kannaḍa it is Huttari; families also say Puttari poḷd, “the Puttari festival”, and Puttari namme.

Birchiyār. It falls in the Kodava month of Birchiyār (November–December), on the night the moon stands in Rohiṇī, close to the full moon.

The rice of the valleys. Kodagu’s flat valley floors (bayal) have been paddy land for centuries, with coffee and forest on the slopes. Rice was its wealth before coffee.

The grandest festival. Of the three great Kodava festivals, Kailpoḷd, Kāverī Saṅkramaṇa and Puttari, Puttari is the grandest and most elaborate.

For everyone in Kodagu. Kodavas, Kodagu Gowdas and other communities of the district all keep it (see Part 12).

ZAPPY: Even families who no longer grow rice?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Many make a token field, or bring a few sheaves from a neighbour. The festival outlived the farms.

The year’s great night belongs to the rice.

FIXING THE HOUR

Astrologers at Igguthappa’s shrine

Two weeks ahead. About fifteen days before the festival, the families connected with Igguthappa’s temple at Pāḍi (Kakkabe) are summoned: the Pardanda family who look after the god, the temple officials, and leading families of Pāḍi, Nelji and Perūr.

Offerings. They bring rice, coconut, jaggery, bananas and areca nut to the shrine.

The astrologers. Astrologers from Ammangeri village work out the day and the exact hour for tying the leaves, cutting the sheaves and the feast.

Thirteen families. Recent reports describe representatives of thirteen families and the temple office-bearers meeting with the astrologers to settle the date.

Announced. The hour is then announced on Malma hill, at the Pardanda ainmane, and at the Makki Śāstāvu temple at Bettu near Nāpoklu, and spreads across Kodagu.

ZAPPY: Why does one temple decide for the whole district?

PROF. BOLT: Because Igguthappa is the god of rain and rice. His festival comes first, and his hour becomes everyone’s.

The god of rice sets the hour.

DĒVA KAṬṬU

Fourteen days of restraint

On Malma hill. Astrologers, priests, caretakers, musicians and devotees climb Malma (Igguthappa Beṭṭa) and announce the hour at a shrine in a sacred grove.

Dēva kaṭṭu. There the caretaker announces the dēva kaṭṭu, “the god’s binding”: fourteen days of restraint for the area of the temple.

What is forbidden. No eating meat, no alcohol, no harming animals or people by hand or word, no pulling plants, no cutting trees. No weddings or funerals are held.

In the temple. Only the Satyanārāyaṇa pūjā may be performed during this time.

Kaladcha. On the day before Puttari the caretaker returns to Malma and declares the restraints lifted (kaṭṭu muripo); the temple deities are adorned, drums play, and decorated oxen dance with rice bags on their backs.

ZAPPY: No cutting even a plant?

PROF. BOLT: The land is about to give its rice. For two weeks people give the land back its peace.

Two weeks of peace before the harvest.

PĀḌI POḶD

The first night, at Igguthappa’s temple

Deva poḷd. The first night is Pāḍi poḷd (also dēva poḷd): Puttari is celebrated first at the Pāḍi Igguthappa temple, Kakkabe.

Who. The temple priest presides over the rites; the dēva takka, the hereditary caretaker, performs them.

The rite. At the appointed hour, near the temple, the leaf bundles are tied (nere kaṭṭuvo) and the first sheaves are cut (kadh eḍpo), as in every home the next night.

Prasāda. A festive meal is served at the temple; one year’s report times the nere kaṭṭuvo at 7.15 pm, the cutting at 8.15 pm, then the prasāda.

Then the land. Once the god has his new rice, the rest of Kodagu may take theirs. See Igguthappa.

ZAPPY: Why does the god eat first?

PROF. BOLT: Good manners and good theology. The giver of the rice receives its first sheaves.

The god takes the first sheaves.

NERE KAṬṬUVO

Tying the leaf bundles

The nere. A nere is a small bundle of leaves tied round a piece of creeper (inoli) with fibre. Neres are the blessing tied to the crop and the house.

Which leaves. Reports name leaves of arali, mango, jackfruit, kumbaḷi and cashew; families keep their own list.

Who. After dusk a designated man of the family ties them and lays them in a basket.

At the house shrine. The family first prays at the nellakki naḍubāḍe, the sacred central hall of the ainmane with its hanging lamp (see The Ainmane).

Invoking the gods. On the way to the field the family calls on its gods by name: Igguthappa, Kāverī Amma, Mahādēva, Bhadrakāḷī and the village deity.

ZAPPY: Why leaves?

PROF. BOLT: Green leaves of fruit trees: the plenty of the land, tied to the grain. The crop is wished every other kind of abundance too.

The land’s plenty tied to the grain.

THE NIGHT IN THE FIELD

Kadh eḍpo, cutting the first sheaves

The story

Nāḍ poḷd: the second night, in every ancestral house, as described by Kodava writers and the Kodagu press.

After dusk an unmarried girl carrying a lit lamp on a plate leads the family to the field; women carry the plate of rice (taḷiyakki bolcha), the head of the house (paṭṭedāra) carries the gun.
At the field the designated man ties a nere to the base of a clump of paddy and pours milk (and ghee) over it from the kutti, a sacred bamboo vessel.
He cuts the sheaves with a sickle while everyone shouts “Poli, poli, dēva!”, “Increase, increase, O God!”
A gun is fired into the air (often three times) and crackers burst.
An odd number of sheaves, five, seven, nine or eleven, is cut and carried home in procession, still to the cry of “Poli, poli, dēva!”

ZAPPY: Why an odd number?

PROF. BOLT: Odd numbers are auspicious in many South Indian rites: never complete, always more to come.

A cry for increase and a shot at the stars.

THE SHEAVES COME HOME

Tied to the house

To the nellakki. The sheaves are carried to the house’s prayer place, the nellakki naḍubāḍe, and offered.

Tied everywhere. Sheaves and neres are tied at the front of the house, the kitchen, the prayer place, the granary and the cow shed, and on farm tools.

Dhānya Lakṣmī. Families describe the moment as welcoming Dhānya Lakṣmī, the goddess of grain, into the house.

The new rice is cooked. A few grains of the new paddy are husked and added to the festive food.

Crackers. The night ends with firecrackers and a family feast.

ZAPPY: Rice tied to the cow shed?

PROF. BOLT: Every part of the farm that helped grow it gets its share of the blessing, the cows most of all.

The grain goddess enters the house.

THE PUTTARI TABLE

Tambuṭṭ and the feast

Recipes vary by family; these are the dishes most often named in reports of the festival.

🍮 Tambuṭṭ

The Puttari sweet: roasted rice flour mixed with ripe mashed banana, sesame, grated coconut, jaggery or sugar and ghee, with cardamom.

🍃 On a leaf

Served on a peepal leaf before the nellakki, shared among the family.

🥔 Kalinji

A boiled tuber is also served in some families.

🍚 New rice

A little of the new rice goes into the meal.

🍖 The feast

After the restraint of the season, a great family feast; meat dishes return on the following days in most homes.

👪 Together

Families living far away come home to the ainmane for this meal.

Rice, banana and sesame: the taste of Puttari.

AFTER THE NIGHT

House-songs and the hunt

Mane pāḍo. On the day after Nāḍ Puttari, four folk singers carry small hourglass drums (duḍi) and visit each house; two of them sing verses praising the family. (House-singing.)

Gifts. The household gives them rice and gifts in return.

The hunt. Traditionally, at a time fixed by the astrologer, the village youth set out on a hunt after the festival, with rules for dividing the game. (Old custom; hunting is now restricted by law.)

Eleven days once. Puttari is said to have run for eleven days in the past; today it is kept over a few days.

Visits. Families visit relatives and neighbours with tambuṭṭ.

ZAPPY: Singers who praise each family?

PROF. BOLT: A village’s memory in song: who your ancestors were, what your house has done. It binds the village together.

A village sings its families.

THE KŌLĀṬ

The stick-dance under the moon

Practised in the moonlight. For eight evenings before Puttari, village men practise the Puttari kōlāṭ (stick-dance) in the moonlight on the mand, the village green.

After the festival. A few days after Puttari the village gathers on the mand: horns and large drums play while men dance in circles, striking short rattan sticks (kōl) in rhythm.

Invocations. At the mands the names of Aiyappa, Mahādēva and Bhagavatī are invoked.

Ūrorme. Then comes the ūrorme, the village gathering, which closes the festival season.

More dances. Bōlakāṭ, Ummattāṭ and Kombāṭ, and the mand itself, are in Part 6.

ZAPPY: Why sticks?

PROF. BOLT: Rhythm you can see and hear. Every strike lands together, or the whole circle hears the mistake.

Sticks striking in one rhythm.

PUTTARI TODAY

In Kodagu, Mysuru and Bengaluru

Away from the fields. Many Kodavas now live outside Kodagu. Families in a region gather and celebrate together, keeping the old order.

Samājas. The Kodava Samājas of Mysuru, Bengaluru and other cities hold “Puttari Namme”: a token paddy field is made on the grounds, the leaves are tied and the sheaves cut at the astrologer’s hour, and tambuṭṭ is shared.

Gowda Samājas. The Kodagu Gowda Samājas celebrate it with equal fervour.

Temples. Temples across Kodagu, such as the Mṛtyuñjaya temple at Baḍagarakēri, hold collective Puttari for their villages.

Why it lasts. A festival of home, family and land is what people far from home miss most.

ZAPPY: A paddy field in a city school?

PROF. BOLT: A few square metres of rice, grown for one night. That is how much the night matters.

A paddy field for one night in the city.
