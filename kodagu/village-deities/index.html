Village Deities of Kodagu

Bhagavatī, Bhadrakāḷī and the Kōlas · explained and illustrated

Every village has its goddess

HOW THIS WORKS: The village deities of Kodagu: Bhagavatī and Bhadrakāḷī, Aiyappa and the guardians, the sacred groves, and the namme festivals with their horses, horn dances, kōlas and disguises.

WHERE WE ARE

Beyond Kāverī and Igguthappa, every Kodava village has its own gods. The most common is the goddess Bhagavatī, also called Bhadrakāḷī or Povvadi, worshipped in nearly every village; with her are Aiyappa of the forest, Mahādēva, and many guardian spirits. Their annual festivals, called namme, fill the mands with drums, horn dances, masked players and wooden horses.

This part covers the village goddess and her companions, the sacred groves, the rules before a namme, the festival rituals, the Kerala-linked kōla performers, the disguise festival and the old rice-gifts to a temple across the border.

The mand where these festivals happen is in Part 6; Igguthappa in Part 5. Next: temples.

BHAGAVATĪ

The goddess of every village

Names. Bhadrakāḷī, also called Bhagavatī or Povvadi, is worshipped in every village in Kodagu.

Protector. She guards the village from disease, danger and misfortune.

Arrival. Temple traditions tell how she came to Kodagu: one says she came from Sōḷi village with Viṣṇumūrti and an assistant god, and with the communities who serve her rites. (Temple tradition.)

Many temples. Each village or pair of villages has its own Bhadrakāḷī temple.

Festival. Her annual festival is the Povvadi namme or Bhagavatī namme.

ZAPPY: Fierce?

PROF. BOLT: Fierce to danger, kind to her people.

The guardian of each village.

HER COMPANIONS

Forest gods and guardians

The set of gods differs from village to village; these are the common ones named in accounts.

🏹 Aiyappa

A forest god of the hills, worshipped in groves; distinct in form from Sabarimala.

🔱 Mahādēva

Śiva, widely worshipped in nāḍ temples.

⚔️ Guardians

Spirits who guard boundaries and serve the goddess.

🐍 Nāgas

Snake shrines and stones.

🌳 Grove gods

Deities of the dēvarakāḍu.

🧘 Viṣṇumūrti

Named in some Bhagavatī temple traditions.

A goddess with a household of gods.

DĒVARAKĀḌU

The sacred groves

Dēvarakāḍu. “God’s forests”: sacred groves attached to village deities.

Protected. By tradition no tree may be felled and no animal hunted inside.

Many. Kodagu has a very large number of these groves, from small patches to large woods.

Ecology. They shelter old trees, springs and wildlife among the coffee.

Threats. Encroachment and neglect threaten some; communities and the state work to protect them.

ZAPPY: Why not cut them?

PROF. BOLT: The god lives there. Faith protected the forest before any law did.

Forests kept by faith.

BEFORE THE NAMME

Namme kaṭṭ: the rules

Announcement. About a week before, villagers gather at the temple to announce the namme kaṭṭ.

Rules. For those days the village keeps rules: often no meat, no hunting, no noise, no leaving the village, by local custom.

Preparation. Costumes, horses and offerings are made ready.

Blessing. In one tradition villagers first visit a nearby Bhagavatī temple with duḍi and drums to seek blessings for a smooth festival.

Turns. Sister villages may host in alternate years.

ZAPPY: What happens if you break the rules?

PROF. BOLT: Elders decide. Most people would not dare.

A village holds its breath.

THE FESTIVAL

A namme day

Processions. Village gods’ emblems are carried to the Bhadrakāḷī temple.

Horses. In some villages wooden horses (kudure) of the gods are dressed and carried in procession.

Kombāṭ. The horn dance is performed at Bhadrakāḷī festivals.

Offerings. Rice, coconuts, fowl and other offerings by local custom.

Feast. The village eats together on the mand.

ZAPPY: Horses?

PROF. BOLT: Wooden ones, carried on men’s shoulders. The gods ride.

The gods ride wooden horses.

THE KŌLAS

Gods who dance

Kōla. In several villages the gods appear as kōlas: performers in costume and paint who embody the deity, as in Kerala’s theyyam and Tulu Nadu’s bhūta kōla.

Performers. These rites are traditionally performed by particular communities, such as the Banna and Maleya.

Kerala roots. Temple traditions tie the gods and their performers to Kerala.

Possession. The kōla speaks and blesses as the god.

Compare. See Bhūta Kōla in Tulu Nadu.

ZAPPY: Like Tulu Nadu?

PROF. BOLT: Cousins across the hills. Kodagu sits between Kerala and the Tulu coast.

The god puts on a face.

THE DISGUISE FESTIVAL

Bōḍ namme

Bōḍ. Some villages hold a festival, often called Bōḍ namme, in which people go about in disguise.

Groups. Kodavas, Kembaṭṭis and others perform in groups, in costume, as characters.

Licence. For those days teasing and comedy are allowed.

Old. Such festivals appear in the 1870 Gazetteer of Coorg.

Where. Accounts name villages such as Halligaṭṭ, Chembebelloor and Bilugunda.

ZAPPY: Why disguises?

PROF. BOLT: Some say to please the goddess with laughter. All say it is great fun.

Faith with a sense of humour.

ACROSS THE BORDER

Rice for Baithurappa

Baithurappa. The god of Baithūr (Vayathūr) near Iritty, in Kerala’s Kannur district, is honoured by Kodagu people.

Eth-pōrāṭa. Before his festival, loads of rice were sent from Kodagu on oxen.

Prasāda. The rice is cooked and served to devotees during the festival.

Visitors. Kodagu people still visit the annual festival.

Meaning. It shows the old bond across the Ghats.

ZAPPY: Kodava gods in Kerala?

PROF. BOLT: The hills never were a wall. Gods, rice and families crossed them.

Rice across the Ghats.
