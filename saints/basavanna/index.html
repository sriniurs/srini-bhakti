Basavaṇṇa

and the Vacanas of the Śaraṇas · explained and illustrated

The hall of experience

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Basavaṇṇa was: the boy from Bāgevāḍi, the minister of Kalyāṇa, the Anubhava Maṇṭapa where anyone could speak, the iṣṭaliṅga worn on the body, “work is worship”, the vacanas and the śaraṇas who wrote them, the crisis that ended Kalyāṇa, and his legacy in Karnataka.

WHERE WE ARE

In twelfth-century Karnataka, a minister of state gathered weavers, cobblers, washermen, queens and ascetics, women and men, in one hall to talk about God and how to live. He was Basavaṇṇa, and the short Kannada prose-poems he and his companions spoke, the vacanas, are among the great treasures of Indian literature.

This guide tells his life as the sources and traditions give it, explains his key ideas, quotes his most famous vacana, introduces the śaraṇas around him, and describes how the movement lives on. Dates and details differ between sources, and we say so where they do.

FROM BĀGEVĀḌI TO KŪḌALASAṄGAMA

The young Basava

The story

Harihara’s Basavarājadēvara Ragaḷe (c. 1180), the earliest account; Basava Purāṇa; Wikipedia, “Basava”.

Basava was born at Basavana Bāgevāḍi in north Karnataka into a Śaiva brāhmaṇa family, to Mādarasa and Mādalāmbike, and named after Nandi, Śiva’s bull (Basava in Kannada).
Tradition says that as a boy he questioned the sacred-thread ceremony that marked caste, and refused it, preferring a devotion open to all. (Tradition.)
He went to Kūḍalasaṅgama, where the Kṛṣṇā and Malaprabhā meet, and studied for twelve years at a Śaiva school there.
The god of that place, Saṅgamēśvara, “lord of the meeting rivers”, became his own: every vacana he spoke ends with “Kūḍalasaṅgamadēva”.
From there he went to Kalyāṇa, capital of the Kalacuri king Bijjaḷa, and married Nīlāmbike (Nīlagaṅgāmbike).

ZAPPY: He signed every poem with a river god?

PROF. BOLT: With the god where two rivers meet, Zappy. Fitting for someone who brought people together.

The lord of the meeting rivers.

MINISTER OF KALYĀṆA

Power used for reform

From accountant to chief minister. Basava began as an accountant at Bijjaḷa’s court and rose to chief minister (daṇḍanāyaka), in charge of the treasury.

Treasury for the people. He used his position to support devotees and reforms: feeding communities, supporting workers, and funding the gatherings of the śaraṇas.

A magnet. Devotees came to Kalyāṇa from all over: weavers, potters, cobblers, washermen, ferrymen, prostitutes who had renounced their trade, and kings who had renounced their thrones.

Bhakti Bhaṇḍāri. His followers called him the treasurer of devotion: the man who managed the king’s money and gave away God’s.

ZAPPY: A minister using his job for good?

PROF. BOLT: The rarest kind, Zappy. And the most dangerous, as we’ll see.

Power in service.

THE ANUBHAVA MAṆṬAPA

A hall where anyone could speak

“Hall of experience”. Basava founded the Anubhava Maṇṭapa, an assembly where men and women of every background met to discuss spiritual, social and economic questions as equals.

Who spoke. Tradition names hundreds of śaraṇas (“those who have surrendered”), many of them women; the mystic Allama Prabhu is said to have presided.

What they said. Their sayings, the vacanas, were often born in these discussions: short, direct, in ordinary Kannada.

Often called a parliament. Modern writers sometimes call it the first democratic assembly; historians caution that much of what we know comes from later texts. (Sources differ.)

ZAPPY: Women spoke too?

PROF. BOLT: Spoke, argued and wrote, Zappy. Some of the greatest vacanas are by women.

Experience shared as equals.

HIS KEY IDEAS

Iṣṭaliṅga, kāyaka, dāsoha

🔘 Iṣṭaliṅga

A small personal liṅga worn on the body on a cord, given to every devotee regardless of birth or sex. Worship does not need a temple or a priest.

🛠️ Kāyaka

Honest work, of any kind, done as worship. “Kāyakave kailāsa”: work is Kailāsa itself. No trade is lower than another.

🍚 Dāsoha

Sharing the fruits of work with the community. Wealth is held in trust, not hoarded.

⚖️ Equality

The vacanas reject caste, sex discrimination, empty ritual and animal sacrifice; the devotee’s conduct is what counts.

ZAPPY: So a cobbler’s work is as holy as a priest’s?

PROF. BOLT: In Basava’s view, Zappy, if it’s honest and offered to Śiva, yes.

Work is worship.

IN THE VERSE

Uḷḷavaru śivālayava

The most famous vacana
uḷḷavaru śivālayava māḍuvarunānēnu māḍali baḍavanayyāenna kālē kambha dēhavē dēgulaśiravē honna kaḷasavayyākūḍalasaṅgamadēvā kēḷayyāsthāvarakkaḷivuṇṭu jaṅgamakkaḷivilla
The rich will build temples for Śiva. What shall I do? I am poor, my lord. My legs are the pillars, my body the shrine, my head a golden finial. Listen, lord of the meeting rivers: what stands still shall fall, what moves shall stay.
WordMeaning

kambhapillar

The body as temple. A poor devotee cannot build a stone temple, so he becomes one. Stone temples crumble; the living devotee, the jaṅgama, endures.

Kannada vacana (12th century, public domain); text as given in Wikipedia, “Vachana sahitya”, and the rec.music.indian.classical archive; our own rendering.
The living outlast the stone.

THE ŚARAṆAS

Allama, Akka Mahādevi and others

Vacana collections as we have them were compiled centuries later; the Śūnyasampādane gathers the dialogues of Allama and the śaraṇas.

🌀 Allama Prabhu

The mystic whose vacanas, signed “Guhēśvara”, are paradoxical and fierce; tradition says he presided over the Anubhava Maṇṭapa. With Basava and Akka he forms the movement’s great three.

🌸 Akka Mahādevi

A young woman who left a king and wandered, clothed only in her hair, for her divine lover “Cennamallikārjuna” of Śrīśaila. Her vacanas are among the most passionate in Indian poetry.

📚 Cennabasavaṇṇa

Basava’s nephew and a systematiser of the movement’s teaching.

⛵ The workers

Śaraṇas of every trade wrote vacanas signed with their work: Ambigara Cauḍayya the ferryman, Madivāḷa Mācayya the washerman, Ayadakki Lakkamma who gathered fallen rice.

ZAPPY: A ferryman wrote poems?

PROF. BOLT: Sharp ones, Zappy. In Kalyāṇa, anyone who experienced God could speak.

Every trade has its saint.

THE KALYĀṆA CRISIS

A marriage that shook a kingdom

The story

Later Vīraśaiva tradition and the Basava Purāṇa; historians differ on details.

The śaraṇas, tradition says, blessed a marriage between the daughter of a brāhmaṇa śaraṇa and the son of a cobbler śaraṇa: in their eyes, devotees had no caste.
The orthodox were outraged. King Bijjaḷa had the fathers punished severely, and in some tellings put to death.
Violence followed in Kalyāṇa. Bijjaḷa himself was killed, and the śaraṇas scattered, carrying their palm-leaf vacanas to safety.
Basava left the city and returned to Kūḍalasaṅgama, where, by most accounts, he died and was laid to rest.
The movement survived in its songs, its communities and its iṣṭaliṅgas, and grew into the Liṅgāyata tradition.

ZAPPY: His idea cost him everything?

PROF. BOLT: His city and his office, Zappy. Not his idea: that outlived the kingdom.

The kingdom fell; the idea lived.

HIS LEGACY

Liṅgāyatas, Karnataka and the world

The Liṅgāyata tradition. Millions in Karnataka and beyond follow the path of the śaraṇas, wearing the iṣṭaliṅga; maṭhas run schools and feed the poor in the spirit of dāsoha. Whether Liṅgāyatas form a separate religion or a Śaiva tradition within Hinduism is debated within the community and in politics. Traditions differ.

Places. His memorial is at Kūḍalasaṅgama (Bāgalkōṭe district); Basavakalyāṇa (old Kalyāṇa, Bīdar district) has a 108-foot statue and the Anubhava Maṇṭapa memorial.

Honours. A statue in Parliament (2003), a commemorative coin, and a statue on the Thames at Lambeth in London, unveiled on 14 November 2015.

Basava Jayantī. His birth is celebrated on Vaiśākha Śukla Tṛtīyā, the same day as Akṣaya Tṛtīyā.

ZAPPY: A Kannada saint on the Thames?

PROF. BOLT: Since 2015, Zappy. Equality travels well.

From Kalyāṇa to the world.

WHAT HE TEACHES

The living temple

God within reach. No priest, no temple, no birth is needed: the devotee carries the liṅga and becomes the temple.

Dignity of work. Every honest trade is worship; no worker is lower than another.

Share what you earn. Dāsoha makes generosity a duty, not a favour.

Speak plainly. The vacanas show that truth can be said in a few clear sentences in your own language.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Basavaṇṇa?

PROF. BOLT: Do today’s work honestly and share something from it, Zappy. That’s kāyaka and dāsoha.

Work, share, be the temple.
