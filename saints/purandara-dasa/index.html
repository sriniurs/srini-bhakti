Purandara Dāsa

and the Haridāsas of Karnataka · explained and illustrated

Singing God’s name in Kannada

HOW THIS WORKS: Who the Haridāsas were: the rich merchant who became Purandara Dāsa, the father of Carnatic music teaching, the warrior-poet Kanaka Dāsa and the wall at Udupi, their songs in plain Kannada against pride and caste, and the line of dāsas who followed.

WHERE WE ARE

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, singers in simple clothes walked the streets of Karnataka with cymbals and a tambura, singing about God in the everyday Kannada of farmers and traders. They were the Haridāsas, “servants of Hari”, and the greatest of them, Purandara Dāsa, also laid down the lessons with which every Carnatic music student still begins.

This guide tells Purandara Dāsa’s life and work, the story of Kanaka Dāsa, the shepherd-warrior who became a poet-saint, and the movement they belonged to. Their god Viṭṭhala has his own guide in Viṭṭhala.

THE HARIDĀSA MOVEMENT

God’s name in the people’s language

Who they were. Devotees in the Mādhva (Dvaita) tradition of Udupi who took the vow of wandering and singing. Some were monks, most householders; they lived simply, often by alms.

Their language. Not Sanskrit but everyday Kannada. They turned the teaching of the Bhāgavata and of Madhva into songs anyone could sing in the fields or at home.

Two streams. Tradition speaks of the Vyāsakūṭa, the scholars writing in Sanskrit, and the Dāsakūṭa, the singers in Kannada. Both served the same teaching.

The founders. The tradition names Śrīpādarāja (fifteenth century) as its father, and his pupil Vyāsatīrtha, guru to the Vijayanagara kings, as its great patron; Purandara and Kanaka were Vyāsatīrtha’s disciples.

ZAPPY: Sanskrit wasn’t enough?

PROF. BOLT: Not for a farmer’s daughter, Zappy. The dāsas sang so she could understand too.

Theology you can sing.

THE MISER AND THE NOSE-RING

How Śrīnivāsa Nāyaka changed

The story

Popular Haridāsa tradition; not in the main encyclopaedic sources.

Śrīnivāsa Nāyaka was a rich dealer in gold, silver and jewels, famous for his wealth and for his meanness.
A poor brāhmaṇa begged him for help with his son’s thread ceremony, and was sent away again and again for months.
The brāhmaṇa went to the merchant’s wife, Sarasvatī Bāī, who gave him her diamond nose-ring. He brought it to her husband’s shop to pawn.
The merchant recognised the ring, locked it away and hurried home to demand it from his wife. In terror she prayed, and, the story goes, found the ring in her cup. Back at the shop, his locked box was empty and the brāhmaṇa gone.
Shaken, he understood that God had come to teach him. He gave away his entire wealth and set out as a wandering singer.

ZAPPY: Did it really happen?

PROF. BOLT: It’s how devotees tell it, Zappy, and we mark it so. Historians agree he really did give everything away.

Wealth given away.

PURANDARA DĀSA

The singer of Viṭṭhala

Life. Born Śrīnivāsa Nāyaka about 1470–84 (scholars differ) into a Kannada Mādhva family; his birthplace is disputed between Āraga near Tīrthahaḷḷi, Purandaraghaṭṭa and Purandaragaḍ near Pune, and a 2018 Karnataka committee favoured Tīrthahaḷḷi.

Initiation. At Vijayanagara he became the disciple of Vyāsatīrtha, who gave him the name Purandara Dāsa. He travelled to Paṇḍharpur, Tirupati and the great temples, singing.

His signature. Every song ends with the aṅkita “Puraṃdara Viṭṭhala”. Devotees say he composed hundreds of thousands of songs; thousands of kīrtanas are known.

His end. He died at Vijayanagara in 1564, according to his son Madhvapati. A pavilion beside Hampi’s Viṭṭhala temple, the Purandara Maṇṭapa, keeps his memory; a statue stands at Alipiri, the foot of Tirumala.

ZAPPY: Hundreds of thousands of songs?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the devotees’ number, Zappy. The real treasure is the few thousand we still sing.

Every song signed to Viṭṭhala.

THE GRANDFATHER OF CARNATIC MUSIC

Why every student starts with him

🎵 Māyāmāḷavagauḷa

He made this rāga the basic scale for teaching; Carnatic students still begin with it.

📶 Graded lessons

Svaravaḷis, jaṇṭi svaras, alaṅkāras (scale exercises in the seven tāḷas), then simple songs called gītas: a step-by-step path still used in every music class.

👑 Saṅgīta Pitāmaha

For this he is called the grandfather of Carnatic music. Tyāgarāja and later composers built on the forms he shaped. See Tyāgarāja.

🌾 Folk and classical

He set his words both to classical rāgas and to folk tunes, so that the same song could be sung in a concert hall or a paddy field.

ZAPPY: My music teacher’s first lesson is from him?

PROF. BOLT: Very likely, Zappy, five hundred years on.

Every lesson begins with him.

WHAT HE SANG ABOUT

Pride, caste and true devotion

Against show. His songs mock outward religion without inner change: bathing in holy rivers while cheating others, wearing marks while speaking lies.

Against caste pride. He sang that birth does not decide a person’s worth; devotion and conduct do.

The goodness of ordinary life. Many songs take images from the kitchen, the market and the farm: God as the fruit-seller, life as a borrowed house.

Simple devotion. Above all, he sang that the name of Hari is enough: chant it with a sincere heart, anywhere, by anyone.

ZAPPY: Even saints criticised pretend piety?

PROF. BOLT: Sharply, Zappy, and with humour. That’s why people loved them.

Devotion without pretence.

KANAKA DĀSA

The warrior who became a poet

The story

Wikipedia, “Kanaka Dasa”.

Timmappa Nāyaka was born in 1508 or 1509 at Bāḍa near Baṅkāpura in present Hāvēri district, into a Kannada Kuruba (shepherd) family.
He served as a warrior at the fort of Baṅkāpura. Badly wounded in battle, he turned to chanting the name of Hari.
He became a disciple of Vyāsatīrtha and took the name Kanaka Dāsa; his songs end with the aṅkita “Kāgineleya Ādikeśava”, after the god of Kāginele.
Besides about 240 songs he wrote long poems: Mohanataraṅgiṇi, Nalacaritre, Haribhaktisāra, and Rāmadhānyacarite, in which the humble grain rāgi argues with rice for Rāma’s favour and wins.
He died at Kāginele in 1606, aged nearly a hundred. Since 2008 his birthday, Kanaka Jayantī, is a Karnataka state festival.

ZAPPY: Rāgi argued with rice?

PROF. BOLT: And won, Zappy, because it endures hardship. Kanaka knew which side he was on.

The humble grain wins.

KANAKANA KIṆḌI

The window at Udupi

Kept outside. When Kanaka came to Udupi, the temple priests, judging him low-born, would not let him in, though Vyāsatīrtha himself asked them to.

He sang outside. He stood behind the temple and sang to Kṛṣṇa with longing.

The wall cracked. The story says the wall cracked open and the image of Kṛṣṇa turned to face him. The opening is honoured as Kanakana Kiṇḍi, “Kanaka’s window”, and to this day devotees take their first view of Kṛṣṇa through it.

Its meaning. For Karnataka, the window stands for God turning toward the devotee whom society turned away. See Udupi Śrī Kṛṣṇa.

ZAPPY: God turned around for him?

PROF. BOLT: So the story says, Zappy. And the window has been kept open ever since.

God turned toward him.

THE LINE OF DĀSAS

From Śrīpādarāja to Jagannātha Dāsa

🌱 The first wave

Śrīpādarāja and Vyāsatīrtha in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries; Vādirāja of Sode, who also composed in Kannada with the signature “Hayavadana”.

🌳 The great generation

Purandara Dāsa and Kanaka Dāsa under Vijayanagara, with many others whose songs survive only in part.

🍃 The revival

In the eighteenth century Vijaya Dāsa, Gopāla Dāsa and Jagannātha Dāsa (author of the Harikathāmṛtasāra) renewed the movement.

🎶 Today

Dāsa songs are sung in homes, temples and concerts across Karnataka; children learn “Lambodara lakumikara” as their first gīta.

ZAPPY: Is the movement still alive?

PROF. BOLT: In every bhajana group and music class in Karnataka, Zappy.

A line still singing.

WHAT THE DĀSAS TEACH

Simple words, deep faith

God speaks your language. The dāsas proved that the deepest teaching can be sung in a mother tongue.

Give, don’t hoard. Purandara’s life is the story of a miser who learned to give everything.

Worth is not birth. Kanaka’s window and Purandara’s songs both say that devotion, not caste, opens the door. (Interpretation.)

Music as worship. Their graded lessons and songs made music a path to God for every child.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for the Haridāsas?

PROF. BOLT: Learn one dāsa song in Kannada, Zappy, and sing it to someone at home.

Sing it in your own language.
