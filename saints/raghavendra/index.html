Rāghavendra Tīrtha

The Master of Mantrālaya · explained and illustrated

Rāyaru by the Tuṅgabhadrā

HOW THIS WORKS: The life of Rāghavendra Tīrtha: the poor scholar-musician Veṅkaṭanātha, the reluctant pontiff, the philosopher of Dvaita, the stories devotees tell, the brindāvana at Mantrālaya, the verse every devotee knows, and his ties to Mysuru.

WHERE WE ARE

In homes across Karnataka a small picture hangs near the shrine: a saffron-robed monk with a staff, beside a stone shrine topped with tulasī. He is Rāghavendra Tīrtha, called Rāyaru (“the master”) or Guru Rāya, a seventeenth-century philosopher of the Mādhva tradition who, devotees believe, still answers prayers from his brindāvana at Mantrālaya.

This guide tells his life as history and tradition record it, explains why he matters to Dvaita philosophy, recounts the stories devotees love, and shows how he is honoured. His shrine town is described in our Mantrālaya guide.

VEṄKAṬANĀTHA

A poor scholar with a vīṇā

The story

Wikipedia, “Raghavendra Tirtha”; Śrī Rāghavendra Svāmī Maṭha, Mantrālaya, life history.

Veṅkaṭanātha was born about 1595 at Bhuvanagiri, in a Kannada Mādhva family of scholars; his great-grandfather had taught the Vijayanagara emperor Kṛṣṇadevarāya.
His father Timmaṇṇa Bhaṭṭa, a scholar and vīṇā player, died when the boy was young. His brother-in-law arranged his studies at Madurai.
He became a superb grammarian, logician and musician, known as “Vīṇe Veṅkaṇṇa”. He married Sarasvatī and had a son, but lived in deep poverty, teaching and debating.
He joined the school of Sudhīndra Tīrtha, head of the maṭha at Kumbhakōṇam, and outshone the other students in debate.
Sudhīndra wished him to be his successor. Veṅkaṭanātha, a householder with a young family, refused, until, the tradition says, a dream of the goddess of learning persuaded him.

ZAPPY: He was poor even though he was brilliant?

PROF. BOLT: Often the way, Zappy. He chose learning over comfort long before he chose the robe.

Learning before comfort.

THE RELUCTANT PONTIFF

From Veṅkaṭanātha to Rāghavendra

The decision. He took sannyāsa at Thañjāvūr and received the name Rāghavendra Tīrtha, becoming head of the Kumbhakōṇam maṭha (sources give 1621 or 1624).

The cost. Tradition tells that his wife Sarasvatī, arriving too late to see him before the rite, was overcome by grief; devotees believe he blessed her soul. The story is told to show what renunciation asks of a family. (Tradition.)

The maṭha. The institution, once called the Vijayīndra or Dakṣiṇādi maṭha, came to be known by his name: the Śrī Rāghavendra Svāmī Maṭha, one of the three chief maṭhas of the Mādhva tradition with the Uttarādi and Vyāsarāja maṭhas.

A travelling teacher. He toured South India for decades, teaching, debating and writing, with stays at Kumbhakōṇam, Śrīraṅgam, Rāmeśvaram, Udupi, Paṇḍharpur and elsewhere.

ZAPPY: Did he regret it?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says he served his family’s welfare in another way, Zappy. The question is a real one; the story doesn’t hide it.

Renunciation has a cost.

THE PHILOSOPHER

Parimaḷa and forty-five works

A grantha is a unit of 32 syllables used to measure Sanskrit works.

📚 Sudhā Parimaḷa

His masterpiece, the Nyāya Sudhā Parimaḷa, a commentary on Jayatīrtha’s Nyāya Sudhā (itself on Madhva’s Anuvyākhyāna), about 19,300 granthas long. It earned him the title Parimaḷācārya.

🔍 Making Madhva clear

About 45 Sanskrit works: glosses on Madhva, Jayatīrtha and Vyāsatīrtha, commentaries on the Upaniṣads, the Brahma Sūtras (Tantradīpikā) and the Ṛgveda (Mantrārthamañjarī), and a work on Mīmāṃsā (Bhāṭṭa Saṅgraha).

⚖️ Dvaita in brief

Madhva’s school teaches that God (Viṣṇu), souls and matter are eternally distinct; souls depend on God; devotion with right knowledge earns his grace. See our Gītā Jayantī page on the commentators.

🎶 Kannada song

He composed songs under the name Veṇugopāla; the Kannada song “Indu Enage Govinda” is attributed to him.

ZAPPY: People pray to a man who wrote commentaries on commentaries?

PROF. BOLT: He made a difficult philosophy clear and kind, Zappy. Scholars still read him; others simply love him.

He made Dvaita clear.

THE SILVER PLATE

How Mantrālaya was chosen

The story

Devotional tradition, as told by the Mantrālaya maṭha and popular accounts.

The governor of Ādoni, Siddi Masūd Khān, serving the Bijapur sultans, heard of the saint and, tradition says, decided to test him.
He brought a covered silver plate of meat as an offering. Rāghavendra sprinkled water on it, and when the cover was lifted the plate held fruit.
Humbled, the Nawab offered him rich lands. The saint asked only for Manchāle, a dry, rocky village on the Tuṅgabhadrā.
Devotees explain why: in his earlier life as Prahlāda, they believe, he had performed a sacrifice on that very spot, and the village goddess Manchālamma had been his deity then.
Manchāle became Mantrālaya, “the abode of mantras”.

ZAPPY: Did the plate really change?

PROF. BOLT: That is what devotees tell, Zappy. Historians record that the maṭha did receive the land at Manchāle.

He chose the rocky land.

RĀYARU AND MYSURU

A grant from Dodda Devarāja

The grant. In about 1663 Rāghavendra visited Mysuru and received a grant from Dodda Devarāja Woḍeyar, the same king credited with the Chāmuṇḍi Hill steps and its great Nandi (see Garuḍa and Nandi).

Rāyara maṭhas. Rāghavendra maṭhas and mṛttikā brindāvanas (shrines made with earth brought from Mantrālaya) stand all over Karnataka: in Mysuru, Bengaluru, Nañjanagūḍu and in almost every town.

Thursday. Guruvāra, Thursday, is his day: devotees visit his maṭha, chant his verse and offer coconuts and flowers.

Kannada devotion. Haridāsa songs, Kannada films and countless household traditions keep him close; for many Kannadigas he is simply “Rāyaru”.

ZAPPY: Is there a Rāyara maṭha in Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: Several, Zappy, and a Thursday queue at each.

Mysuru’s Thursday guru.

THE BRINDĀVANA

1671: entering while alive

The day. In 1671, on Śrāvaṇa Kṛṣṇa Dvitīyā by tradition, Rāghavendra addressed his disciples, sat in the stone brindāvana he had had prepared, and began japa. As he chanted, the slabs were set in place over him.

What it means. Entering a brindāvana alive is a samādhi: the body rests, but devotees believe the saint’s presence remains, as he promised, for seven hundred years.

Appaṇṇācārya. His disciple Appaṇṇācārya, the story goes, was across the river and rushed back composing a hymn as he ran; he reached the end of a line just as the last slab closed, and could not finish. The saint’s voice is said to have completed it from within.

The Ārādhanā. The anniversary is kept as a three-day festival, Pūrva, Madhya and Uttara Ārādhanā, at Mantrālaya and in every Rāyara maṭha.

ZAPPY: Seven hundred years?

PROF. BOLT: That is the devotees’ belief, Zappy. They come in hundreds of thousands every Ārādhanā.

Present, devotees say, still.

IN THE VERSE

Pūjyāya Rāghavendrāya

The verse every devotee knows
pūjyāya rāghavendrāya satyadharmaratāya cabhajatāṃ kalpavṛkṣāya namatāṃ kāmadhenave
To the worshipful Rāghavendra, devoted to truth and dharma: a wish-fulfilling tree to those who worship him, a wish-granting cow to those who bow to him.
WordMeaning
pūjyāyato the worshipful
satya-dharma-ratāyadevoted to truth and right conduct
kalpa-vṛkṣathe wish-fulfilling tree
kāma-dhenuthe wish-granting cow
namatāmof those who bow

Two pictures. The kalpavṛkṣa and kāmadhenu are heaven’s two great givers. Note what comes first: satya and dharma. Devotees chant the verse 108 times, often on Thursdays, with the mantra “Oṃ Śrī Rāghavendrāya namaḥ”.

From Appaṇṇācārya’s Śrī Rāghavendra Stotra; text as given by madhwasakha.com and mantra.tips.
Truth first, then every gift.

THE BELIEF IN EARLIER LIVES

Prahlāda, Bāhlīka, Vyāsatīrtha

🦁 Prahlāda

The child devotee whom Narasiṃha saved from his father Hiraṇyakaśipu. Devotees believe Rāghavendra was Prahlāda, which is why Narasiṃha is so honoured at Mantrālaya. See Narasiṃha.

🏹 Bāhlīka

A Kuru elder in the Mahābhārata who fought on the Kaurava side but loved Kṛṣṇa.

📿 Vyāsatīrtha

The great sixteenth-century Mādhva teacher (Vyāsarāja), guru to Purandara Dāsa and Kanaka Dāsa and royal teacher at Vijayanagara.

🌟 Śaṅkukarṇa

All three are held to be births of a celestial named Śaṅkukarṇa. The Mantrālaya maṭha presents this as the belief of realised teachers; it is a matter of faith, not history.

ZAPPY: So he was Prahlāda?

PROF. BOLT: His devotees believe so, Zappy, and it shapes how they see him. We record it as their belief.

A line of devotees, by faith.

WHAT HE TAUGHT

Devotion, duty and plain kindness

Devotion with knowledge. In the Mādhva view the soul is saved by Viṣṇu’s grace, which comes through bhakti joined to right understanding. Rāghavendra wrote for scholars and spoke simply to everyone.

Duty as worship. Tradition attributes to him the teaching that every honest task, done for God, is worship. (Tradition.)

All were welcome. His devotees came, then as now, from every caste and creed, including the Nawab of Ādoni; scholars note this breadth.

His last talk. Before entering the brindāvana he urged his listeners, tradition says, to live rightly, serve others and keep faith in Hari. (Tradition.)

ZAPPY: What would he say to me?

PROF. BOLT: Probably: study hard, be kind, and do your work as an offering, Zappy. Then come on Thursday.

Work as worship.

WHY HE MATTERS

A guru for ordinary lives

A bridge. He joined the high scholarship of Dvaita to the daily faith of ordinary households. Few scholars are loved so widely. (Interpretation.)

Near and kind. Devotees speak of him as a living guru who hears small troubles: illness, exams, jobs, family. The verse calls him a wish-giving tree; the first words call him devoted to truth.

A sign of renunciation. His story does not hide the cost of leaving the world, nor the poverty he chose for learning.

On this site. His tradition’s great teacher Madhva lived at Udupi; his maṭha’s shrine is at Mantrālaya.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Rāyaru?

PROF. BOLT: Chant “Pūjyāya Rāghavendrāya” on a Thursday, Zappy, and keep one promise to tell the truth that day.

Truth first, like the verse.
