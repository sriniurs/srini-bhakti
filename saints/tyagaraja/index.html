TIRUVĀRŪR · TIRUVAIYĀRU · RĀMA’S SINGER

Tyāgarāja

Rāma’s Singer of the Kāverī · explained and illustrated

Singing for Rāma by the Kāverī

HOW THIS WORKS: The life of Tyāgarāja: the boy from Tiruvārūr, his devotion to Rāma, the king he refused, the Pañcaratna songs, the music-dramas, the stories devotees tell, his last day, and the Ārādhanā that a singer from Nañjanagūḍu rebuilt.

WHERE WE ARE

Every January, on the bank of the Kāverī at Tiruvaiyāru, hundreds of musicians sit together and sing the same five songs in unison. They are honouring Tyāgarāja (1767–1847), the singer-saint whose songs to Rāma form the heart of Carnatic music, and for whom music and prayer were one act.

This guide tells his life as history and tradition record it: his family and teacher, his choice of devotion over court, his great compositions and music-dramas, the stories his devotees love, his last day, and the festival at his samādhi, rebuilt by a remarkable woman from Nañjanagūḍu. Rāma himself has his own guide in Rāma.

THE BOY FROM TIRUVĀRŪR

Family, teacher and the first song

The story

Wikipedia, “Tyagaraja”; traditional biographies.

Tyāgarāja was born on 4 May 1767 at Tiruvārūr, third son of Kākarla Rāmabrahmam and Sītamma, a Telugu-speaking family whose ancestors had moved south after the fall of Vijayanagara. He was named after Tyāgarāja, the Śiva of Tiruvārūr’s great temple.
The family moved to Tiruvaiyāru on the Kāverī. His father read the Rāmāyaṇa aloud; Rāma became the centre of the boy’s life.
He studied Sanskrit and the Vedas, and music under Soṇṭi Veṅkaṭaramaṇayya, chief musician at the Thañjāvūr court.
His first song, tradition says, was “Namō Namō Rāghavāya” in the rāga Deśika Tōḍi.
He married Pārvatī, and after her early death her sister Kamala; he had one daughter, Sītālakṣmī.

ZAPPY: Named after Śiva, devoted to Rāma?

PROF. BOLT: He sang to Śiva, Devī and Gaṇeśa too, Zappy. But Rāma was his home.

Rāma at the centre.

THE KING’S INVITATION

Is wealth happiness?

The story

Wikipedia, “Tyagaraja”, and traditional biographies.

Serfoji II, the Marāṭhā king of Thañjāvūr, a great patron, invited Tyāgarāja to his court and sent rich gifts.
Court musicians lived well; his family was poor. His brother, tradition says, urged him to accept.
Tyāgarāja refused. He felt that serving a king would bind his music and his devotion.
His answer, tradition holds, was a song in the rāga Kalyāṇi: “Nidhi cāla sukhamā”, asking whether wealth brings happiness, or the presence of Rāma.
He went on living by uñcavṛtti, accepting rice from householders while singing in the streets, as a devotee who asks for nothing more.

ZAPPY: He chose to stay poor?

PROF. BOLT: He chose to stay free, Zappy. To him the song belonged to Rāma, not to a court.

Free to sing for Rāma.

THE FIVE GEMS

The Pañcaratna kṛtis

💎 Five songs, five rāgas

All in Ādi tāḷa: “Jagadānandakāraka” (Nāṭa, Sanskrit), “Duḍuku gala” (Gauḷa), “Sādhiñcenē” (Ārabhi), “Kana kana rucirā” (Varāḷi) and “Endarō Mahānubhāvulu” (Śrī), the last four in Telugu.

🎼 The “ghana” rāgas

These five are the weighty rāgas of the old vīṇā tradition. The Pañcaratnas are long, built like a concert piece with sung svara passages in each stanza.

🎤 Sung together

At the Ārādhanā they are sung in unison by every musician present, the most famous moment in Carnatic music’s year.

📜 About 720 more

Some 720 kṛtis survive, mostly in Telugu with many in Sanskrit, on Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Śiva, the Devī, Gaṇeśa, Subrahmaṇya and Hanumān. Claims of 24,000 are not supported by evidence, scholars say.

ZAPPY: Everyone sings the same song at once?

PROF. BOLT: Hundreds of voices, Zappy, from famous names to students. It sounds like a river.

Five gems, one voice.

IN THE VERSE

Endarō Mahānubhāvulu

The pallavi of the fifth gem
endarō mahānubhāvuluandariki vandanamulu
How many great souls there are: my salutations to all of them.
WordMeaning
endarōhow many
mahā-anubhāvulugreat souls, those of great experience
andarikito all of them
vandanamulusalutations

Gratitude before everything. Telugu, not Sanskrit: the language of his home. The song then describes those great souls, people who see Rāma in their hearts and sing with feeling, and ends by naming himself as their servant. Tradition says it was sung after hearing a great musician from Kerala; another account says a disciple requested it. (Sources differ.)

Pallavi as given in Wikipedia, “Endaro Mahanubhavulu” (Telugu, Śrī rāga, Ādi tāḷa); the song is in the public domain.
Bow to all the great, known and unknown.

THE MUSIC-DRAMAS

Prahlāda and the boat

🦁 Prahlāda Bhakti Vijayam

“The triumph of Prahlāda’s devotion”: a Telugu music-drama in five acts with 45 kṛtis in 28 rāgas, on the child-devotee saved by Narasiṃha.

⛵ Naukā Caritram

“The boat story”: a one-act drama with 21 kṛtis in 13 rāgas. Kṛṣṇa takes the gopīs boating on the Yamunā; when they grow proud, a storm humbles them. It is Tyāgarāja’s own story, not from the Bhāgavata.

🎭 Why drama

Both plays teach through music what his kṛtis teach in a few lines: devotion without pride.

📜 How they survive

His disciples wrote his songs on palm leaves and copper plates; many manuscripts are kept at the Sarasvatī Mahal Library in Thañjāvūr.

ZAPPY: Kṛṣṇa capsizes the gopīs?

PROF. BOLT: Nearly, Zappy, until they drop their pride. Then the sea calms.

Drama for humility.

THE IMAGE IN THE RIVER

A story devotees tell

The story

A devotional tradition; not recorded in the main encyclopaedic sources.

Tyāgarāja kept small images of Rāma, Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa and Hanumān, worshipped daily with song.
His elder brother, tradition says, angry at his refusal of royal wealth and his poverty, threw the images into the Kāverī.
Tyāgarāja searched and grieved, and went on a pilgrimage singing songs of longing for Rāma.
After a long time, the story goes, he found the images in the river and brought them home, singing in joy; the kṛti “Rārā mā iṇṭidāka” is linked to this homecoming by some.
Whatever its history, the story shows how devotees understood him: a man whose whole life was his Rāma.

ZAPPY: Is this story true?

PROF. BOLT: It isn’t in the records, Zappy, and we mark it so. It is true to how people loved him.

Lost, longed for, found.

HIS LAST DAY

Sannyāsa and the samādhi

Sannyāsa. In his last days he took sannyāsa, formal renunciation, on 5 January 1847.

The end. The next day, 6 January, Puṣya Bahuḷa Pañcamī, he died at Tiruvaiyāru aged seventy-nine, surrounded by disciples singing. Tradition says he foresaw the day.

The samādhi. He was buried on the bank of the Kāverī the following day; the spot became his samādhi, his memorial, where the Ārādhanā is held.

His disciples. They included Veṅkaṭaramaṇa Bhāgavatar, Kṛṣṇasvāmī Bhāgavatar, Vīṇā Kuppayyar and Subbarāya Śāstri, son of Śyāmā Śāstri; through them, almost every Carnatic musician today traces a line back to him.

ZAPPY: He died singing?

PROF. BOLT: Listening to his own songs sung by his students, Zappy. Many musicians would call that the best ending.

He left in song.

THE ĀRĀDHANĀ

A festival rebuilt by a woman from Nañjanagūḍu

The festival. The Tyāgarāja Ārādhanā at Tiruvaiyāru runs for about a week in January; on Puṣya Bahuḷa Pañcamī, the anniversary, the Pañcaratnas are sung in unison.

Bangalore Nāgaratnamma. Born in 1878 at Nañjanagūḍu near Mysuru, she was a leading singer, Sanskrit scholar and devadāsī. Finding the samādhi neglected, she spent her savings to buy the land and build a shrine over it; it was consecrated in January 1925.

Women in. When women were kept off the Ārādhanā stage, she organised a parallel festival in 1927 at which forty women performed; her will left her wealth to the memorial on condition that women be allowed to pay homage freely. She lived out her life at Tiruvaiyāru and died there in 1952.

Everywhere. Ārādhanās are held in Mysuru, Bengaluru, Chennai, Tirupati, Kākarla and abroad; his songs are learned by every Carnatic student.

ZAPPY: A singer from near Mysuru rebuilt his shrine?

PROF. BOLT: From Nañjanagūḍu, Zappy. She also built a Mysore choultry there for Kannada pilgrims.

Rebuilt by a devotee’s savings.

THE TRINITY

Tyāgarāja, Dīkṣitar and Śyāmā Śāstri

🎵 Three in one region

All three were born in the Thañjāvūr region within a few years (1762–1775) and shaped Carnatic music as it is heard today.

📿 Different gods, different voices

Tyāgarāja sang mostly in Telugu, mostly to Rāma; Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar in Sanskrit, much of it Śrīvidyā; Śyāmā Śāstri to the Devī Kāmākṣī.

🎹 Different styles

Tyāgarāja’s songs are direct and personal, like a conversation with Rāma; Dīkṣitar’s are stately and dense; Śyāmā Śāstri’s are intricate in rhythm.

🪷 One spirit

All three treated music as worship, nādopāsana, “worship through sound”.

ZAPPY: Which one is the best?

PROF. BOLT: A question musicians love to argue, Zappy. The answer is: learn all three.

Three voices, one worship.

WHAT TYĀGARĀJA TEACHES

Music as worship, humility as art

Nādopāsana. Sound itself can be worship. For Tyāgarāja, singing well and loving Rāma were the same practice. (Interpretation.)

Freedom over comfort. He refused a king to keep his music honest. Many artists remember him when tempted to sell their art short.

Humility. His most famous song bows to every great soul before saying anything else.

Everyone’s inheritance. His songs belong to every student who learns them; in Mysuru and across the South, a child’s first kṛtis are often his.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Tyāgarāja?

PROF. BOLT: Learn the pallavi of “Endarō Mahānubhāvulu”, Zappy, and sing it for someone who taught you.

Sing as an offering.

WORDS TO KNOW

The vocabulary of this article

🎼 Kṛti

A composed song: pallavi, anupallavi, caraṇam.

💎 Pañcaratna

The “five gems”, his five great kṛtis.

🎵 Rāga

A melodic framework with its own mood.

🥁 Tāḷa

A rhythmic cycle; the Pañcaratnas use Ādi.

Living on rice given while singing in the streets.

🙏 Ārādhanā

The anniversary festival at his samādhi.

🔔 Nādopāsana

Worship through sound.

🪦 Samādhi

A saint’s memorial over his resting place.
