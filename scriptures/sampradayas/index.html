Sampradāyas

The Great Traditions of Hindu Worship · explained and illustrated

One tree, many branches

HOW THIS WORKS: A guide to the sampradāyas, the great traditions of Hindu worship: what a sampradāya is, the four main families (Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, Śākta, Smārta) and their schools, their teachers, texts and marks, the smaller Gāṇapatya and Saura paths, and how they overlap in an ordinary home.

WHERE WE ARE

Ask two Hindu families how they worship and you may get two different answers: one bows first to Viṣṇu, one to Śiva, one to the Goddess, one to five gods at once. These are sampradāyas, traditions passed down from teacher to student, each with its own view of the supreme, its own texts and its own marks on the forehead.

This guide maps them simply and fairly: the four great families, their main schools and teachers, and the smaller paths, with links to the guides where each is explored. It describes each tradition in its own terms; it does not rank them.

WHAT IS A SAMPRADĀYA?

A tradition handed down

The word. Sampradāya means “that which is handed over”: a tradition passed from teacher to student in an unbroken line, a paramparā.

What it gives. A view of God and the world, a set of authoritative texts and commentaries, rites and initiations, festivals, and often a mark on the forehead and a mantra.

Not walls. Hindus speak of their iṣṭa devatā, the chosen deity, and of their family tradition, but most also honour other gods and visit other temples. Scholars call this “polycentrism”.

Families and schools. The big families (Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, Śākta, Smārta) contain many schools, which can differ more from each other than from schools in another family.

ZAPPY: So I could visit a Śiva temple if my family is Vaiṣṇava?

PROF. BOLT: Millions do, Zappy, every day. The branches touch.

VAIṢṆAVA TRADITIONS

Viṣṇu and his avatāras

Traditionally four Vaiṣṇava sampradāyas are counted: Śrī (Rāmānuja), Brahma (Madhva), Rudra (Vallabha) and Kumāra (Nimbārka).

🪷 Śrīvaiṣṇava

Rāmānuja and Vedānta Deśika; Viśiṣṭādvaita; Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa; the Āḻvārs’ Tamil songs. Two branches, Vaḍakalai and Teṅkalai. See Śrīraṅgam.

🐚 Mādhva

Madhvācārya of Udupi; Dvaita; Viṣṇu supreme and distinct from souls; the Haridāsas. See Rāghavendra Tīrtha.

🎶 Vallabha, Nimbārka, Gauḍīya

Vallabha’s Puṣṭimārga (Kṛṣṇa as Śrīnāthjī); Nimbārka’s Rādhā-Kṛṣṇa; Caitanya’s Gauḍīya school, from which ISKCON comes. See Jagannātha.

🙏 Marks and texts

Vertical marks (ūrdhva-puṇḍra); the Pañcarātra and Vaikhānasa Āgamas; the Bhāgavata and Viṣṇu Purāṇas; the Gītā. Regional streams include the Vārkarīs (Viṭṭhala).

ZAPPY: So many Vaiṣṇava schools!

PROF. BOLT: All agree Viṣṇu is supreme, Zappy. They differ on how the soul and God relate.

Many schools, one Lord.

ŚAIVA TRADITIONS

Śiva as the supreme

Marks: tripuṇḍra of sacred ash; rudrākṣa beads. Śaiva temples and households across Karnataka also honour the Devī and Gaṇeśa daily.

🔱 Śaiva Siddhānta

Strong in Tamil Nadu; the 28 Śaiva Āgamas and the Tamil Tirumurai of the Nāyanmārs; temple ritual. Teachers include Meykaṇḍār.

🌀 Kashmir Śaivism

Non-dual: all is Śiva’s consciousness; Vasugupta and Abhinavagupta.

🔘 Vīraśaiva-Liṅgāyata

Basavaṇṇa and the śaraṇas; the iṣṭaliṅga worn on the body; rejection of caste. See Basavaṇṇa. Its relation to wider Hinduism is debated.

🧘 Pāśupata and Nātha

The ancient Pāśupata ascetics (Lakulīśa); the Nātha yogīs of Gorakhnāth and Matsyendranāth, linked to haṭha yoga.

ZAPPY: Ash marks for Śaivas?

PROF. BOLT: Three horizontal lines of vibhūti, the tripuṇḍra, Zappy. Easy to spot.

Many paths to Śiva.

ŚĀKTA TRADITIONS

The Goddess as supreme

🔺 Śrīkula

The family of Śrī: the Goddess as Lalitā Tripurasundarī, worshipped in the Śrīcakra; Śrīvidyā. See our Śrīvidyā series.

🌑 Kālīkula

The family of Kālī: the fierce forms, strong in Bengal and Assam; the Mahāvidyās.

📖 Texts

The Devī Māhātmya, the Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa, the Lalitā Sahasranāma and the Tantras.

🌺 Everywhere

Śākta worship blends with Śaiva and village traditions; Navarātri is its great festival across India.

ZAPPY: Śiva and Śakti are separable?

PROF. BOLT: Hardly, Zappy. Many Śaivas revere Śakti, and Śāktas revere Śiva as her partner.

The Mother as all.

THE SMĀRTA TRADITION

Five gods, one Brahman

Who. Smārtas follow the Smṛti texts and Vedic ritual, and in Vedānta mostly follow Ādi Śaṅkara’s Advaita: all deities are forms of the one Brahman.

Pañcāyatana pūjā. Five deities are worshipped together, Gaṇeśa, Śiva, the Devī, Viṣṇu and Sūrya, often as natural stones; the family’s chosen one sits in the centre. Many add Skanda as a sixth.

The maṭhas. Śaṅkara is credited with founding monastic seats: Śṛṅgeri in the south, Purī, Dvārakā and Jyotirmaṭha; the Kāñcī maṭha also traces itself to him. See Śṛṅgeri Śāradā.

In Karnataka. Many Kannada brāhmaṇa families are Smārta, following Śṛṅgeri; many are Mādhva or Śrīvaiṣṇava. The differences show in marks, prayers and festival customs.

ZAPPY: Five gods in one box?

PROF. BOLT: Five doors to one room, Zappy, is how a Smārta would put it.

Five doors, one room.

SMALLER AND OLDER PATHS

Gāṇapatya, Saura, Kaumāra and more

🐘 Gāṇapatya

Gaṇeśa as the supreme Brahman; strong in Maharashtra, with the Gaṇeśa and Mudgala Purāṇas. See Gāṇapatya.

☀️ Saura

The Sun as the visible Brahman; living today in Chhaṭh in Bihar and in sun temples. See Ratha Saptamī.

🦚 Kaumāra

Skanda-Murugan as supreme, strong in Tamil Nadu; see Skanda.

🪨 Village and clan gods

Grāmadevatās, daivas and kuladevatās, like Tuḷunāḍu’s daivas, form a vast layer of worship that runs alongside all the sampradāyas. See Daiva.

ZAPPY: Is the village goddess in a sampradāya?

PROF. BOLT: Usually beside all of them, Zappy. Most families keep their village goddess and their sampradāya both.

Older paths still walking.

READING THE MARKS

What the forehead tells you

Marks vary by school and region; see Why Do We Wear Tilaka?.

⬆️ Vertical (ūrdhva-puṇḍra)

Vaiṣṇava: two white lines with a red or yellow line between (Śrīvaiṣṇava “nāmam”), or the Mādhva single line with a black charcoal stripe and a dot.

➖ Horizontal (tripuṇḍra)

Śaiva and many Smārtas: three lines of ash, often with a red dot.

🔴 Kuṅkuma

Śākta and many women of every tradition: a red dot or vertical line of kuṅkuma.

🌿 Beads

Tulasī beads for Vaiṣṇavas, rudrākṣa for Śaivas, often worn with the marks.

ZAPPY: Can you always tell from the mark?

PROF. BOLT: Often, Zappy, not always. Many people wear their family’s mark and pray everywhere.

The forehead tells a story.

WHERE THEY MEET

What all share

Shared texts. All revere the Vedas and Upaniṣads; most honour the Gītā, the Rāmāyaṇa and the Mahābhārata. Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Śākta schools add their Āgamas.

Shared ideas. Karma, rebirth, liberation, dharma, the value of devotion and of a teacher.

Shared calendar. Dīpāvali, Navarātri, Saṅkrānti and Ekādaśī are kept across traditions, often with different emphases.

Shared homes. A single household may have a Smārta father, a Śrīvaiṣṇava mother’s customs, a Śākta village goddess and a picture of Rāghavendra. That is ordinary Hindu life. Debate between schools has been sharp at times, but daily life mixes them.

ZAPPY: So they argue but live together?

PROF. BOLT: Like a big family at a festival, Zappy. Arguing is part of the closeness.

Different branches, shared roots.

WHY IT MATTERS

Knowing your tradition, respecting others

Knowing your own. Knowing your family’s sampradāya explains its prayers, marks, festivals and teachers; it gives practice a context.

Respecting others. Knowing the others prevents easy mistakes: not every Hindu sees Śiva, Viṣṇu or the Devī the same way.

Freedom to explore. Many seekers learn from more than one tradition; the Smārta and many modern teachers encourage it, while some schools ask for exclusive devotion. Traditions differ.

Read on. Our guides to Viṣṇu, Śiva and the Devī show each tradition’s supreme deity.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing?

PROF. BOLT: Find out your family’s tradition, Zappy, then visit a temple of another with respect.

Know yours; respect theirs.
