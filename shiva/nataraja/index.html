ŚIVA · THE LORD OF DANCE

Naṭarāja

The Lord of Dance · explained and illustrated

The dance of the universe

HOW THIS WORKS: Naṭarāja, Śiva as the Lord of Dance: reading the famous bronze, the ring of fire, the drum and flame, the raised foot and the dwarf of ignorance, the five acts of God, the dance at Chidambaram, the contest with Kālī, the Chola bronzes, and Ārudrā Darśanam, the night of his cosmic dance in Mārgaḻi.

WHERE WE ARE

There may be no more famous image of a Hindu god than this: Śiva dancing in a ring of fire, one foot on a small demon, the other lifted in the air, hair flying, face perfectly calm. This is Naṭarāja, “King of Dancers”.

Every part of the bronze has meaning. This guide reads it piece by piece, tells the stories of his dance, and explains Ārudrā Darśanam, the winter night when devotees see him dance. For Śiva himself, see Who Is Śiva?.

READING THE BRONZE (1)

Hands and fire

🥁 Upper right: the drum

The ḍamaru beats the rhythm of creation; tradition says the sounds of language came from it.

🔥 Upper left: fire

Agni, the fire that dissolves the universe at the end of time.

✋ Lower right: “fear not”

Abhaya mudrā, protection, often with a snake coiled on the arm.

🐘 Lower left: elephant-trunk hand

Gaja-hasta, the arm thrown across the body, pointing to the raised foot: refuge.

ZAPPY: Creation and destruction in two hands!

PROF. BOLT: And protection in the third, Zappy. All at once.

Make, keep, end.

READING THE BRONZE (2)

Feet, hair and ring

👹 Apasmāra

The dwarf under the right foot is ignorance and forgetfulness: held down, not killed, because ignorance is never finally destroyed in the world.

🦶 Raised foot

The lifted left foot offers release (mokṣa) to the devotee.

🌊 Flying locks

Hair whirls out with the speed of the dance; the river goddess Gaṅgā sits in it.

⭕ Ring of fire

The prabhāmaṇḍala is the cosmos, or the cycle of time, in which the dance takes place.

ZAPPY: Why not kill the dwarf?

PROF. BOLT: Then there’d be nothing to overcome, Zappy. Each of us still must.

Ignorance underfoot.

THE FIVE ACTS

Pañcakṛtya

Five actions of God. Śaiva Siddhānta teaches that Śiva performs five acts: sṛṣṭi (creation), sthiti (preservation), saṃhāra (dissolution), tirobhāva (concealment) and anugraha (grace).

In the dance. The drum is creation, the open hand preservation, the fire dissolution, the foot on the dwarf concealment, and the raised foot grace.

A Tamil theology. Texts such as the Uṇmai Viḷakkam spell this out; the idea is central in the Tamil Śaiva tradition.

All at once. The dance is not a story with an end; all five acts happen in every moment.

ZAPPY: Concealment is an act of God?

PROF. BOLT: Hiding himself so souls can grow, Zappy, until grace reveals him.

Five acts, one dance.

THE DANCE IN THE FOREST

Why he dances at Chidambaram

The story

Tamil temple tradition (Koyil Purāṇam), told with variations.

In the Dāruka forest, proud sages believed rituals alone gave them power. Śiva came among them as a wandering beggar to humble them.
Angry, they sent a tiger; he flayed it and wore its skin. They sent a serpent; he wore it as a garland. They sent the dwarf Apasmāra; he stepped on its back and began to dance.
The sages fell at his feet. Ādiśeṣa, Viṣṇu’s serpent, longed to see the dance again and was born as the sage Patañjali, half-serpent.
With the tiger-footed sage Vyāghrapāda, he worshipped at Tillai, the forest of Chidambaram. There Śiva performed the ānanda tāṇḍava for them.
Chidambaram’s golden hall (Kanaka Sabhā) marks the place. See Chidambaram.

ZAPPY: A tiger-footed sage!

PROF. BOLT: So he could climb trees for flowers before the bees reached them, Zappy.

Pride danced away.

THE CONTEST WITH KĀLĪ

The raised leg

The story

Tiruvālaṅgāḍu temple tradition.

At Tiruvālaṅgāḍu (the forest of banyans), the fierce goddess Kālī was disturbing the world with her wild dance.
Śiva challenged her to a dance contest. Move matched move; neither could win.
Then, an earring fell; Śiva caught it with his toe and raised his leg straight up to put it back: the ūrdhva tāṇḍava.
Kālī, out of modesty, would not lift her leg so. She yielded, and her fury was calmed.
The temple there keeps the Ratna Sabhā, the gem hall of the dance.

ZAPPY: Won by a dance move!

PROF. BOLT: The highest kick in mythology, Zappy.

The highest kick.

THE FIVE HALLS

Where he dances

🟡 Kanaka (gold)

Chidambaram: the golden-roofed hall of the ānanda tāṇḍava.

⚪ Velli (silver)

Madurai: here Śiva dances with the right leg raised, a change made at the request of a Pāṇḍya king, says tradition. See Madurai.

🔴 Ratna (gem)

Tiruvālaṅgāḍu: the contest with Kālī.

🟤 Tāmra and Citra

Copper hall at Tirunelveli, picture hall at Kuṟṟālam.

PROF. BOLT: Tamil Nadu’s pañca sabhai, Zappy.

THE CHOLA BRONZES

Art in metal

Lost wax. Each bronze is cast by the lost-wax method: a wax model, coated in clay, melted out and replaced by metal. Each mould is used only once.

The great age. Some of the oldest dancing-Śiva images are carved in stone in caves at Bādāmi, Ellora and Elephanta (around the 6th century). The classic bronzes come from the Chola period in Tamil Nadu, about the 10th to 12th centuries.

Made to move. Temple bronzes have rings on the base for poles, so the deity can be carried in procession.

Still made. Svāmimalai near Kumbakonam continues the craft. A 2-metre Naṭarāja stands at CERN in Geneva, given by India in 2004.

ZAPPY: A dancing Śiva at a physics lab!

PROF. BOLT: A gift about the dance of energy and matter, Zappy.

Cast once, kept forever.

ĀRUDRĀ DARŚANAM

The night of the dance

When. On the Ārdrā nakṣatra (Tamil: Tiruvādirai) in the month of Mārgaḻi (Dec–Jan), near the full moon; Ārdrā is Śiva’s star. See Nakṣatras.

Darśana. At Chidambaram the ten-day festival climaxes at dawn as Naṭarāja and Śivakāmasundarī come out of the golden hall to dance before the crowds.

Abhiṣeka. A great night-long bath of the bronze precedes it, in many Śiva temples.

Kaḷi. Homes make tiruvādirai kaḷi, a sweet of rice and jaggery, with a mixed-vegetable kootu, offered and shared.

ZAPPY: Sweet rice for Śiva’s dance night!

PROF. BOLT: Kaḷi and kootu, Zappy: the Tamil Ārudrā meal.

See him dance at dawn.

WHAT THE DANCE TEACHES

Joy at the heart of change

Everything moves. Stars, seasons, cells, lives: the universe is never still. The dance makes that movement sacred.

The still face. Amid wild motion, Naṭarāja’s face is calm: bliss at the centre of change. (Interpretation.)

Fear not. One hand says it plainly. Change and loss are part of the dance, not outside God’s care.

Grace. The raised foot is offered to anyone who comes; the dance is for our release. Ananda Coomaraswamy’s 1918 essay “The Dance of Śiva” made this reading known worldwide.
