HOW THIS WORKS: Who Śiva is, from Rudra of the Veda to the Lord of Kailāsa: his name, his look, his forms, four great stories, the liṅga, worship, his temples and what he teaches.

WHERE WE ARE

Śiva is the god of opposites held together: the wild one of the cremation ground and the gentle husband of Pārvatī, the still yogī on Kailāsa and the cosmic dancer, the destroyer who is called Śiva, “the auspicious”.

This guide answers the simple question: who is Śiva? It starts in the Veda, reads his body and ornaments, meets his forms, tells four of his great stories in full, and ends with worship, temples and the teachers who loved him.

His household is in our Śiva’s Family and Symbols guide; his hymns are in the Śiva section.

THE NAME AND THE VEDA

From Rudra to Śiva

Śiva. The word means “auspicious, benign, kind”. In the Veda it is first an adjective, a kind name given to a fearsome god.

Rudra. Only three of the Ṛgveda’s 1,028 hymns are addressed to Rudra, “the roarer”, a storm-god who sends sickness and heals it. The Veda already sees two sides in him: wild and gentle.

The Śatarudrīya. The Yajurveda’s great litany of his names, the Śrī Rudram · 3 parts, bows to him in hundreds of forms, from the god of hills to the thief and the potter.

The Upaniṣad. The Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad first presents Rudra-Śiva as the one God, creator and liberator, identical with Brahman.

ZAPPY: So Śiva was a storm first?

PROF. BOLT: The roar came first, Zappy. The kindness was always inside it.

Rudra becomes Śiva.

READING HIS BODY

Every ornament means something

👁️ Third eye

The eye of fire on his forehead, which burnt Kāma, the god of desire, to ash.

🌙 Crescent moon

He wears the moon in his hair: Candraśekhara, “moon-crowned”.

🌊 Gaṅgā

The river falls into his matted locks and flows gently out: Gaṅgādhara.

🐍 The snake

Vāsuki coiled round his neck: fear tamed and worn as an ornament.

🔱 Trident and drum

The triśūla, often read as the three guṇas or three powers; the ḍamaru, the drum of creation’s rhythm.

⚪ Ash and blue throat

Ash for impermanence; the blue throat (Nīlakaṇṭha) from holding the world’s poison.

ZAPPY: He wears a snake as a necklace?

PROF. BOLT: What frightens everyone else is jewellery to him, Zappy.

Every ornament a story.

HIS FORMS

Many faces of one god

💃 Naṭarāja

Lord of the dance, in a ring of fire: the fierce tāṇḍava and the graceful lāsya. Cidambaram is his great seat.

🌳 Dakṣiṇāmūrti

The silent teacher facing south under a banyan, teaching sages by silence.

☯️ Ardhanārīśvara

Half Śiva, half Pārvatī: the two powers of the world in one body.

🐕 Bhairava

The fierce guardian; read our Kāla Bhairavāṣṭakam.

🪨 The liṅga

The formless sign of Śiva, the most common way he is worshipped.

🐄 Paśupati

“Lord of beings”: the herdsman of all souls.

ZAPPY: Which one is the real Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: All of them, Zappy. One god, many doors.

STORY 1 · THE POISON

Nīlakaṇṭha, the blue-throated

The story

From the Purāṇic account of the churning of the ocean.

Gods and demons churned the ocean of milk with Mount Mandara and the serpent Vāsuki, hoping for nectar.
Before any treasure came up, a deadly poison, hālāhala, rose and began to burn the worlds.
Everyone ran to Śiva. Out of compassion he gathered the poison and drank it.
Pārvatī pressed his throat so it would not go down. It stayed there and turned his throat blue.
So he is Nīlakaṇṭha. Many keep Pradoṣa in memory of this night; read our Pradoṣa · 8 articles guides.

ZAPPY: He just drank it?

PROF. BOLT: Someone had to, Zappy. That is Śiva: he takes what no one else can.

He holds the poison.

STORY 2 · THE DESCENT OF GAṄGĀ

The river in his hair

The story

From the Rāmāyaṇa and Purāṇas.

King Bhagīratha needed the heavenly river to purify the ashes of his ancestors, the sons of Sagara.
Gaṅgā agreed to come, but her fall would shatter the earth.
Bhagīratha prayed to Śiva, who stood beneath the sky and caught the river in his matted hair.
Gaṅgā wandered in his locks until he released her in gentle streams.
So he is Gaṅgādhara, bearer of the Gaṅgā, and the river still flows from the Himālaya he guards.

ZAPPY: A river lost in his hair!

PROF. BOLT: Strength that catches a fall so no one breaks, Zappy.

He breaks the fall.

STORY 3 · KĀMA AND PĀRVATĪ

The third eye opens

The story

From the Purāṇas and Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava.

After Satī’s death Śiva sat in deep meditation. The gods needed his son to defeat the demon Tāraka.
Indra sent Kāma, god of desire, to stir him; Kāma shot a flower-arrow as Pārvatī served him.
Śiva opened his third eye and burnt Kāma to ash.
Pārvatī won him not by charm but by fierce penance, eating not even a leaf, so she is called Aparṇā.
Śiva, disguised, tested her, then revealed himself; they married, and Skanda was born. Read our Subrahmaṇya · 4 parts guides.

ZAPPY: Desire didn’t work but penance did?

PROF. BOLT: That is the whole point, Zappy.

Won by penance.

STORY 4 · THE PILLAR OF FIRE

Liṅgodbhava: where is his end?

The story

From the Śiva and Liṅga Purāṇas.

Brahmā and Viṣṇu argued over which of them was supreme.
A blazing pillar of light appeared between them, with no top and no bottom.
Brahmā became a swan and flew up; Viṣṇu became a boar and dug down. Neither found an end.
Śiva appeared from the pillar: the liṅga of light, beyond both.
This is Liṅgodbhava, the root of the Jyotirliṅgas · 13 articles and of Śivarātri.

ZAPPY: Even Viṣṇu couldn’t find the end?

PROF. BOLT: Because there isn’t one, Zappy.

No end to him.

THE LIṄGA

The sign of the formless

A sign. Liṅga means “mark, sign”. It shows the god who has no form: a smooth column on a base (pīṭha), worshipped in every Śiva temple.

Abhiṣeka. Water, milk and other offerings are poured over it, often from a pot hanging above, while the Rudram or Namaḥ Śivāya is chanted.

Bilva. The three-lobed bilva leaf is his favourite offering; read our Bilvāṣṭakam.

Readings. Traditions read the liṅga as the pillar of light, the axis of the world, or Śiva-Śakti together with its base. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: Why not a statue?

PROF. BOLT: Because he is beyond form, Zappy, and the liṅga says so.

The formless shown.

HOW HE IS WORSHIPPED

Mantra, night and Monday

Namaḥ Śivāya. The five-syllable mantra, the pañcākṣara; read our Pañcākṣara Stotram.

Mondays and Pradoṣa. Monday is his day; Pradoṣa, the twilight of the thirteenth lunar day, is his hour.

Mahāśivarātri. His great night of fasting and vigil; read our Mahāśivarātri guide.

Ash and rudrākṣa. Devotees wear three lines of ash and rudrākṣa beads.

The death-conquering mantra. The Mahāmṛtyuñjaya · 4 parts in 1 article is chanted for healing.

ZAPPY: Why at night?

PROF. BOLT: His is the god of the dark hour, Zappy, when all else sleeps.

Namaḥ Śivāya.

HIS GREAT TEMPLES

Light, elements and mountains

The twelve Jyotirliṅgas. From Somanātha to Ghṛṣṇeśvara; read our 12 Jyotirliṅgas · 13 articles series.

The five elements. Earth, water, fire, air and space at Kāñcī, Tiruvānaikkā, Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, Kāḷahasti and Cidambaram: our Pañcabhūta Sthalas · 6 articles.

The Himālaya. The Pañca Kedār · 6 articles and Kailāsa itself.

Close to home. Nañjanagūḍu, the god who swallowed poison, and Male Mahadeshwara.

ZAPPY: Kailāsa is a real mountain?

PROF. BOLT: In Tibet, Zappy. Pilgrims walk round it, never climb it.

Śiva everywhere.

HIS DEVOTEES AND TRADITIONS

Saints, philosophers and Basavaṇṇa

Śaiva Siddhānta. The Tamil tradition of the Āgamas and the 63 Nāyanmār saints, whose hymns (Tēvāram) are still sung in temples.

Kashmir Śaivism. A monistic philosophy (Trika) in which the world is Śiva’s own free play of consciousness.

Vīraśaivas or Liṅgāyats. Basavaṇṇa and the vacana poets of Karnataka taught that every devotee carries the liṅga on the body.

Śaṅkara. The Advaita teacher wrote hymns such as the Nirvāṇa Ṣaṭkam: “I am Śiva”.

ZAPPY: A liṅga worn on the body?

PROF. BOLT: The temple inside you, Zappy. That was Basavaṇṇa’s idea.

Many paths to him.

WHAT ŚIVA TEACHES

Destruction that renews

Endings make room. As destroyer he clears the old so the new can grow. Many read death and change as his grace. (Interpretation.)

Holding pain. Nīlakaṇṭha keeps the poison in his throat: strength is holding what hurts without passing it on. (Interpretation.)

Stillness. The yogī on Kailāsa shows that peace is found within.

Opposites joined. Ascetic and husband, fierce and gentle, male and female: Śiva holds them together.

ZAPPY: So destruction can be kind?

PROF. BOLT: When it clears the way, Zappy. That’s why he’s called Śiva.

Destruction that is auspicious.
