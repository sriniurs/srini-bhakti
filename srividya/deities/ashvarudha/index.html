AŚVĀRŪḌHĀ

Commander of the Goddess’s cavalry

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · DEITIES

FOREWORD

Riding a white horse at the head of countless cavalry, holding a noose and a goad, is Aśvārūḍhā, “she who rides the horse”. She commands the horses of Lalitā’s army.

Tradition says she rose from Lalitā’s own noose. This guide covers her name, form, war, her line in the Sahasranāma, horses in Indian thought, and the old image of the senses as horses that she rides.

ArmyMillions upon millions of horses, by the Sahasranāma’s count

MeaningThe senses, swift as horses, brought under the Goddess’s rein
WorshipMostly within lineages; rarely a temple of her own

ZAPPY: A cavalry goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Every army needs speed. She's Lalitā's lightning.

Lalitā’s army in the Lalitopākhyāna is organised like a real army, with commanders for each arm. Aśvārūḍhā commands the cavalry. She is less famous than Vārāhī or Śyāmalā, but she is named in the Sahasranāma and honoured in many lineages, and her image of a goddess riding at speed is memorable.

This guide covers her name, her origin from Lalitā’s noose, her form, her place in the war and in the Sahasranāma, the place of horses in Indian thought, and the inner reading that makes her the rider of the senses.

Her lineage worship, including her vidyā, belongs to teachers and is not described.

THE NAME

Horse · mounted
AśvaHorse, one of the most prized animals of Vedic India
ĀrūḍhāMounted, risen upon
AśvārūḍhāThe horse-rider
Other titlesAśvārūḍhā Devī; sometimes Turaṅgārūḍhā, with another word for horse
OfficeCommander of the turaga-sainya, the horse-army
GenderA woman commanding cavalry: unremarkable in the Goddess’s army, rare in history

ZAPPY: Did women really ride into battle in India?

PROF. BOLT: Some did: queens like Rāṇī Durgāvatī and Rāṇī Lakṣmībāī. In Lalitā's army, it's the whole command structure.

Aśva, horse, is one of the oldest and most prestigious words in Indo-European languages, and horses were central to Vedic culture: wealth, war, speed and the sun’s chariot. A goddess named “the horse-rider” carries all that weight of meaning.

Ārūḍhā, “mounted”, also has a subtler sense: “risen upon”, “established on”. Some commentators enjoy reading her name as the one established upon the senses, mastering them from above.

Women riding to war are rare in Indian history but not absent. Rāṇī Durgāvatī of Gondwana and Rāṇī Lakṣmībāī of Jhansi rode at the head of their troops. In the Goddess’s army, female command is simply the order of things.

There is also a Vedic echo. The sun is often called a horse, or a rider on horses, and the dawn goddess Uṣas drives a chariot of red horses. A goddess on a white horse at the front of the Goddess’s army belongs, in imagery at least, to that ancient picture of light racing across the sky.

BORN FROM THE NOOSE

Where she comes from
The nooseLalitā’s pāśa, which is attachment (rāga) in her hand
EmergenceFrom it, tradition says, Aśvārūḍhā rose with her army of horses
PairSampatkarī rose from the goad (aṅkuśa), with the elephants
MeaningLalitā’s weapons become living commanders
CautionTellings differ on details; the pairing is common in Śrīvidyā writing

ZAPPY: Her weapons turn into generals?

PROF. BOLT: That's the image. What Lalitā holds becomes who fights for her. The noose of attachment, mastered, becomes a cavalry.

Śrīvidyā writers often describe Lalitā’s four weapons as giving rise to her officers. The noose and goad, in particular, are said to produce Aśvārūḍhā and Sampatkarī with their armies of horses and elephants. Tellings differ, and some give the origins differently; the general idea is widespread.

The image is theologically neat. Lalitā’s weapons are symbols: the noose is attachment, the goad anger. When they become living commanders, the symbols come alive and fight on her side. Attachment, mastered, becomes a cavalry; anger, mastered, becomes a force of elephants.

This also explains why both commanders carry Lalitā’s noose and goad in their own hands. They are those weapons, in person.

There is a parallel in the way Lalitā’s other emblems behave in the war. Her fingernails produce the ten avatāras of Viṣṇu, her smile produces Gaṇeśa, her glance produces fresh forces. In the Lalitopākhyāna, every part of the Goddess is potentially an army. The noose and goad giving rise to commanders fits that pattern.

HER FORM

ZAPPY: Why does she carry the noose and goad too?

PROF. BOLT: She's acting with Lalitā's authority. The noose catches, the goad steers. Exactly what you need to manage a horse army.

Images of Aśvārūḍhā are rare outside paintings of Lalitā’s war and lineage illustrations. Where she appears, she is usually a crowned goddess on a white horse, holding the noose and goad, sometimes with a whip or cane in another hand.

The white horse recalls Uccaiḥśravas, the seven-headed white horse that rose from the churning of the ocean, and the white horses of the sun. Whiteness signals purity and divine origin.

Some lineage descriptions give her a darker colour and a fierce aspect. As with many attendant deities, details vary between teachers.

Lineage paintings sometimes show her seated sideways on the horse, as queens rode in procession, rather than astride at a gallop. The two images carry different moods: one ceremonial and royal, one martial and swift. Both are found.

IN LALITĀ’S WAR

The cavalry in action
RankAmong Lalitā’s field commanders, below Vārāhī the general
ArmyCountless horses, swift and terrible
PartnerSampatkarī’s elephants: speed and weight together
PlacementThe cavalry surrounds Lalitā’s chariot in the Sahasranāma’s picture
MorePart 4 of the Lalitopākhyāna

ZAPPY: Who wins, horses or elephants?

PROF. BOLT: On Lalitā's side, both. Horses for speed, elephants for weight. A good army uses each where it's needed.

In the Lalitopākhyāna’s battle scenes, the Śakti army advances in vast formations. The cavalry under Aśvārūḍhā and the elephant corps under Sampatkarī surround the three great chariots of Lalitā, Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā. Their numbers are described in the language of the infinite.

Individual episodes centre on the higher commanders and on Lalitā herself, but the cavalry is ever-present, a moving wall around the Goddess. Painters who illustrate the war show rows of horses and riders streaming across the canvas.

Our Lalitopākhyāna retelling, Part 4, follows the battle in order.

Commentators also note the strategic logic of the army. The cavalry are fast but light; the elephants are slow but crushing; the chariots carry the commanders and their divine weapons. Each part has its role, and the Goddess’s victory depends on all working together, a picture of a well-ordered mind as much as a well-ordered army.

AfterNames for the chariots of Lalitā, Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā

PatternThe thousand names march through Lalitā’s army in order

ZAPPY: Crores of crores of horses? That's a lot.

PROF. BOLT: It's poetry for ‘beyond counting’. The point is her army is limitless.

sampatkarīsamārūḍhasindhuravrajasevitā
aśvārūḍhādhiṣṭhitāśvakoṭikoṭibhir āvṛtā

She who is attended by herds of elephants ridden by Sampatkarī. She who is surrounded by crores upon crores of horses commanded by Aśvārūḍhā. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

The two army names in the Sahasranāma come together: first the elephants of Sampatkarī, then the horses of Aśvārūḍhā. They are followed by the names for the three great chariots, so that the chant moves from the outer ranks inward to the commanders.

Koṭi, ten million, multiplied by itself, is the Sanskrit way of saying “beyond counting”. The Sahasranāma uses such numbers not as a census but to convey limitlessness: the Goddess’s forces cannot be exhausted.

Bhāskararāya comments that the horses, which move swiftly, can be read as the senses, and the elephants, which are strong and heavy, as the deeper tendencies of the mind. This inner reading runs through much later writing on these two goddesses.

HORSES IN INDIAN THOUGHT

Speed, power, the senses
VedicThe horse is wealth, victory and the sun’s speed
AśvamedhaThe horse sacrifice of kings: the horse roams, and where it goes the king rules
UccaiḥśravasThe white horse that rose from the churning of the ocean
The AśvinsTwin horse-gods of healing
HayagrīvaThe horse-headed teacher of the Lalitopākhyāna: his guide follows in this series

ZAPPY: Horses everywhere!

PROF. BOLT: India's gods loved them. Speed, light, kingship, healing: the horse carries all of it.

Horses entered Indian religion early and deeply. The Ṛgveda praises horses as wealth and as the swift steeds of the gods; the Aśvamedha, the horse sacrifice, was the grandest royal rite, in which a horse was allowed to roam for a year and wherever it went, the king’s rule was claimed.

The Purāṇas add Uccaiḥśravas, the seven-headed white horse from the churning of the ocean, claimed by Indra; the Aśvins, the twin horse-headed physicians of the gods; and Sūrya’s chariot drawn by seven horses. The horse is everywhere associated with light, speed and power.

Hayagrīva, the horse-headed form of Viṣṇu who narrates the Lalitopākhyāna, completes the picture. The tradition’s chief storyteller and one of its army commanders are both linked to the horse.

THE CHARIOT OF THE BODY

The Kaṭha Upaniṣad’s image
The imageThe self is the rider; the body the chariot; the intellect the driver
HorsesThe senses
ReinsThe mind
RoadsThe objects of the senses
LessonWild horses drag the chariot anywhere; tamed ones reach the goal
AśvārūḍhāThe one who rides the horses well: the senses under divine rein

ZAPPY: So she's the perfect driver?

PROF. BOLT: That's the inner reading. Your senses are horses. She shows you can ride them without being thrown.

The Kaṭha Upaniṣad gives one of the most famous images in Indian thought: know the Self as the lord of a chariot, the body as the chariot, the intellect as the charioteer and the mind as the reins. The senses are the horses, and the objects of the senses are the roads they run along.

If the charioteer is unwise and the reins slack, the senses run wild like untamed horses. If the charioteer is wise and the reins steady, the horses obey, and the chariot reaches its goal. The Upaniṣad uses this image to teach self-mastery as the path to liberation.

Śrīvidyā writers draw the natural connection: Aśvārūḍhā, the perfect rider of horses, is the Goddess as the power that holds the senses in steady, loving control.

THE NOOSE AND THE HORSES

Attachment and the senses
Lalitā’s nooseRāga: attachment, desire for things
From itThe horses, the senses that run after things
Under commandIn Aśvārūḍhā’s hands, those horses serve the Goddess
Not destroyedThe senses are not killed but ridden
ContrastAscetic paths may close the senses; Śrīvidyā tames them

ZAPPY: So attachment makes horses, and she rides them?

PROF. BOLT: Neatly put. Attachment sends the senses running. Under her rein, the same speed carries you to the Goddess.

The link between the noose and the horses is a lovely piece of symbolism. The noose is attachment, the pull toward things we desire. The senses are the horses that run toward those things. A noose catches and holds; a rider holds the reins.

In ordinary life, attachment drives the senses and the senses drag the person. In Aśvārūḍhā, the order is reversed: the Goddess holds the noose and rides the horses. Attachment and senses alike serve her purpose.

This is characteristic of Śrīvidyā’s approach. It does not ask the practitioner to kill the senses or abandon desire, but to bring them under the Goddess’s rule.

There is also a warning in the image. A horse can be ridden or it can bolt. The same senses that carry a person toward the good can carry them away from it. Aśvārūḍhā’s presence in the army is a reminder that the work of riding never stops; the reins must always be held.

IN LINEAGE WORSHIP

Where she is honoured
StatusOne of the important attendant deities in some Śrīvidyā lineages
VidyāHer own vidyā is given within those lineages
PurposeOften associated with swift help and with mastery over the senses
TemplesVery few public shrines; images mostly in paintings of Lalitā’s army

ZAPPY: Why so little public worship?

PROF. BOLT: She's an officer of the army, honoured where the whole army is honoured. Most devotees meet her in the Sahasranāma and the story.

In some Śrīvidyā lineages, Aśvārūḍhā has her own vidyā and is honoured as an attendant deity of Lalitā, alongside Sampatkarī. Practitioners associate her with swift help and with mastery of the senses.

Public temples to her are very rare. She is most often encountered in the Sahasranāma, in paintings of the war, and in lineage worship.

As always, her vidyā and worship are given by teachers and are not described in this series.

Practitioners who honour her sometimes speak of her as especially close to those whose work demands speed and decisiveness: soldiers, doctors, drivers, people who must act quickly under pressure. That association grows naturally from her image, not from any special rule.

IN ART

The army on canvas
PaintingsTanjore and Mysore paintings of Lalitā’s war show her cavalry
MuralsTemple murals of the Lalitopākhyāna, where they survive
Horse imagerySouth Indian temple art loves rearing horses, as on the pillars of Śrīraṅgam
ModernIllustrated retellings of the war for children

ZAPPY: Are there statues of her?

PROF. BOLT: Very few. Painters liked her better than sculptors: you need a whole battlefield to show a cavalry.

Paintings of the Lalitopākhyāna war, especially in the Tanjore and Mysore styles, often show the Śakti army in full array: the three towering chariots at the centre, Vārāhī and Śyāmalā prominent, and around them the cavalry and elephants of Aśvārūḍhā and Sampatkarī. These are some of the busiest and most joyful paintings of the tradition.

South Indian temple sculpture is famous for its horses: the rearing horse pillars of the Śeṣagiri maṇḍapa at Śrīraṅgam, the horses of the chariot-shaped halls at Kumbhakoṇam and elsewhere. These are not images of Aśvārūḍhā, but they show the love for the horse that her imagery draws on.

Modern illustrated books of the Lalitopākhyāna for young readers have brought the cavalry goddess to a new audience.

A good way to find her in a painting of the war is to look for the one goddess on horseback near Lalitā’s chariot, with the noose and goad in her hands. Painters usually give her a slightly larger size than the riders around her, to mark her as their commander.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Speed under command
SpeedSwift help: devotees pray to her when time is short
MasterySenses as horses, ridden not suppressed
ServiceEven attachment, mastered, becomes a servant of the Goddess
CourageA woman at the head of cavalry: fearlessness
Inner readingThe trained mind that holds the reins of the senses

ZAPPY: So I should ride my senses, not fight them?

PROF. BOLT: That's her lesson. A rider who hates horses never gets anywhere. Love them, train them, steer them.

Devotees who know her often pray to her when they need help quickly, in an emergency or under pressure. Her speed is her gift.

Her deeper lesson is about the senses. Śrīvidyā does not treat the senses as enemies. It treats them as horses: powerful, fast, valuable, and in need of a good rider. Aśvārūḍhā is that rider.

Inwardly, many teachers read her as the trained mind that holds the reins of the senses lightly but firmly, the way a good rider and a good horse move as one.

There is a simple daily version of her teaching. Before acting on an impulse, notice it; hold it for a moment; then decide where to go. That pause, the rider’s hand on the reins, is a small act of the discipline she embodies.

WHAT SHE IS NOT

Clearing up mix-ups
Not a minor figureA named commander in Lalitā’s hymn
Not separate from LalitāHer power, arising from her noose
Not a speed charmSwift help is prayed for, not guaranteed
Not a war goddess for human warsHer battles are with Bhaṇḍa, and within
Not do-it-yourselfHer lineage worship comes from teachers

ZAPPY: Can I pray to her when I'm late for school?

PROF. BOLT: You can pray anytime. She might suggest leaving earlier next time.

Because she is little known, Aśvārūḍhā is sometimes presented online as an obscure “secret” goddess with special powers. In the tradition, she is a well-defined officer of Lalitā’s army, named in the Sahasranāma and honoured within lineages.

She is also sometimes taken as a war goddess for human conflicts. Her battle is with Bhaṇḍa, and, in the inner reading, with the restlessness of the senses.

Her vidyā, like all vidyās, comes from a teacher.

A last mix-up worth correcting: Aśvārūḍhā is sometimes confused with Hayagrīva because both are linked to horses. They are entirely different: Hayagrīva is a horse-headed form of Viṣṇu and the narrator of the Lalitopākhyāna; Aśvārūḍhā is a goddess who rides a horse in Lalitā’s army. The Hayagrīva guide explains his role as Agastya’s teacher.
