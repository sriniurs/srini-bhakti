BĀLĀ

The Goddess as a girl

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · DEITIES

FOREWORD

She is the smallest figure in Lalitā’s court: a girl of about nine, red as the dawn, holding a book and a rosary. She is Bālā Tripurasundarī, and in the great war she defeats, alone, the thirty sons of the demon Bhaṇḍa.

This guide covers her form and gifts, her battle, the Sahasranāma name that remembers it, the three ages of the Goddess, the worship of girls as the Goddess at Navarātri, her place in families, and her shrines.

Śrī Bālāmbikāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

AT A GLANCE

Bālā on one page

NameBālā, “the girl”; Bālā Tripurasundarī; Bālāmbikā
AgeTraditionally about nine
FormRed, four-armed: book, rosary, fear-not and boon gestures; seated on a red lotus
Great deedKills the thirty sons of the demon Bhaṇḍa
GiftsLearning, a fresh start, protection of children
Place on the pathOften the first form of the Goddess a Śrīvidyā student meets
ShrineNemili, Tamil Nadu, among others

ZAPPY: A goddess who's a child?

PROF. BOLT: A child who wins a war. The tradition loves turning expectations upside down.

Bālā is the most approachable form of the Goddess in Śrīvidyā. She is small, smiling and bright, and her image of a red-clad girl with a book and a rosary belongs in a child’s room as naturally as in a temple. Yet her story is one of the boldest in the Lalitopākhyāna.

The Foundations guide on Lalitā placed Bālā among the Goddess’s three ages. This guide goes further: her own form and gifts, her war in detail, the name in the Sahasranāma that remembers her, the worship of young girls as the Goddess, her place in families, and her shrines.

As throughout this series, her vidyā and the rites of her worship are left to teachers. Her story, form, names and meaning are given in full.

WHO IS BĀLĀ?

Daughter, or the queen as a girl
As daughterIn the Lalitopākhyāna, Bālā is Lalitā’s daughter, born of her own power
As Lalitā herselfMany lineages treat Bālā as Lalitā in childhood, not a separate goddess
TripurasundarīHer full name places her within the same Goddess as Ṣoḍaśī and Lalitā
Bālāmbikā“Mother Bālā”: even a child-goddess is called Mother
CharacterPlayful, fearless, quick, loving
Not to be confusedWith Bālā forms of other gods, such as the child Kṛṣṇa or Bāla Subrahmaṇya

ZAPPY: Daughter or the same person? Which is it?

PROF. BOLT: Both readings are old. In the story she's a daughter; in worship she's the Goddess young. A mother sees herself in her daughter, and the tradition takes that literally.

Indian goddess traditions often present the Divine at different ages, each with a different emotional tone. Bālā is the tone of tenderness and delight: the love a parent feels for a small daughter, offered to the Goddess.

The question of whether Bālā is a separate goddess has a practical answer in worship. Śrīvidyā lineages usually treat her as Lalitā’s own form, honoured on the same Śrīcakra. The Lalitopākhyāna’s presentation of her as a daughter gives the war story its emotional power without dividing the Goddess.

There are many other “child” forms in Hindu worship: Bāla Kṛṣṇa, Bāla Murugan, Bāla Gaṇapati. Bālā is the Goddess’s counterpart to these, and like them she draws a particular kind of affectionate devotion.

Some writers also link Bālā to the Goddess’s first manifestation in the Lalitopākhyāna. When Lalitā rises from the fire of the gods’ sacrifice, she appears in the full bloom of youth; Bālā, in this reading, is the potential of that youth before it unfolds, the Goddess as she is before any story begins.

HER FORM

How she is pictured
ColourRed as the rising sun, or as the early morning sky
Upper handsBook (pustaka) and rosary (akṣamālā): learning and practice
Lower handsAbhaya (fear not) and varada (boon-giving)
SeatA red lotus
CrownSmall, with the crescent moon
ExpressionSmiling, bright-eyed, with a child’s directness

ZAPPY: Book and beads, like a student?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. She's the eternal student and the teacher at once. The book is knowledge, the beads are steady practice. Every learner starts there.

Her colour is the red of dawn rather than the deep red of Lalitā’s full sovereignty. Writers describe it as like the first light of morning or a freshly bloomed hibiscus: the start of the day, matching her role as the start of the path.

The book and rosary are among the oldest emblems of learning in Indian art, held by Sarasvatī, by Brahmā and by many sages. In Bālā’s hands they mark her as a patron of students, and the two lower gestures make her approachable: do not fear; ask.

Some descriptions give her a crown of rubies and a garland of flowers, and some show her seated on a lion or in the lap of Lalitā. The four-armed form with book and rosary is by far the most common.

In meditation, practitioners are often told to picture the Goddess at a particular age, and the age changes the feeling of the meditation. Picturing Bālā brings tenderness and lightness; picturing Lalitā brings awe and majesty. Bālā is often the form taught to those who find the full royal image overwhelming.

THE SONS OF BHAṆḌA

Bālā’s war
SettingThe great war of Lalitā against Bhaṇḍa, Part 5 of the Lalitopākhyāna
Lalitā’s hesitationShe is reluctant to send so young a girl into battle
Bālā insistsShe begs to fight, and is allowed
The deedShe destroys all thirty of Bhaṇḍa’s sons
JoyLalitā and the whole army rejoice in the child’s valour
AfterBhaṇḍa, grieving and enraged, moves toward the final battle

ZAPPY: Thirty demons, one girl?

PROF. BOLT: And she wins easily. The story's message is simple: never mistake small for weak.

In the Lalitopākhyāna, the war goes in stages. After the officers have done their work, Bhaṇḍa sends his thirty sons into battle. Bālā, watching, asks to fight them. Lalitā is unwilling to risk so young a child, but Bālā will not be refused, and she rides out.

The Purāṇa describes her destroying the thirty sons in a single engagement, to the amazement and delight of the Śakti army. Lalitā embraces her and praises her. The scene is one of the most affectionate in the whole war.

The loss breaks Bhaṇḍa. Grief and rage drive him to his final moves: the obstacle-yantra destroyed by Gaṇeśa, and at last the confrontation with Lalitā herself. Bālā’s victory is thus a turning point in the story, told in Part 5 of our Lalitopākhyāna retelling.

IN THE SAHASRANĀMA

The name that remembers her
The nameLalitā is “delighted by the valour of Bālā, eager to slay the sons of Bhaṇḍa”
PlacementIn the war section, before the names on Gaṇeśa and the officers’ victories
MeaningThe queen’s joy in her daughter’s courage
Other linksNames describing the Goddess as ever-young, a girl and a maiden
CommentaryBhāskararāya explains the name with the Lalitopākhyāna story

ZAPPY: Lalitā is proud of her daughter!

PROF. BOLT: Of course. And the thousand names stop to say so. A mother's pride, recorded in scripture.

bhaṇḍaputravadhodyuktabālāvikramananditā

She who is delighted by the valour of Bālā, eager to slay the sons of Bhaṇḍa. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

The Sahasranāma’s war section gives each phase of the battle a name. Bālā’s name comes after the officers’ and before Gaṇeśa’s, following the Lalitopākhyāna’s order. Chanting the sequence is like walking through the story.

The word nanditā, “delighted”, is worth noticing. The Sahasranāma could have said simply that Lalitā won; instead it records her emotion. The Goddess here is not an impersonal force but a mother taking joy in her child.

Bhāskararāya also gives inner readings of the war names, in which Bhaṇḍa’s sons stand for the restless offspring of ignorance. A fresh, innocent devotion, Bālā, is what destroys them.

Other names in the thousand call the Goddess bālā directly or describe her as one who plays like a child, and commentators connect these with Bālā as well. The Sahasranāma itself, in this sense, moves between the ages of the Goddess as freely as devotees do.

THREE AGES, ONE GODDESS

Bālā, Ṣoḍaśī, Lalitā
BālāThe girl: beginning, learning, innocence
ṢoḍaśīThe sixteen-year-old: fullness of youth and beauty
Lalitā · RājarājeśvarīThe queen: sovereignty and complete power
Not declineThe tradition’s ages never include old age: the Goddess does not wither
MoonSome writers match the ages to the waxing moon: thin crescent to full
ContrastDhūmāvatī among the Mahāvidyās, the aged widow, is the one who shows the other end of life

ZAPPY: No grandmother form?

PROF. BOLT: Not in Śrīvidyā. Other traditions have one: Dhūmāvatī, the old widow. Śrīvidyā keeps its Goddess forever rising, like the morning.

The three ages also map onto the stages of a practitioner’s life on the path. The beginner meets the Goddess as Bālā, close and gentle. With maturity comes Ṣoḍaśī, the fullness of beauty and power. At the summit stands Lalitā, the sovereign, in whom the practitioner’s own sovereignty is recognised.

The absence of old age is deliberate. Lalitā is called nityayauvanā, ever-young. Decline belongs to the world of time, and the Goddess, in this tradition, stands beyond it. Other traditions, such as the Mahāvidyās, deliberately include old age and widowhood as faces of the Divine; Śrīvidyā keeps to the rising arc.

The moon’s phases offer another parallel. The waxing crescent grows night by night to the full moon; Bālā, Ṣoḍaśī and Lalitā can be read as that growth, with the Tithi Nityās, the goddesses of the lunar days, filling in the steps.

PATRON OF LEARNING

Book and rosary
StudentsPrayed to before study, exams and the start of a course
VidyārambhaThe first lesson of a small child, often on Vijayadaśamī
AkṣamālāRosary of letters: in Sanskrit, akṣa can mean the letters a to kṣa
Beginner’s mindBālā is the patron of starting fresh, at any age
LinkClose in spirit to Sarasvatī and to Śyāmalā

ZAPPY: Her rosary is made of letters?

PROF. BOLT: One reading of the word: akṣa-mālā, a garland from a to kṣa, the whole alphabet. Learning to read is already a prayer.

In Indian homes, the start of learning is itself a ceremony. A small child, often on Vijayadaśamī, writes the first letters in rice or sand with a parent or teacher guiding the finger. Many families invoke Sarasvatī; Śrīvidyā families often invoke Bālā as well.

The reading of akṣamālā as a garland of letters comes from the tradition’s fondness for sound. The fifty letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, from a to kṣa, are imagined as beads strung on a thread. To count them is to recite the whole of language, the raw material of every mantra and every book.

Bālā’s gift of a fresh start is valued by adults too. People beginning a new course of study, a new job or a new phase of life sometimes pray to her for the openness of a beginner.

Many schools in South India, especially traditional pāṭhaśālās where Sanskrit and the Vedas are taught, keep an image of Sarasvatī or of the Goddess with book and rosary at the front of the classroom. Bālā fits naturally in that company, and some teachers ask students to begin each day with a short prayer to her.

THE GODDESS IN A GIRL

Kanyā and Kumārī pūjā
PracticeAt Navarātri, young girls are honoured as the Goddess: their feet washed, fed, given gifts
Devī BhāgavataNames the girls by age, from two to ten; texts and regions differ
SouthOften on the eighth or ninth day of Navarātri
North and eastKanjak on Aṣṭamī; Kumārī pūjā in Bengal at Durgā Pūjā
NepalThe living goddess Kumārī of Kathmandu: a related but distinct tradition
Link to BālāDevotees often think of Bālā when they honour a girl as the Goddess

ZAPPY: Little girls are worshipped?

PROF. BOLT: For a day, yes. Washed feet, new clothes, sweets. It reminds everyone that the Goddess is in every girl, and that she's to be honoured, not just protected.

The worship of young girls as the Goddess is one of the most widespread Navarātri practices in India. Girls, often nine of them, are invited to a home, their feet washed, their foreheads marked with kumkum, and they are fed and given gifts. Each is treated, for that hour, as the Goddess herself.

The Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa gives detailed instructions and assigns names to girls of different ages. Regional customs vary widely in the age range, the day and the number of girls. In Bengal the Kumārī pūjā at Belur Math is especially famous.

Nepal’s Kumārī, a young girl chosen to be worshipped as a living goddess for years until puberty, is a related but separate tradition linked with the goddess Taleju. It is mentioned here as a parallel, not as part of Śrīvidyā.

BĀLĀ AND FAMILIES

A child-goddess for children
For childrenParents pray to her for their children’s health, learning and courage
For girlsA model of a fearless girl who is cherished, not sheltered into weakness
For childless couplesSome pray to Bālā for a child; Santāna Lakṣmī is also invoked
LullabiesSongs to the Goddess as a little girl are sung in Tamil and Telugu homes
Home shrinesA small Bālā image or picture is common in Śrīvidyā households

ZAPPY: So she's like a big sister to kids?

PROF. BOLT: A big sister who once beat thirty demons. Not a bad one to have.

Bālā’s appeal to families is natural. Parents pray to her for their children’s health, studies and confidence, and many homes keep her picture where children study. For a child, a goddess who is herself a child is easy to love.

Her story also offers a strong image for girls. Bālā is cherished by her mother, yet she is not kept at home: she asks to face the hardest fight, and she wins. Many modern devotees see in her a picture of a girl who is both loved and capable.

For couples longing for a child, prayers to Bālā sit alongside other traditions: Santāna Lakṣmī, Santāna Gopāla and local fertility shrines. The tradition presents these as prayers, not as guarantees.

The festival calendar gives her a natural place too. On Navarātri’s ninth and tenth days, when books and instruments are honoured and children start their learning, Bālā’s two emblems, book and rosary, sum up the occasion: knowledge received and knowledge practised.

WHERE SHE IS HONOURED

Shrines of Bālā
NemiliThe Śrī Bālā Pīṭham, near Arakkonam, Tamil Nadu: a family shrine that draws many devotees
Lalitā templesBālā often has her own image or shrine
Home worshipMost of her worship happens in homes, not great temples
NavarātriKanyā pūjā links her worship to the autumn festival everywhere
Our approachPlaces described; no claims of special powers made for any shrine

ZAPPY: Why so few big temples?

PROF. BOLT: She's a household goddess at heart. Children live in homes, and so does Bālā.

The shrine at Nemili, near Arakkonam in Tamil Nadu, is perhaps the best-known Bālā centre today. It is a family-maintained shrine with a small image, and draws devotees from across the South. Its tradition tells of the image being found by a devotee of the family generations ago.

Bālā images and shrines also appear in many Lalitā and Kāmākṣī temples, and in some Śrīvidyā maṭhas. But most of her worship is domestic: a picture or small image in the home shrine, with simple offerings of milk, sweets and flowers.

Because so much of her worship is in homes, Bālā’s tradition is less visible to visitors than that of the great temple goddesses. It is no less alive for that.

Visitors to any Bālā shrine notice the tone of worship. The offerings are often things a child would enjoy: sweets, fruit, small toys, bangles and bright clothes. Devotees speak to her with the familiarity reserved for a beloved child of the family.

IN ART AND MUSIC

How she is shown and sung
PaintingsTanjore and Mysore paintings show a small red-clad girl on a lotus with book and rosary
With LalitāCourt scenes show Bālā near the throne, or riding out to battle
Carnatic musicCompositions address Bālāmbikā and Bālā Tripurasundarī
TodayCalendar prints show a bright, smiling girl in a crown
ContrastUnlike most war goddesses, she is rarely shown fierce

ZAPPY: A war-winner who's never painted angry?

PROF. BOLT: That's her charm. She wins smiling. The fierceness is all in the deed, none in the face.

Painters have always enjoyed Bālā because she lets them combine royal splendour with childlike charm: a tiny figure in a full crown and ornaments, seated on a lotus, often with a serene, slightly mischievous smile.

In court scenes of the Lalitopākhyāna, Bālā appears near the throne or riding to battle in a small chariot. These paintings are among the few places where the war is shown in pictures, and her figure is easy to pick out: the smallest warrior in the scene.

Several Carnatic compositions address Bālāmbikā, and devotional songs to Bālā exist in Tamil, Telugu and Kannada. Their tone is often tender, like songs to the child Kṛṣṇa.

Modern artists have taken up Bālā too, in children’s books and illustrated stories of the Lalitopākhyāna. Her war against Bhaṇḍa’s sons is one of the few episodes of the story that is regularly retold for young readers, as a tale of a brave girl who would not be held back.

WHAT SHE MEANS

The power of beginnings
BeginningsEvery path starts with a beginner; she blesses that first step
InnocencePurity of heart as strength, not weakness
CourageSmallness is no excuse: she volunteers for the hardest fight
LearningBook and rosary: study and steady practice
Inner readingThe first awakening of devotion in the heart, small but unstoppable

ZAPPY: So she's about starting things?

PROF. BOLT: Starting things and finishing a war. The first spark that, given a chance, burns down thirty demons.

Bālā’s chief lesson may be about small beginnings. Most great things start small: a seed, a first lesson, a first prayer. The tradition makes that small beginning a goddess and gives her a victory that the great generals could not win.

Her innocence is not naivety. Bālā knows what she is fighting and insists on fighting it. The innocence the tradition prizes is a clear, undivided heart, which acts without the hesitations of a more complicated mind.

Inwardly, many teachers read her as the first stirring of devotion. It looks small and fragile, but given room to grow, it can overcome the whole brood of restless desires that Bhaṇḍa’s sons represent.

There is also a lesson for parents in Lalitā’s hesitation. The mother wants to protect her daughter; the daughter asks to be trusted. Lalitā lets her go, and the trust is rewarded. The story asks parents to see strength in their children, and to give it room.

WHAT BĀLĀ IS NOT

Clearing up mix-ups
Not a lesser goddessThe tradition counts her as the full Goddess in a young form
Not only for childrenAdults pray to her for fresh starts, learning and courage
Not a shortcutHer practice, like all Śrīvidyā practice, is learned from a teacher
Not a separate cultShe belongs within the worship of Lalitā
Not decorationHer war story is part of the core narrative, not a side tale

ZAPPY: Some people say her mantra is easy, so anyone can do it…

PROF. BOLT: The tradition says otherwise: it comes from a teacher, like every vidyā. Her names and her story are open to everyone.

Because Bālā is often the first form a Śrīvidyā student meets, she is sometimes presented online as an easy, do-it-yourself deity. Lineages reject this: her vidyā, like all vidyās in the tradition, is received from a teacher.

She is also sometimes treated as a minor, decorative goddess. In the texts and in worship she is the full Goddess, honoured on the Śrīcakra, given her own name in the Sahasranāma and credited with a decisive victory in the war.

What is open to everyone is everything else: her story, her image, her names, the kanyā pūjā of Navarātri, and the simple affection of a household that keeps her picture and offers her a lamp.

A last point: the worship of young girls at Navarātri is about honour and care. Devotional writers and teachers insist that a society which honours girls as the Goddess for a day must also protect, educate and respect them every day. That reading of kanyā pūjā is now widely preached in temples and homes.
