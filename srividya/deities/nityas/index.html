THE TITHI NITYĀS

Fifteen goddesses of the moon, and the sixteenth

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · THE TITHI NITYĀS

FOREWORD

After the Śrīcakra, we go to the goddesses who sit closest to its centre. The fifteen Tithi Nityās line the innermost triangle, and each is a day of the moon. Lalitā herself is the sixteenth: the digit that never wanes.

In this overview we meet all fifteen at once: their names, their days, their forms, the night they won a battle together, and the fire wall one of them became. Then each has a guide of her own.

Śrī Lalitāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, are there goddesses for days of the week?

PROF. BOLT: Not quite. Śrīvidyā has goddesses for days of the moon. Fifteen of them, and their queen.

ZAPPY: Fifteen goddesses for fifteen days?

PROF. BOLT: One for each day the moon grows, Zappy. And the sixteenth, who never shrinks, is Lalitā herself.

ZAPPY: Why does the list stop at fifteen and then add one?

PROF. BOLT: Because the moon has fifteen days to grow, but sixteen digits. The last one is never lost. That one is her.

Several of the names also appear in other settings. Śivadūtī is a warrior goddess of the Devī Māhātmya; Sarvamaṅgalā is a familiar name of Pārvatī; Jvālāmālinī is also the name of a Jain guardian goddess with a famous shrine in Karnataka; and Vijayā is a common name of Durgā. Each guide in this series notes where a shared name points to a different figure.

Read down the list and the meanings already tell a story. The first names are about desire and fortune (Kāmeśvarī, Bhagamālinī, Nityaklinnā); the middle ones are fierce and swift (Bheruṇḍā, Vahnivāsinī, Śivadūtī, Tvaritā); the later ones are about learning, banners and victory (Kulasundarī, Nīlapatākā, Vijayā); and the cycle closes in auspiciousness, flame and many colours (Sarvamaṅgalā, Jvālāmālinī, Citrā). This is our observation, not a teaching of the texts, but it is a useful way to hold fifteen names in mind.

The list is fixed in the Tantric sources and is repeated almost word for word in the Lalitopākhyāna, which calls the same goddesses Kāmeśī, Bhagamālā, Vahnivāsā and so on, using shorter forms of their names. The tenth is sometimes written Nityanityā, “eternal of the eternal”, to tell her apart from the group name.

THE MOON AND TIME

Why lunar days?
TithiThe time the moon takes to gain twelve degrees on the sun
FortnightFifteen tithis wax to the full moon; fifteen wane to the new
Sixteen digitsOld Indian thought gives the moon sixteen kalās; fifteen come and go, one remains
UpaniṣadThe Bṛhadāraṇyaka (1.5.14) calls the year a being of sixteen digits, fifteen of which are the nights
The NityāsEach Nityā is one waxing or waning digit; Lalitā is the sixteenth
KālacakraTogether they are called the wheel of time

ZAPPY: So when the moon disappears on new-moon night…

PROF. BOLT: The Upaniṣad says the sixteenth digit isn’t gone. It goes into every living thing and comes back. Śrīvidyā calls that digit Lalitā.

Indian calendars still reckon festivals by tithi rather than by solar date. Ekādaśī fasts, Caturthī for Gaṇeśa, Aṣṭamī and Navamī in Navarātri, and the full-moon and new-moon observances all follow the moon. For a Śrīvidyā devotee every one of those days also has its Nityā: the eleventh is Nīlapatākā’s, the fourth Bheruṇḍā’s, and so on. The calendar on the kitchen wall becomes, in this reading, a list of goddesses.

The Upaniṣadic passage is older than Śrīvidyā by many centuries and says nothing about goddesses. Śrīvidyā writers took up its picture of a sixteenth digit that survives the new moon and gave that digit a name: Lalitā. The fifteen visible digits became her attendants, the Nityās.

TIME IN THE BODY

Breaths, vowels and arms
BreathsA day and night are said to hold 21,600 breaths; each Nityā rules 1,440
VowelsSanskrit has sixteen vowels; each Nityā is linked with one, in order, and Lalitā with the last
ArmsThe arms of all the Nityās are said to total 108, a sacred number
SpaceOne text places Lalitā on Mount Meru and fourteen Nityās in the seven islands and seven oceans of Purāṇic geography
One ideaTime, sound, space and the body are mapped onto one another, with the Goddess at the centre

ZAPPY: Why does a breath count matter?

PROF. BOLT: Because it brings the moon down to you. The same rhythm that runs the month runs your lungs, and the Nityās are both.

The 21,600 figure is old. The Haṃsa or Ajapā teaching, found in several yoga and Tantric texts, says a person breathes about 21,600 times between one sunrise and the next, and that each breath silently repeats “so’ham”, “I am That”. Dividing that day by the fifteen Nityās gives 1,440 breaths each, roughly one and a half hours of ordinary breathing. The modern physiological figure is close: a resting adult takes about twelve to sixteen breaths a minute.

Gopinath Kaviraj, one of the great Tantric scholars of the twentieth century, summarised the system in his introduction to the Yoginīhṛdaya: the fifteen digits of the moon, the fifteen lunar days and the fifteen Nityās are one series, and the Goddess is their source. The numbers of breaths and arms are part of the same effort to show that the small and the great follow the same pattern.

ZAPPY: They’re so different! Some warlike, some like Sarasvatī…

PROF. BOLT: That’s the point. Each day has a different temper. Together they show every mood of the Goddess.

Notice how often Lalitā’s own weapons return: the noose, the goad, the sugarcane bow and the flower arrows are held by Kāmeśvarī, Bhagamālinī, Mahāvajreśvarī and Nityā, and partly by several others. Notice too the exceptions. Bheruṇḍā is armed for war; Kulasundarī carries a book and a pen; Tvaritā dresses like a forest dweller; Sarvamaṅgalā has only two hands. The variety is deliberate: the Goddess appears in every temperament, from the warrior to the scholar.

A dhyāna is not a portrait in the modern sense. Each item a goddess holds carries a meaning, and the meditator builds the image piece by piece. Lalitā’s own four weapons, the noose, goad, sugarcane bow and flower arrows, recur in many of the Nityās, a reminder that they are her rays.

THE NIGHT BATTLE

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 25
SettingAfter the first day of war, Bhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga leads a silent night attack on the rear
DangerLalitā’s royal fan is shattered by an arrow; her guard goddesses are shaken
The NityāsThe Tithi Nityās bow to Lalitā and ask leave to fight; she agrees
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light the dark field so the enemy can be seen
LeaderKāmeśvarī leads all fifteen, bows drawn, roaring “like lions”
LengthThree watches of the night; fifteen generals and their armies fall
ViṣaṅgaKāmeśvarī’s arrows split his armour; he flees, for his death is kept for another
DawnLalitā heals her wounded Nityās with a single glance

ZAPPY: Why couldn’t they kill Viṣaṅga himself?

PROF. BOLT: The story says his death was reserved for a particular goddess’s arrow. In the Purāṇa that’s Daṇḍanāthā, Vārāhī. The Sahasranāma gives it to Mantriṇī. Both versions are told.

The scene is carefully staged. The attackers move silently, with no drums, under cover of darkness, and they reach the rear of Lalitā’s own chariot before anyone notices. The Nityās’ first act is to ask Lalitā’s leave, a courtly touch that runs through the whole Lalitopākhyāna: even in an emergency, her generals do nothing without her word. Their second act is to make light, because an enemy who cannot be seen cannot be fought.

The night battle is the Nityās’ great scene in the Purāṇa. It is told in chapter 25 of the Lalitopākhyāna, between the first day’s fighting and the battles with Bhaṇḍa’s sons. It is also the source of the Sahasranāma name in which Lalitā is “eager to watch the valour of the Nityās”.

ZAPPY: Two Nityās share one enemy twice?

PROF. BOLT: Yes: Nityaklinnā and Bheruṇḍā take on two generals together; Nīlapatākā and Vijayā share one. The story isn’t a neat fifteen-for-fifteen.

For readers who like patterns, the pairings are worth noticing. The third and fourth Nityās fight together; so do the eleventh and twelfth. The ninth, Kulasundarī, takes two generals alone. The tenth has no named victim in our translation, and one general, Jambukākṣa, has no named end. Rather than force a tidy match we report the text as it stands.

The names of the generals are obscure and the manuscripts disagree on several of them. We follow G. V. Tagare’s translation and give his variant readings in brackets in each Nityā’s guide. None of these generals appears elsewhere in the story.

THE FIRE RAMPART

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 26
WhyAfter the night attack, Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā urge a protected camp
OrderLalitā summons Jvālāmālinī, the Nityā of the fourteenth day
The wallA ring of flame a hundred yojanas round and thirty high
The gapA single opening one yojana wide
ImageThe text likens it to a garland of fire, or of ketakī flowers, circling the sky
SahasranāmaLalitā is “she who stands within the fire-rampart thrown up by Jvālāmālinī”

ZAPPY: A wall made of a goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Jvālāmālinī becomes the wall. Her name means ‘garlanded with flames’, and here she is the garland.

A yojana is a Purāṇic unit of distance, usually reckoned at several miles, so the numbers are meant to astonish rather than to be measured. What matters is the picture: a circle of living flame, tall enough to touch the sky, with the Goddess and her army safe inside it.

Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā propose the wall; Lalitā gives the order; Jvālāmālinī carries it out. That chain of counsel, command and action is the Lalitopākhyāna’s picture of a well-run kingdom. The army then rests inside the ring of fire and, when ready, marches out through the single opening to the battles with Bhaṇḍa’s sons, where Bālā, Lalitā’s nine-year-old daughter, takes the field.

The Purāṇa itself ties Jvālāmālinī to her lunar day: it calls her the Nityā of the fourteenth day, Caturdaśī, when it describes the wall. It is one of the few places where the story and the calendar meet in a single sentence.

ZAPPY: So the Nityās are the last ring before the centre?

PROF. BOLT: On the yantra, yes. They’re the nearest company the Goddess keeps. Past them there is only her.

ZAPPY: Is the Tantrarāja easy to read?

PROF. BOLT: Not at all. It’s technical and full of mantras and rites. We take only the forms, the meanings and the ideas.

The Vāmakeśvara Tantra is usually treated as two works: the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava, which gives the ritual system and lends the Nityās their collective title, and the Yoginīhṛdaya, which gives its inner meaning. Bhāskararāya commented on the first in his Setubandha, and Amṛtānanda on the second in his Dīpikā.

The Tantrarāja was first printed in the Tantrik Texts series edited by Arthur Avalon (Sir John Woodroffe) with the Sanskrit editor Lakṣmaṇa Śāstrī, early in the twentieth century. Woodroffe’s English introduction remains the most accessible summary of its ideas about time, sound and the body, though it is a summary and not a translation.

Like the rest of this series, these guides describe; they do not teach practice. Each Nityā has her own mantra in the Tantric sources, and some of these are printed in modern books and on websites. We do not reproduce, assemble or explain any of them. They belong to the teacher and the lineage.

TWO FORTNIGHTS

Waxing and waning
Common viewKāmeśvarī is the first day of the bright fortnight, Citrā the full moon
ReversalMany say the order runs backwards in the dark fortnight
Other viewsSome modern writers put Kāmeśvarī first in the dark fortnight instead
NityotsavaThis ritual manual lays down both an ascending and a descending order
Our stanceWe number them 1 to 15 as the texts list them, and show both views

ZAPPY: Which one’s right?

PROF. BOLT: Both are taught by living lineages. We don’t pick. The list order itself is the same everywhere.

A practical note: because a tithi does not match a solar day, the Nityā of a given calendar date may change during the day. Almanacs (pañcāṅgas) list the hour at which each tithi begins and ends, and devotees who follow the Nityās use those times.

Whichever order a lineage follows, every account agrees on two things: there are fifteen Nityās who come and go with the moon, and the sixteenth, Lalitā, does not. Modern devotional calendars and websites often print a “Nityā of the day”; when they disagree, it is usually because they follow different fortnight orders.

The difference matters only to those who worship the Nityās day by day. For a reader, the more interesting point is the image itself: the Goddess’s companions arriving one by one as the moon fills, and leaving one by one as it wanes, while she alone remains.

THE INNER READING

The month as a garland
ChangeThe fifteen are what changes: moods, days, breaths, desires
ChangelessThe sixteenth is what never changes: awareness itself
Not oppositesThe changing digits are rays of the changeless moon
Every dayAny day of the month is a door to the same Goddess
Our noteReadings like these come from commentators; texts frame them differently

ZAPPY: So each day is a different face of one person?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the reading. Fifteen faces, one face behind them.

Commentators also read the fifteen as stages of the mind: desire first stirs (Kāmeśvarī), gathers fortune and feeling, meets fear and fire, grows swift and clear, turns to knowledge and victory, and comes at last to the full moon of Citrā, all colours at once. Beyond the full moon is the sixteenth, which has no stage at all.

This also explains why the Nityās are called “eternal” at all. A lunar day lasts a day; how can its goddess be eternal? The answer the tradition gives is that the day passes but the power that makes it a day does not. Each month, Kāmeśvarī returns on her day, as she always has, and will.

The changing and the changeless are not two places. The moon on any night is the same moon, only lit differently. In the same way, the commentators say, each Nityā is the whole Goddess seen through one day of time.

HOW TO READ THIS SERIES

Each guideEnds with links to the Nityā before and after her
What we leave outEvery Nityā has a mantra and rites; we give none of them

ZAPPY: Can I read them in any order?

PROF. BOLT: Sure. But reading them in moon order is fun: start at the new moon and do one a night.

If you are new to Śrīvidyā, it helps to read the Lalitopākhyāna guides first, especially Part 4, which tells the first day of the war, and the Śrīcakra guides, especially the eighth enclosure. The Nityā guides lean on both.

Each Nityā’s guide follows the same plan: at a glance, the name, her form from the meditation verse, her lunar day, her part in the night battle, her place on the triangle, the texts, one or two pages special to her, what she means, a glossary, a one-page summary and the sources.

The guides are written so that each stands alone. Facts repeated in every guide, such as the shape of the night battle, are kept short, and each guide spends most of its pages on what is particular to its Nityā.

Where the facts come from

Sources differ / unverified: Descriptions of each Nityā’s form differ between the Tantrarāja and the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, and between modern summaries. The tradition says the arms of all fifteen total 108, but the counts in individual meditation verses as summarised do not add up exactly. Whether Kāmeśvarī belongs to the first day of the bright fortnight or of the dark one, and whether the order reverses in the other fortnight, varies by lineage and writer. Which corner of the central triangle the row of Nityās starts from also varies. Names of the demon generals follow Tagare, with his variant readings.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
