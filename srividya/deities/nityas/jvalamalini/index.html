JVĀLĀMĀLINĪ

“She who is garlanded with flames”

THE TITHI NITYĀS · 14 OF 15

FOREWORD

The fourteenth Nityā has the biggest role in the story of any of the fifteen. Jvālāmālinī, “garlanded with flames”, helps light the dark battlefield, kills a general, and the next day becomes, at Lalitā’s command, a wall of fire round the whole camp. The Lalitā Sahasranāma remembers that wall in one of Lalitā’s own names.

Her body is fire itself; she has six faces and twelve arms and sits on a lion. Readers in Karnataka will know another Jvālāmālinī, the Jain guardian goddess of Narasimharajapura. We look at both, and keep them apart.

Śrī Jvālāmālinyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, which Nityā turned into a wall?

PROF. BOLT: Jvālāmālinī, Zappy. A hundred yojanas of flame round Lalitā’s camp.

THE NAME

“She who is garlanded with flames”
JvālāFlame, blaze
MālinīGarlanded, wearing a garland
Together“She who is garlanded with flames”
In the SahasranāmaLalitā is “she who stands within the fire-rampart thrown up by Jvālāmālinī”
Short formJvālāmālinikā in the Sahasranāma

ZAPPY: She’s in the Sahasranāma by name?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, inside one of Lalitā’s names. Very few Nityās get that honour.

Jvālā is a blaze or flame; mālinī, as in Bhagamālinī, means one who wears a garland. A garland of flames can be pictured two ways: flames worn round her neck, or a circle of flame round something she protects. Chapter 26 of the Lalitopākhyāna gives her the second meaning, and the Sahasranāma keeps it.

The Sahasranāma name in full means “she who is in the middle of the fire-rampart thrown up by Jvālāmālinikā”. It stands in a sequence of names that retell the war, just before the name in which Lalitā delights in the valour of her Śaktis and the one in which she is eager to watch the valour of the Nityās.

Every Nityā’s name can also be read as a name of Lalitā herself, and several of them appear in her hymns as her own titles. The tradition does not see this as confusion: each Nityā is one ray of the queen, so the ray and the source may share a name.

HER FORM

From the meditation verse
ColourA body of blazing fire
Arms and eyesTwelve arms; six faces, each with three eyes
HoldsNoose, goad, arrow, mace, tortoise, spear and flame; the gestures of giving and of fearlessness
Seat or settingA lion throne
AlsoSurrounded by attendant goddesses like herself

ZAPPY: Six faces and twelve arms, all on fire.

PROF. BOLT: The most dramatic of the fifteen. And seated on a lion, like Durgā.

Her body is described as made of flame. With six faces, each three-eyed, and twelve arms, she is the most dramatic figure of the fifteen, and her lion throne recalls Durgā, who rides a lion. Most of what she holds is martial: noose, goad, arrow, mace, spear and a flame.

The tortoise is the surprise. In Indian imagery the tortoise is steady and withdrawn: the Bhagavad Gītā compares the wise person, who draws the senses in from their objects, to a tortoise drawing in its limbs. A tortoise in the hand of a fire goddess may be read as calm at the heart of the blaze. That reading is ours; the verse simply lists it.

Meditation verses (dhyānas) are meant to be pictured in detail, item by item, and the colours, hands and seats differ slightly between texts. We give the form as summarised from the Tantrarāja tradition, and note where another text differs.

ZAPPY: Day fourteen, Caturdaśī.

PROF. BOLT: The night before the full moon. And the Purāṇa itself calls her the Nityā of the fourteenth day.

Caturdaśī, the fourteenth day, is the night before the full moon in the bright fortnight and before the new moon in the dark one. Mahāśivarātri falls on a dark-fortnight Caturdaśī, and Naraka Caturdaśī begins Dīpāvalī in the south with lamps and early baths. A fire goddess suits a day of lamps.

Indian calendars still run on tithis. A tithi is the time the moon takes to gain twelve degrees on the sun, so it can be longer or shorter than a solar day and may begin at any hour. That is why festival dates move from year to year, and why a tithi named in a calendar may start in the afternoon.

IN THE NIGHT BATTLE

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 25
The attackBhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga creeps round to strike Lalitā’s army from the rear after dark
The NityāsAll fifteen ask Lalitā’s leave, dismount, and fight under Kāmeśvarī
DangerAn arrow shatters Lalitā’s royal fan; her guard goddesses are shaken
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light up the dark field
Her enemyTrikaṇṭaka (variant Trikarṇaka)
Her special taskWith Vahnivāsinī she lights the dark battlefield
The endAfter three watches of the night, Viṣaṅga flees; Lalitā heals the Nityās with one glance

ZAPPY: She lit the field and killed a general?

PROF. BOLT: And the next day became a wall of fire. Busiest Nityā in the story.

Her victim is Trikaṇṭaka (Tagare also reads Trikarṇaka), the fourteenth general in the list. Like Vahnivāsinī she fights after lighting the field: the fire goddesses are both lamp and weapon.

Her work does not end at dawn. The next chapter makes her the wall that keeps the camp safe from any further surprise attack. No other Nityā carries the story across two chapters.

The night battle is the only place in the Lalitopākhyāna where all fifteen Nityās fight together as a company. Earlier in the war they ride with Lalitā on the great chariot Cakrarāja; here they dismount, and the story gives each of them her own enemy, so the battle reads like a roll-call of the lunar days.

At the centreMahānityā, Lalitā herself, the sixteenth, at the bindu

ZAPPY: Fourteenth, nearly at the apex.

PROF. BOLT: Fourth on the left side. One more, Citrā, and the row returns to where Kāmeśvarī began.

She stands fourth on the left side of the triangle, close to the apex. Only Citrā, the full moon, comes after her before the row returns to its starting point beside Kāmeśvarī.

In Śrīvidyā thought time and space are mapped onto one another. The same fifteen goddesses who are the days of the fortnight are also placed on the innermost triangle of the Śrīcakra, so that the month itself is pictured as surrounding the Goddess at the centre.

ZAPPY: The story and the calendar agree?

PROF. BOLT: Here, in so many words. It’s a lovely moment: the Purāṇa remembers she is the fourteenth day.

The Sahasranāma’s version is compressed into a single name, and its commentators fill in the story from the Purāṇa. Because the hymn is recited daily by many devotees, Jvālāmālinī is probably the most often named of all the Nityās, even by people who could not list the other fourteen.

The Tantrarāja gives her a full meditation verse and yantra, like her sisters. Her fiery form there matches her role in the Purāṇa closely, which is not true of every Nityā: some have portraits with no story attached, and some, like the tenth, a place in the story with no deed.

Chapter 26 is the only place in the Purāṇa where a Nityā’s lunar day is mentioned in the story itself. When Jvālāmālinī takes up her task, the text describes her as the Nityā who presides over the fourteenth day. Calendar and story meet in a single verse.

A note on sources: the forms in this series come from modern summaries of the meditation verses, not from our own reading of the Sanskrit manuscripts, and the verses themselves differ between texts. Where two descriptions disagree we say so in the sources page at the end of each guide.

THE FIRE RAMPART

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 26
After the night attackMantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā urge Lalitā to protect the camp
The orderLalitā summons Jvālāmālinī and tells her to encircle the camp
SizeA hundred yojanas round, thirty yojanas high
One openingA single gap, one yojana wide
ImageLike a garland of fire, or of ketakī flowers, circling the sky
ThenThe army rests inside, and marches out through the gap to the next battles

ZAPPY: A goddess becomes a wall?

PROF. BOLT: She takes the form of flame and stands round the camp. Her name, ‘garlanded with flames’, becomes the camp’s garland.

The counsel comes from Lalitā’s two great ministers. Mantriṇī, the queen’s chief counsellor, and Daṇḍanāthā, her commander, advise a fortified camp after the surprise of the night attack. Lalitā agrees and calls Jvālāmālinī. The chain of counsel, command and action is the Purāṇa’s picture of good government.

The numbers are huge on purpose. A yojana is a Purāṇic distance of several miles; a ring a hundred yojanas round and thirty high is beyond any real fortress. The text likens it to a garland of fire, or of ketakī flowers, hanging in a circle in the sky.

The single opening matters too. A wall with no gate is a prison. Through the gap the army later marches out to the battles with Bhaṇḍa’s sons, where Bālā takes the field. Protection, in this story, is never meant to be permanent confinement.

THE JAIN JVĀLĀMĀLINĪ

Same name, another goddess
WhoThe yakṣī, or guardian goddess, of the eighth Tīrthaṅkara, Candraprabha, in the Digambara tradition
FormA fire goddess with flames rising from her crown; eight-armed, riding a buffalo
TextThe Jvālāmālinī-kalpa of Indranandi, composed in 939 CE at Mānyakheṭa under the Rāṣṭrakūṭas
KarnatakaOne of the most widely worshipped yakṣīs in early medieval Karnataka
TempleA well-known shrine at Narasimharajapura, Chikkamagaluru district
Our stanceA different goddess sharing a name and a fiery nature; we keep the two apart

ZAPPY: So Karnataka has two Jvālāmālinīs?

PROF. BOLT: Two goddesses, one name. The Jain guardian goddess is very much alive in Karnataka; the Nityā lives on the Śrīcakra.

The Jain Jvālāmālinī is the yakṣī, or guardian goddess, of the eighth Tīrthaṅkara, Candraprabha, in the Digambara tradition. She is a fire goddess, shown with flames rising from her crown, and was one of the most widely worshipped yakṣīs in early medieval Karnataka.

Her worship is described in the Jvālāmālinī-kalpa of Indranandi, written in 939 CE at Mānyakheṭa under the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Kṛṣṇa III. Indranandi traces the cult to an earlier teacher, Helācārya. Her best-known shrine today is at Narasimharajapura in Chikkamagaluru district.

The two goddesses share a name and a fiery nature, and both belong to Tantric traditions of worship. We have found no source that makes them one, and we do not suggest it. Readers who visit the Jain shrine are meeting the Jain Jvālāmālinī.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Reading her form and name
FlamePurifying fire that burns away what is false
Wall of fireProtection no enemy can cross
TortoiseSteadiness and withdrawal: the cool centre of the fire
Lion seatCourage
Fourteenth dayThe last darkness before the full moon

ZAPPY: Why is the fire goddess so near the end?

PROF. BOLT: Our reading: before the full moon, everything left over is burned away. Then only light remains.

Commentators who read the fifteen as stages place the purifying fire here. On the day before the full moon, whatever still stands between the devotee and the Goddess is burned away. What remains is the full light of Citrā, and beyond her Lalitā.

Her wall of fire adds a second meaning. Fire that purifies also protects. The devotee who has come this far is surrounded by the Goddess as the camp was surrounded by Jvālāmālinī: safe, with one door left open.

Readings like these are offered as ways in, not as doctrine. Śrīvidyā commentators interpret the Nityās at several levels at once: as goddesses to be honoured, as days of the month, as powers within the body and as stages of awareness. A reader can take whichever level helps.
