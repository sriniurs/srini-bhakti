ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

MAHĀNITYĀ

Lalitā, the digit that never wanes

THE TITHI NITYĀS · THE SIXTEENTH

FOREWORD

Fifteen guides have introduced the fifteen Tithi Nityās, one for each day of the waxing moon. This last guide is about the sixteenth, who is not a sixteenth day at all. Mahānityā, “the great eternal one”, is Lalitā herself: the digit of the moon that never waxes or wanes.

She sits at the bindu of the Śrīcakra with the fifteen around her, watches their valour in battle, and heals them with a glance. Her other name, Ṣoḍaśī, “she who is sixteen”, also makes her one of the ten Mahāvidyās.

Śrī Lalitāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

PROF. BOLT: There is, Zappy, and you’ve met her already. She’s the queen.

ZAPPY: So the sixteenth Nityā is… the queen?

PROF. BOLT: The queen, Zappy. The fifteen were her digits all along.

This last guide in the Nityā series turns from the fifteen to the one at their centre. Everything in the earlier guides points here: each Nityā was a ray, and this is the source of the light.

THE NAMES

Mahānityā, Ṣoḍaśī, Mahātripurasundarī
Mahānityā“The great eternal one”: greater than the fifteen eternal ones
Ṣoḍaśī“She who is sixteen”: the sixteenth digit; also read as ever sixteen years old, ever young
Mahātripurasundarī“Great beauty of the three cities”, her name at the centre of the Śrīcakra
NityāṣoḍaśikārūpāA Sahasranāma name: “whose form is the sixteen Nityās”
Lalitā“She who plays”: the name most devotees use
A noteṢoḍaśī is also the name of an initiatory mantra; we give the name’s meaning only

ZAPPY: Why ‘sixteen’ for a goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Two reasons: the sixteenth digit of the moon, and eternal youth. Sixteen was the classic age of youthful beauty in Indian poetry.

Mahātripurasundarī is her name at the bindu in the ninth enclosure, where the presiding goddesses of the eight outer enclosures, from Tripurā to Tripurāmbā, give way to her. All her titles meet at the centre: queen of the three cities, the great eternal one, the sixteenth.

Ṣoḍaśī is the name most likely to cause confusion. It is a name of the Goddess, one of the ten Mahāvidyās; it is also the name of the most closely guarded mantra in Śrīvidyā, given only by a teacher. This guide explains the name and does not touch the mantra.

Mahānityā is the simplest of her titles here. All fifteen are nityā, eternal, because their days return forever. She is mahā-nityā, the great eternal one, because she does not even come and go.

THE SIXTEENTH DIGIT

The moon that never wanes
Sixteen kalāsIndian thought gives the moon sixteen digits
FifteenCome and go: one a day, waxing and waning
OneThe sixteenth never changes; it is what remains when the moon seems gone
UpaniṣadThe Bṛhadāraṇyaka (1.5.14) speaks of a sixteenth digit that is constant
SādākhyāTantric sources call the sixteenth kalā Sādākhyā, and identify it with Lalitā
ŚrīvidyāThe fifteen are the Nityās; the sixteenth is Mahānityā

ZAPPY: Where does the moon go at new moon, then?

PROF. BOLT: The Upaniṣad says that sixteenth digit enters every living thing that night, and is reborn next morning. The moon isn’t gone; it’s in you.

There is a simple observation behind the picture. Even on the darkest night, people know the moon has not been destroyed; it will return. Something about the moon persists through every phase. The sixteenth digit is the name for that persistence, and Śrīvidyā sees the Goddess in it.

The idea of a sixteenth digit is older than Śrīvidyā. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad describes the year, pictured as the creator Prajāpati, as having sixteen digits: the fifteen nights are fifteen of them, and the sixteenth is constant. Śrīvidyā took up this picture and gave it a face.

Tantric sources quoted by Gopinath Kaviraj and others call this sixteenth kalā Sādākhyā and identify it with Lalitā. The fifteen are the Nityās from Kāmeśvarī to Citrā; the sixteenth is the Goddess herself.

THE NEW MOON

Where the sixteenth goes
AmāvāsyāThe night with no visible moon
The UpaniṣadOn that night the year’s sixteenth digit enters all that breathes, and is born again at dawn
Its adviceThe Upaniṣad asks that no creature be harmed that night, out of respect for that digit
Śrīvidyā readingThe Goddess is never absent: when no Nityā shines, she remains
Our noteThe Upaniṣad does not mention Lalitā; Śrīvidyā applies its image to her

ZAPPY: That’s beautiful. The moon hides inside everyone.

PROF. BOLT: That’s the picture. And for Śrīvidyā, what hides there is the Goddess herself.

The full moon and the new moon thus frame the Nityā cycle. At the full moon all fifteen shine and Citrā presides; at the new moon none shines, and only the sixteenth remains, hidden in every living thing. Either way, the Goddess is the constant.

The Upaniṣad’s image is tender. On the new-moon night the sixteenth digit enters every living thing, and from it the moon is born again the next morning. Out of respect, the text asks that on that night no one should take the life of any creature, not even a lizard.

Śrīvidyā reads this as a statement about the Goddess. When every Nityā has waned and no light shows, she has not gone. She has withdrawn into all living beings and will rise again. Amāvāsyā is therefore not an empty night for the devotee; many lineages hold it sacred.

AT THE CENTRE

The bindu of the Śrīcakra
BinduThe central point, the ninth enclosure, Sarvānandamaya, “made of all bliss”
Around herThe fifteen Nityās on the central triangle, five to a side
With herKāmeśvara: in the bindu the two are one point
CornersMahākāmeśvarī, Mahāvajreśvarī and Mahābhagamālinī at the triangle’s corners
The imageThe whole month surrounds the Goddess; she is its still centre

ZAPPY: So the triangle is like a clock face round her?

PROF. BOLT: A good picture: fifteen marks for fifteen days, and the hand that never moves in the middle.

The guide to the ninth enclosure in this series describes the bindu more fully. Here it is enough to see the arrangement as a whole: the bindu, the triangle around it with its three corner goddesses, and the fifteen Nityās along its sides. Time surrounds the timeless at the very heart of the yantra.

In the usual arrangement the row begins beside the triangle’s apex with Kāmeśvarī and runs anticlockwise, five to a side, ending with Citrā beside the same apex. The circuit closes on itself, as the month does.

THE FIFTEEN AS HER RAYS

One goddess, sixteen digits
Each NityāOne ray, one day, one face of the queen
KāmeśvarīThe first and closest copy: Lalitā’s own four weapons
The many formsWarrior, scholar, forest goddess, standard-bearer: every temperament of one Goddess
Shared weaponsNoose, goad, bow and arrows recur through the cycle
NamesSeveral Nityā names are also names of Lalitā in her hymns
VāmakeśvaraIts first part is called the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava, “ocean of the sixteen Nityās”

ZAPPY: So when I honour any Nityā…

PROF. BOLT: …you’re honouring Lalitā through one window. That’s the tradition’s view.

A ray of light is not a separate thing from the sun; it is the sun reaching outward. In the same way, the tradition says, each Nityā is Lalitā reaching into one day of time. To meet a Nityā is to meet Lalitā in that day.

The individual guides showed how varied the fifteen are. Kāmeśvarī and Mahāvajreśvarī hold Lalitā’s own four weapons; Bheruṇḍā is armed for war; Tvaritā wears leaves and serpents; Kulasundarī carries a book and a golden pen; Sarvamaṅgalā has only two hands. The variety is the point: every temperament is a ray of one Goddess.

The Sahasranāma name Nityāṣoḍaśikārūpā sums it up: Lalitā is “she whose form is the sixteen Nityās”. The fifteen and the one together are her whole form; take away any one and the picture is incomplete.

IN THE STORY

The queen and her Nityās
BeforeThe Nityās ride with her on the chariot Cakrarāja
The night attackHer fan is shattered; she grows angry
PermissionThe Nityās bow and ask leave to fight; she grants it
WatchingThe Sahasranāma: she is “eager to see the valour of the Nityās”
AfterThey return bloodied; she heals them with a single compassionate glance
Next dayShe orders Jvālāmālinī to become the fire rampart

ZAPPY: She didn’t fight herself?

PROF. BOLT: Not that night. She let her digits shine. A queen who trusts her own rays.

It is the only moment in the war where the Nityās act as a group, and the only one in which Lalitā is shown simply watching her companions with pride. The scene gives the cycle of time a heart: the queen’s pleasure in her own rays.

The Lalitopākhyāna gives Lalitā a quiet role in the night battle. She grows angry when the attack reaches her chariot, but it is the Nityās who fight. She grants their request and then watches. The Sahasranāma turns this into a name: she is eager to behold the valour of the Nityās.

Her healing glance at dawn is one of the gentlest moments in the war. The Nityās come back wounded and covered in blood; she looks at them once with compassion and their wounds are gone. The queen does not need to touch them: her look is enough.

ṢOḌAŚĪ AMONG THE MAHĀVIDYĀS

A wider family
MahāvidyāsTen great wisdom goddesses: Kālī, Tārā, Ṣoḍaśī, Bhuvaneśvarī and others
ṢoḍaśīThe third in the usual list; identified with Tripurasundarī
Same goddessŚrīvidyā and the Mahāvidyā tradition agree that Ṣoḍaśī is Lalitā
Different settingsThe Mahāvidyās have their own texts and worship
Our approachWe describe the family; we do not treat its practices

ZAPPY: So Lalitā shows up in the ten Mahāvidyās too?

PROF. BOLT: As Ṣoḍaśī. Different family portrait, same face.

In iconography the Mahāvidyā Ṣoḍaśī is often shown seated on a couch whose legs are gods and whose plank is Śiva, much as Lalitā is described on her throne in Śrīpura. The two descriptions grew in different traditions but clearly picture the same queen.

The ten Mahāvidyās are a family of goddesses worshipped in many Śākta traditions, especially in eastern India: Kālī, Tārā, Ṣoḍaśī, Bhuvaneśvarī, Bhairavī, Chinnamastā, Dhūmāvatī, Bagalāmukhī, Mātaṅgī and Kamalā. Ṣoḍaśī, often called Tripurasundarī there, is the same goddess Śrīvidyā calls Lalitā.

Mātaṅgī in the same list is closely related to Rājaśyāmalā, Lalitā’s minister, who has her own guide in this series. The overlap shows how the great Śākta traditions share their goddesses while keeping their own texts and ways of worship.

WITH KĀMEŚVARA

Śiva and Śakti in the bindu
KāmeśvaraŚiva as the lord of desire, Lalitā’s husband
The binduThe two are pictured as one point
KāmeśvarīThe first Nityā shares his name in the feminine
The circleFrom Kāmeśvarī at the edge to Kāmeśvara at the centre
MeaningTime (the Nityās) flows from the union of Śiva and Śakti and returns to it

ZAPPY: The first Nityā and the centre have the same name?

PROF. BOLT: Nearly. Kāmeśvarī on the first day, Kāmeśvara with Lalitā at the bindu. The road starts and ends with that name.

In the overview to this series we noted that the first Nityā’s name and the consort’s name are the same word in two genders. The journey through the fifteen begins with Kāmeśvarī at the edge of the triangle and ends with Kāmeśvara and Lalitā at its centre.

Kāmeśvara and Lalitā are never really separate in Śrīvidyā. The guide to Kāmeśvara in this series tells his story; the guide to the bindu shows the two as one point. The Nityās, which begin with Kāmeśvarī, can be read as the time that flows from that union and returns to it.

The Sahasranāma calls Lalitā the queen of the great Kāmeśa, and places her on his lap. Mahānityā is not alone at the centre; the sixteenth digit is the union of Śiva and Śakti seen as light.

THE INNER READING

The changeless witness
FifteenChanging states: moods, days, breaths, thoughts
SixteenthWhat is aware of all of them, and does not change
Not separateThe changing states are her rays, not her rivals
PracticeMany lineages honour the Nityās daily and the sixteenth always
Our noteThis is the commentators’ reading, in plain words

ZAPPY: So I’m the moon, and my moods are the phases?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the reading. The phases come and go. The moon doesn’t.

Readers who began with the first guide in the Śrīvidyā series, What is Śrīvidyā, will recognise the destination. The whole tradition, with its stories, yantra, deities and days of the moon, is a way of approaching that one centre from every side.

Commentators often turn the system inward. The fifteen Nityās are the states that pass through us every day: wanting, having, softening, guarding, seeing, steadying, acting, hurrying, knowing, living in the body, rallying, winning, blessing, burning away, and fullness. The sixteenth is the awareness in which they all appear.

That awareness does not wax or wane. On good days and bad, at the full moon and the new, it is there. To recognise it, the commentators say, is to know Mahānityā. This is the end of the Nityā series, and in a sense the end of every Śrīvidyā series: the Goddess at the centre, unchanged.

Where the facts come from

Sources: Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna chs. 25–26 (G. V. Tagare’s translation, Motilal Banarsidass; the night battle and the fire rampart); the Tantrarāja Tantra (ed. Sources differ / unverified: Descriptions of each Nityā’s form differ between the Tantrarāja and the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, and between modern summaries. The tradition says the arms of all fifteen total 108, but the counts in individual meditation verses as summarised do not add up exactly. Whether Kāmeśvarī belongs to the first day of the bright fortnight or of the dark one, and whether the order reverses in the other fortnight, varies by lineage and writer. Which corner of the central triangle the row of Nityās starts from also varies. Names of the demon generals follow Tagare, with his variant readings. Whether Ṣoḍaśī as a name refers first to the sixteenth digit or to eternal youth is explained differently by different writers; both are given here.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
