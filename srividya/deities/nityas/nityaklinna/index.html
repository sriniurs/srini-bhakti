NITYAKLINNĀ

“The ever-moist one”

THE TITHI NITYĀS · 3 OF 15

FOREWORD

The third Nityā has a name that puzzles at first. Nityaklinnā means “ever-moist” or “always melted”, and in the language of devotion that is high praise: a heart that never dries, a compassion that never runs out.

She is red, crowned with a half-moon, and holds a noose, a goad and a cup, with one hand raised to remove fear. In the night battle she fights shoulder to shoulder with Bheruṇḍā, the fourth Nityā.

Śrī Nityaklinnāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, can a goddess be described as ‘wet’?

PROF. BOLT: In Sanskrit, Zappy, a ‘melted’ heart is the best kind. Hers stays melted forever.

ZAPPY: ‘Ever-moist’? That’s an odd name.

PROF. BOLT: Odd until you hear what’s moist: a heart that never dries out. Compassion that never stops flowing.

THE NAME

“The ever-moist one”
NityaAlways, eternal
KlinnāMoist, wet, melted, softened
Together“The ever-moist one”
Devotional readingHer heart is always melted with compassion; it never hardens
Older readingMoist with love and longing: the Goddess who is always tender towards Śiva and her devotees
In the Sahasranāma“Nityaklinnā” is also one of Lalitā’s own thousand names

ZAPPY: Her name’s in the Sahasranāma too?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, right next to ‘Nityāṣoḍaśikārūpā’, ‘whose form is the sixteen Nityās’. The hymn knows the Nityās well.

Klinna comes from a root meaning to be wet or to become soft. In poetry it is used of eyes wet with tears, of earth softened by rain and of hearts softened by love. Nitya adds “always”. The name therefore describes a state that never changes: the Goddess’s tenderness is not a mood that comes and goes with the moon, though her day does.

Her name stands in the Lalitā Sahasranāma among names that describe the Goddess as beyond all limits, and the very next name calls Lalitā “she whose form is the sixteen Nityās”. The hymn thus places a Nityā’s name directly beside the doctrine of the Nityās, a small sign of how closely the two belong together.

Every Nityā’s name can also be read as a name of Lalitā herself, and several of them appear in her hymns as her own titles. The tradition does not see this as confusion: each Nityā is one ray of the queen, so the ray and the source may share a name.

HER FORM

From the meditation verse
ColourRed, anointed with red sandal paste, in red clothes
Arms and eyesFour hands, by what she holds
HoldsA noose, a goad and a cup; one hand shows the gesture that removes fear
Seat or settingNot specified in the summary we used
AlsoA half-moon on her head; “restless with love”; nineteen attendant goddesses around her

ZAPPY: No bow and arrows this time?

PROF. BOLT: Just noose, goad and cup, and a hand that says ‘don’t be afraid’. A gentler kit for a gentler Nityā.

Some Nityās are fierce and many-armed; Nityaklinnā is not. Her four hands hold the two tools of guidance that Lalitā also holds, a cup of nectar, and the abhaya gesture, the raised palm that says “do not fear”. The half-moon on her head is the moon of her own day, still young.

Her nineteen attendants are named in the Tantric sources; we do not list them. The number is one of many such details that show how fully each Nityā was imagined: not a single figure but a small court of her own.

Meditation verses (dhyānas) are meant to be pictured in detail, item by item, and the colours, hands and seats differ slightly between texts. We give the form as summarised from the Tantrarāja tradition, and note where another text differs.

BreathThe tradition gives each Nityā 1,440 of the 21,600 breaths of a day and night

ZAPPY: Day three!

PROF. BOLT: Tṛtīyā. The crescent is a clear bow in the evening sky now. Some of the best-loved fasts fall on this day.

Tṛtīyā is a much-loved day in the Hindu year. Akṣaya Tṛtīyā in spring is thought to make every good act imperishable, and Hartālikā Tṛtīyā and Gaurī Tṛtīyā honour the Goddess as Pārvatī. Those festivals have their own traditions, but a Śrīvidyā devotee will note that every Tṛtīyā is also Nityaklinnā’s.

Indian calendars still run on tithis. A tithi is the time the moon takes to gain twelve degrees on the sun, so it can be longer or shorter than a solar day and may begin at any hour. That is why festival dates move from year to year, and why a tithi named in a calendar may start in the afternoon.

IN THE NIGHT BATTLE

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 25
The attackBhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga creeps round to strike Lalitā’s army from the rear after dark
The NityāsAll fifteen ask Lalitā’s leave, dismount, and fight under Kāmeśvarī
DangerAn arrow shatters Lalitā’s royal fan; her guard goddesses are shaken
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light up the dark field
Her enemyHumbaka and Hulumulla, together with Bheruṇḍā
PartnerShe fights alongside Bheruṇḍā, the fourth Nityā
The endAfter three watches of the night, Viṣaṅga flees; Lalitā heals the Nityās with one glance

ZAPPY: Two Nityās against two generals?

PROF. BOLT: Humbaka and Hulumulla. The text doesn’t say who killed which. They shared the work.

It is worth remembering what the fight was for. The demons had crept up in darkness on Lalitā’s own chariot and wounded her guard. Even the gentlest Nityā takes the field when the queen is threatened: compassion in this tradition is not the same as passivity.

The two generals she fights, Humbaka and Hulumulla, are the third and fourth in the Purāṇa’s list, just as she and Bheruṇḍā are the third and fourth Nityās. The pairing is neat: the gentle Nityā fights beside the most heavily armed one.

The night battle is the only place in the Lalitopākhyāna where all fifteen Nityās fight together as a company. Earlier in the war they ride with Lalitā on the great chariot Cakrarāja; here they dismount, and the story gives each of them her own enemy, so the battle reads like a roll-call of the lunar days.

AMONG HER SISTERS

Her place on the Śrīcakra
On the yantraThe fifteen Nityās are placed on the innermost triangle, five to a side, starting beside the apex and running anticlockwise
Her sideIn the usual arrangement she falls on the right-hand side, rising from the apex
At the centreMahānityā, Lalitā herself, the sixteenth, at the bindu
Before herNityā 2, Bhagamālinī
After herNityā 4, Bheruṇḍā

ZAPPY: Third on the line.

PROF. BOLT: Third of five on the first side. Bheruṇḍā, her battle partner, stands right beside her.

On the central triangle she stands third along the first side, between Bhagamālinī and Bheruṇḍā. In worship that follows the days of the moon, she is honoured on the third day; in worship of the whole yantra, she is honoured in her place among the fifteen.

In Śrīvidyā thought time and space are mapped onto one another. The same fifteen goddesses who are the days of the fortnight are also placed on the innermost triangle of the Śrīcakra, so that the month itself is pictured as surrounding the Goddess at the centre.

ZAPPY: Did the Sahasranāma borrow her name?

PROF. BOLT: Or she borrowed Lalitā’s. In this tradition every Nityā’s name is ultimately a name of the queen.

The Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, a Śaiva text that treats Śrīvidyā in detail, gives its own account of her, including her root mantra in its forty-second chapter according to modern summaries. We mention the existence of that material only so that readers know where it lies.

A reader of the Sahasranāma can take the name Nityaklinnā in two ways: in its plain sense, the Goddess whose heart is always moist with compassion, or as a reference to the third Nityā. The hymn often works at two levels like this, and its commentators, Bhāskararāya above all, are fond of drawing out both.

A note on sources: the forms in this series come from modern summaries of the meditation verses, not from our own reading of the Sanskrit manuscripts, and the verses themselves differ between texts. Where two descriptions disagree we say so in the sources page at the end of each guide.

COMPASSION AS MOISTURE

Why “wet” is a compliment
Dry heartIn Indian devotional language a hard, dry heart is one that cannot love
Melted heartThe devotee’s heart “melts” (dravati) in bhakti
MoonThe moon is the source of dew and of soma, the nectar that moistens the world
Her dayShe belongs to the moon’s third day, when its nectar is growing
Her name“Ever-moist”: compassion that never dries up, whatever we do
GestureHer fear-removing hand says the same thing

ZAPPY: So her name is really about kindness?

PROF. BOLT: Kindness that can’t run out. ‘Always melted’ is how a mother’s heart is described in Sanskrit poetry.

The image runs deep in Indian thought. The moon is called the store of soma, the nectar that keeps plants and creatures alive; dew falls at night, under the moon. A Nityā of the growing moon whose very name is “ever-moist” gathers all of this into one figure: the Goddess as the nectar that keeps the world from drying out.

In bhakti poetry, from the Tamil saints to the Kannada vacanakāras, the devotee’s heart is said to melt, and tears of love are a sign of true devotion. Nityaklinnā is the divine side of the same exchange: the devotee’s heart melts towards her because hers is always melted towards the devotee.

There is also an older, more sensual reading of the name, connected with the Goddess’s love for Śiva. The Tantras do not shy away from it. In this guide we give the devotional reading first because it is the one most devotees live with.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Reading her form and name
MoistureCompassion that never dries
Noose and goadShe draws and guides, as Lalitā does
CupNourishment, nectar, satisfaction
No fearHer raised hand: the first thing compassion does is remove fear
Half-moonShe wears the moon whose day she rules

ZAPPY: If I had to pick one Nityā to talk to…

PROF. BOLT: Many would pick her. A goddess whose name means her heart is always soft is easy to approach.

Commentators who read the Nityās as stages of awareness put her third for a reason: after desire awakens (Kāmeśvarī) and fullness appears (Bhagamālinī), the heart softens. Only a softened heart can go further. This is a reading, not a rule of the texts, but it fits her name.

Of all fifteen, Nityaklinnā may be the easiest to understand without any Tantric training. Her name, her raised hand and her cup all say the same thing: the Goddess is kind, always. The third day of the moon is a good day to remember that.

Readings like these are offered as ways in, not as doctrine. Śrīvidyā commentators interpret the Nityās at several levels at once: as goddesses to be honoured, as days of the month, as powers within the body and as stages of awareness. A reader can take whichever level helps.

Where the facts come from

Sources: Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna chs. 25–26 (G. V. Tagare’s translation, Motilal Banarsidass; the night battle and the fire rampart); the Tantrarāja Tantra (ed. Lakṣmaṇa Śāstrī, with Sir John Woodroffe’s introduction) and the Vāmakeśvara Tantra (Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava), as summarised in modern studies; the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā as quoted in those studies; Gopinath Kaviraj’s introduction to the Yoginīhṛdaya (1963); the Lalitā Sahasranāma with Bhāskararāya’s Saubhāgyabhāskara; Mike Magee’s summaries of the Nityā meditation verses (shivashakti.com), used for iconography only.

Sources differ / unverified: Descriptions of each Nityā’s form differ between the Tantrarāja and the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, and between modern summaries. The tradition says the arms of all fifteen total 108, but the counts in individual meditation verses as summarised do not add up exactly. Whether Kāmeśvarī belongs to the first day of the bright fortnight or of the dark one, and whether the order reverses in the other fortnight, varies by lineage and writer. Which corner of the central triangle the row of Nityās starts from also varies. Names of the demon generals follow Tagare, with his variant readings.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
