SARVAMAṄGALĀ

“All-auspicious”

THE TITHI NITYĀS · 13 OF 15

FOREWORD

The thirteenth Nityā is the simplest of all fifteen. Sarvamaṅgalā, “all-auspicious”, has one head and only two arms: one holds a citron, the other gives boons. Her eyes are the sun and the moon, and she sits on her own lotus yantra.

Her name is familiar to almost every Hindu household, because it opens a famous verse of the Devī Māhātmya recited at the end of countless pūjās: sarvamaṅgala-māṅgalye. As a Nityā she rules the thirteenth lunar day.

Śrī Sarvamaṅgalāyai namaḥ,

ZAPPY: Prof, which Nityā do I already know without knowing it?

THE NAME

“All-auspicious”
SarvaAll, every
MaṅgalaAuspicious, blessed, good fortune
Together“All-auspicious”, “the blessing of all blessings”
A famous verseThe Devī Māhātmya greets the Goddess as “sarvamaṅgala-māṅgalye”
PārvatīSarvamaṅgalā is a well-loved name of Pārvatī
Purāṇa formThe Lalitopākhyāna calls her Sarvamaṅgalikā

ZAPPY: I’ve heard ‘sarvamaṅgala-māṅgalye’ at every pūjā!

PROF. BOLT: Of course. It’s one of the most recited lines in Hinduism. Sarvamaṅgalā’s name lives inside it.

Maṅgala means auspicious, blessed, fortunate; it is the root of words for weddings (maṅgala-kārya), the sacred thread worn by married women (maṅgala-sūtra) and the planet Mars. Sarva makes it total. Sarvamaṅgalā is the one in whom every blessing is gathered.

The Lalitopākhyāna calls her Sarvamaṅgalikā, a slightly fuller form with the same meaning. Outside Śrīvidyā the name belongs to Pārvatī and is used for the Goddess in many temples and hymns.

Every Nityā’s name can also be read as a name of Lalitā herself, and several of them appear in her hymns as her own titles. The tradition does not see this as confusion: each Nityā is one ray of the queen, so the ray and the source may share a name.

HER FORM

From the meditation verse

Arms and eyesTwo arms only; one head; her eyes are the sun and the moon

Seat or settingSeated on her own lotus yantra
AlsoSeventy-six attendants, of the sun, the moon and fire, each with a consort

ZAPPY: Her eyes are the sun and the moon?

PROF. BOLT: Day-eye and night-eye. She sees all of time, which a Nityā should.

Two arms are unusual in this cycle, where most Nityās have four to twelve. Her meditation verse gives her only a citron and the boon-giving hand. The plainness is the point: she does not need weapons or many tools. Her eyes, one the sun and one the moon, are those of a goddess who sees by day and by night.

Her seventy-six attendants are described as solar, lunar and fiery, each with a consort. The number and the three kinds of light echo an old scheme in which the sun, the moon and fire are the three lights of the world, also found in Śrīvidyā’s reading of the Śrīcakra.

Meditation verses (dhyānas) are meant to be pictured in detail, item by item, and the colours, hands and seats differ slightly between texts. We give the form as summarised from the Tantrarāja tradition, and note where another text differs.

ZAPPY: Day thirteen: Trayodaśī.

PROF. BOLT: Pradoṣa, Śiva’s twilight worship, falls on it. A day of Śiva, and of the all-auspicious Nityā.

Trayodaśī, the thirteenth day, is the day of Pradoṣa, the twilight worship of Śiva kept in both fortnights. Dhanteras, before Dīpāvalī, is also a Trayodaśī. In the Śrīvidyā calendar every Trayodaśī belongs to Sarvamaṅgalā.

Indian calendars still run on tithis. A tithi is the time the moon takes to gain twelve degrees on the sun, so it can be longer or shorter than a solar day and may begin at any hour. That is why festival dates move from year to year, and why a tithi named in a calendar may start in the afternoon.

IN THE NIGHT BATTLE

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 25
The attackBhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga creeps round to strike Lalitā’s army from the rear after dark
The NityāsAll fifteen ask Lalitā’s leave, dismount, and fight under Kāmeśvarī
DangerAn arrow shatters Lalitā’s royal fan; her guard goddesses are shaken
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light up the dark field
Her enemyTīkṣṇaśṛṅga (“Sharp-horn”), whom she cut down
The endAfter three watches of the night, Viṣaṅga flees; Lalitā heals the Nityās with one glance

ZAPPY: The two-armed Nityā chopped off a general?

PROF. BOLT: Tīkṣṇaśṛṅga, ‘Sharp-horn’. Auspiciousness isn’t weakness.

Her victim, Tīkṣṇaśṛṅga, “Sharp-horn”, is the thirteenth general in the Purāṇa’s list. Tagare translates that she “chopped off” the demon. The gentlest-looking Nityā strikes as decisively as any.

The pattern recurs through the scene: Nityaklinnā, the ever-moist, and Sarvamaṅgalā, the all-auspicious, fight as hard as Bheruṇḍā or Jvālāmālinī. The Purāṇa’s point seems to be that every face of the Goddess can defend her.

The night battle is the only place in the Lalitopākhyāna where all fifteen Nityās fight together as a company. Earlier in the war they ride with Lalitā on the great chariot Cakrarāja; here they dismount, and the story gives each of them her own enemy, so the battle reads like a roll-call of the lunar days.

AMONG HER SISTERS

Her place on the Śrīcakra
On the yantraThe fifteen Nityās are placed on the innermost triangle, five to a side, starting beside the apex and running anticlockwise
Her sideIn the usual arrangement she falls on the left-hand side, descending to the apex
At the centreMahānityā, Lalitā herself, the sixteenth, at the bindu
Before herNityā 12, Vijayā
After herNityā 14, Jvālāmālinī

ZAPPY: Thirteenth, middle of the left side.

PROF. BOLT: Halfway down towards the apex, between Vijayā and Jvālāmālinī.

She stands at the middle of the left side of the triangle. Below her come Jvālāmālinī and Citrā; then the row reaches the apex beside which Kāmeśvarī began, closing the circuit.

In Śrīvidyā thought time and space are mapped onto one another. The same fifteen goddesses who are the days of the fortnight are also placed on the innermost triangle of the Śrīcakra, so that the month itself is pictured as surrounding the Goddess at the centre.

ZAPPY: Is the temple Sarvamaṅgalā the same as the Nityā?

PROF. BOLT: Same name, broader goddess. Pārvatī as Sarvamaṅgalā is loved everywhere; the Nityā is her name on the thirteenth day.

Like every Nityā she has her own yantra and mantra in the Tantrarāja; uniquely, her meditation verse seats her on the yantra itself, the lotus diagram that is her own form in geometry. The image is of a goddess sitting on her own sign.

The Tantrarāja tradition gives her the fewest attributes of any Nityā. It is one of the clearest signs that the variety in the cycle is deliberate: one Nityā carries a whole armoury; another carries a single fruit.

A note on sources: the forms in this series come from modern summaries of the meditation verses, not from our own reading of the Sanskrit manuscripts, and the verses themselves differ between texts. Where two descriptions disagree we say so in the sources page at the end of each guide.

“SARVAMAṄGALA-MĀṄGALYE”

The verse everyone knows
SourceDevī Māhātmya, chapter 11, the gods’ hymn to Nārāyaṇī
Openingsarvamaṅgala-māṅgalye śive sarvārtha-sādhike
Meaning“O auspiciousness of all that is auspicious, O Śivā, accomplisher of every aim”
UseRecited at the end of countless pūjās, weddings and household rites
The NityāHer name, Sarvamaṅgalā, is the word the verse begins with
Our noteThe verse addresses the Goddess as a whole, not the Nityā in particular

ZAPPY: So every time someone says that verse…

PROF. BOLT: They’re calling the Goddess all-auspicious. A Śrīvidyā reader can hear the thirteenth Nityā’s name in it too.

The full verse, in our own rendering, says: O auspiciousness of all that is auspicious, O Śivā, accomplisher of every aim, O refuge, O three-eyed Gaurī, O Nārāyaṇī, salutation to you. It comes from the gods’ hymn to the Goddess as Nārāyaṇī in the eleventh chapter of the Devī Māhātmya, after her final victory over Śumbha.

The verse has escaped its context and become a household blessing. It is said at the end of pūjās, printed on wedding invitations and sung in temples across India. Most people who say it think of the Goddess in general, not of a Nityā, and that is the right reading of the verse.

For a Śrīvidyā reader there is a pleasant overlap. The verse opens with the very word that names the thirteenth Nityā. Every recitation can be heard, if one wishes, as a greeting to her too.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Reading her form and name
SimplicityThe least ornate Nityā carries the greatest blessing
CitronThe fruit of action: reward
BoonWhat is given freely
Sun and moonDay and night, the whole of time, in her sight
AttendantsSun, moon and fire, with their consorts: every light, paired

ZAPPY: Why does the simplest one get the biggest name?

PROF. BOLT: Because blessing doesn’t need twelve arms. One open hand is enough.

The citron, a large fragrant fruit, is held by many deities, among them Gaṇapati and several goddesses, and is usually read as the fruit of action. In her hand, with the boon-giving gesture beside it, it reads as the promise that good effort bears good fruit.

Read as a stage, Sarvamaṅgalā comes after victory. When the struggle is won, what remains is simple: blessing. The commentators’ picture is of a mind that, having won, no longer needs weapons and holds only a fruit and an open hand.

Her eyes of sun and moon also point forward. In two more days the moon will be full; the Nityā who sees with both lights stands just before the end of the cycle.

Readings like these are offered as ways in, not as doctrine. Śrīvidyā commentators interpret the Nityās at several levels at once: as goddesses to be honoured, as days of the month, as powers within the body and as stages of awareness. A reader can take whichever level helps.

Sources differ / unverified: Descriptions of each Nityā’s form differ between the Tantrarāja and the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, and between modern summaries. The tradition says the arms of all fifteen total 108, but the counts in individual meditation verses as summarised do not add up exactly. Whether Kāmeśvarī belongs to the first day of the bright fortnight or of the dark one, and whether the order reverses in the other fortnight, varies by lineage and writer. Which corner of the central triangle the row of Nityās starts from also varies. Names of the demon generals follow Tagare, with his variant readings.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
