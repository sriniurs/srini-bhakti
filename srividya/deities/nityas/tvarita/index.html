ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

TVARITĀ

“The swift one” · explained and illustrated

THE TITHI NITYĀS · 8 OF 15

HOW THIS WORKS: Tvaritā, “the swift one”, the eighth of the fifteen Tithi Nityās of Śrīvidyā: her lunar day Aṣṭamī, her form from the meditation verse, her deed in the night battle of the Lalitopākhyāna, her place on the Śrīcakra, the texts that describe her, and what she means.

FOREWORD

The eighth Nityā looks like no other. Tvaritā, “the swift one”, is dark and youthful, dressed in fresh leaves, with eight serpents for jewels, a crystal crown crested with peacock feathers, and necklaces of red guñjā berries. Among jewelled queens she comes as a goddess of the forest.

She rules Aṣṭamī, the half-moon, the very middle of the fortnight. Her name promises speed: she is said to grant results quickly. In the night battle she does not use arrows at all, but tears her enemy apart with her arms.

Śrī Tvaritāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, which Nityā answers fastest?

PROF. BOLT: Her name says it, Zappy: Tvaritā, the swift. She doesn’t even stop to dress like a queen.

ZAPPY: Leaves, snakes and peacock feathers? She looks like a forest queen.

PROF. BOLT: That’s exactly the impression. Among fifteen jewelled court ladies, Tvaritā walks in from the forest.

THE NAME

“The swift one”
TvarāHaste, speed
Tvaritā“The swift one”, “she who hurries”
Why swiftShe is said to grant results quickly
Other nameAlso called Totalā
In the storyShe tears apart her enemy “with mighty arms”: speed and strength together
Short formThe Lalitopākhyāna also calls her Tvaritā

ZAPPY: A goddess named ‘Quick’?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees like that. When you’re in trouble, you want the one who comes fast.

Tvaritā comes from tvarā, haste. In devotional usage the name is a promise: this Nityā is said to bring her devotee’s wish to fruit without delay. The Tantric sources also call her Totalā, a name whose meaning is less clear; we give it without guessing.

Speed is a quality devotees prize in the Goddess. Many hymns end by asking her to come quickly, and several goddesses carry names meaning “quick” or “at once”. Tvaritā is the Nityā of that hope.

Every Nityā’s name can also be read as a name of Lalitā herself, and several of them appear in her hymns as her own titles. The tradition does not see this as confusion: each Nityā is one ray of the queen, so the ray and the source may share a name.

HER FORM

From the meditation verse
ColourDark, in the first flush of youth
Arms and eyesFour hands; three eyes
HoldsA noose and a goad; the gestures of fearlessness and of giving
Seat or settingNot specified in the summary we used
AlsoDressed in fresh leaves; eight serpents as ornaments; a crystal crown with a crest of peacock feathers; peacock-feather bangles; necklaces of red guñjā berries; red sandal and kuṅkuma. Attendants: a dark servant with a mace, and Jaya and Vijaya with golden canes

ZAPPY: Why the forest look?

PROF. BOLT: Our reading: the Goddess isn’t only for palaces. She appears as the forest people too, the hill tribes, the hunters. The texts just describe; that’s an interpretation.

The details of her meditation verse are unusually earthy. Instead of silk, leaves; instead of gold, serpents; instead of a jewelled crest, peacock feathers; instead of pearls, red guñjā berries. Red sandal paste and kuṅkuma complete the picture. Her attendants are equally vivid: a dark servant carrying a mace, and two doorkeepers, Jaya and Vijaya, with golden canes.

Guñjā seeds are bright red with a black spot and were once used as a jeweller’s weight. They are also poisonous if crushed and eaten, which may suit a goddess who wears serpents. Strung as necklaces, they are a forest-dweller’s ornament.

Meditation verses (dhyānas) are meant to be pictured in detail, item by item, and the colours, hands and seats differ slightly between texts. We give the form as summarised from the Tantrarāja tradition, and note where another text differs.

Before herŚivadūtī on Saptamī
After herKulasundarī on Navamī
A vowelEach Nityā is linked with one of the sixteen Sanskrit vowels; she has the eighth
BreathThe tradition gives each Nityā 1,440 of the 21,600 breaths of a day and night
Two fortnightsShe returns in both halves of the month; the order used in the dark half varies by lineage

ZAPPY: Day eight: half-moon!

PROF. BOLT: Aṣṭamī, the half-way day. A big day for the Goddess: Durgāṣṭamī, Kṛṣṇa’s birth on an Aṣṭamī. And Tvaritā’s.

Aṣṭamī, the eighth day, is the half-moon and one of the most sacred days for the Goddess. Durgāṣṭamī falls in Navarātri; Kṛṣṇa’s birth, Janmāṣṭamī, falls on the eighth day of the dark fortnight. For Śrīvidyā, every Aṣṭamī is Tvaritā’s.

Indian calendars still run on tithis. A tithi is the time the moon takes to gain twelve degrees on the sun, so it can be longer or shorter than a solar day and may begin at any hour. That is why festival dates move from year to year, and why a tithi named in a calendar may start in the afternoon.

IN THE NIGHT BATTLE

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 25
The attackBhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga creeps round to strike Lalitā’s army from the rear after dark
The NityāsAll fifteen ask Lalitā’s leave, dismount, and fight under Kāmeśvarī
DangerAn arrow shatters Lalitā’s royal fan; her guard goddesses are shaken
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light up the dark field
Her enemyPuṇḍraketu, torn apart “with mighty arms”
Her styleNot arrows but “mighty arms”: she fights hand to hand
The endAfter three watches of the night, Viṣaṅga flees; Lalitā heals the Nityās with one glance

ZAPPY: Bare hands against a demon general?

PROF. BOLT: The text says ‘with mighty arms’. The swift goddess doesn’t wait to string a bow.

Her victim Puṇḍraketu is the eighth general in the Purāṇa’s list, matching her place. The Purāṇa singles out her method: she tears him apart “with mighty arms”. It is one of the few kills in the scene that is not made with arrows.

The detail fits her name. The swift Nityā closes the distance and fights at arm’s length, while her sisters shoot from where they stand.

The night battle is the only place in the Lalitopākhyāna where all fifteen Nityās fight together as a company. Earlier in the war they ride with Lalitā on the great chariot Cakrarāja; here they dismount, and the story gives each of them her own enemy, so the battle reads like a roll-call of the lunar days.

AMONG HER SISTERS

Her place on the Śrīcakra
On the yantraThe fifteen Nityās are placed on the innermost triangle, five to a side, starting beside the apex and running anticlockwise
Her sideIn the usual arrangement she falls on the upper (base) side
At the centreMahānityā, Lalitā herself, the sixteenth, at the bindu
Before herNityā 7, Śivadūtī
After herNityā 9, Kulasundarī

ZAPPY: Middle of the top side.

PROF. BOLT: Eighth of fifteen, the very centre of the cycle, on the half-moon.

On the triangle she stands in the middle of the top side, which runs from Mahāvajreśvarī to Nityā, and in the middle of the fifteen. The half-moon’s Nityā is the cycle’s balance point.

In Śrīvidyā thought time and space are mapped onto one another. The same fifteen goddesses who are the days of the fortnight are also placed on the innermost triangle of the Śrīcakra, so that the month itself is pictured as surrounding the Goddess at the centre.

IN THE TEXTS

AttendantsJaya and Vijaya, “Victory” and “Triumph”, carry golden canes before her

ZAPPY: She has fans outside Śrīvidyā?

PROF. BOLT: So it’s said. We’ll stick to what we could check: her form, her day and her battle.

Tvaritā is one of the Nityās who is said to have a following outside Śrīvidyā, with texts and rites of her own. Modern sources mention this but often without details we could check, so we leave it at that.

The two attendants Jaya and Vijaya share their names with Viṣṇu’s doorkeepers in Vaikuṇṭha. Doorkeeper pairs with these names appear in many temples, of Viṣṇu, of Śiva and of the Goddess, and the names simply mean victory and triumph.

A note on sources: the forms in this series come from modern summaries of the meditation verses, not from our own reading of the Sanskrit manuscripts, and the verses themselves differ between texts. Where two descriptions disagree we say so in the sources page at the end of each guide.

THE FOREST GODDESS

Leaves, serpents and peacock feathers
LeavesShe wears fresh leaves instead of silk
SerpentsEight snakes as ornaments, worn like jewels
Peacock feathersIn her crown and on her wrists
GuñjāRed-and-black jungle berries, strung as necklaces
The lookThat of a youthful forest or hill woman, a Śabarī or kirātī
A parallelŚiva too takes a hunter’s (kirāta) form in the Mahābhārata; our comparison, not her text’s

ZAPPY: Why would a queen’s companion dress like a hunter?

PROF. BOLT: Because the Goddess is everyone’s. The forest is hers as much as the city. That’s one reading; another sees protective power in serpents and wild things.

India has always had goddesses of the forest and the hills, worshipped by hunters, herders and tribes, and the great traditions have repeatedly drawn them in. Tvaritā’s image reads like one such goddess welcomed into Lalitā’s court, still wearing her leaves. That is our reading of the image; the texts do not tell her history.

There is a well-known parallel in the Mahābhārata. Śiva comes to test Arjuna disguised as a kirāta, a hunter, and Pārvatī comes with him dressed as a hunter’s wife. The great gods, in this picture, are as much at home in the forest as anywhere.

The serpents she wears are worth a thought. Śiva wears snakes too. A snake worn as an ornament is danger mastered: what could kill is turned into decoration. For a devotee afraid of something, the image says the Goddess wears that very danger lightly.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Reading her form and name
SpeedGrace that does not delay
ForestThe Goddess of the wild as well as the city
SerpentsDanger worn as an ornament: mastered, not feared
Half-moonBalance: half light, half dark
Two gestures“Don’t be afraid” and “take this”: protection and gift

ZAPPY: If I had to sum her up?

PROF. BOLT: Help that comes running, dressed in whatever it had on.

Commentators who read the Nityās as stages place the half-moon at the point of balance. Half the journey is done and half remains; light and dark are equal. The swift Nityā stands here because, at the midpoint, help is most needed and should not be slow.

Her two gestures are simple and complete: fear removed, gift given. A goddess dressed in leaves who offers both is perhaps the most approachable of all fifteen.

Readings like these are offered as ways in, not as doctrine. Śrīvidyā commentators interpret the Nityās at several levels at once: as goddesses to be honoured, as days of the month, as powers within the body and as stages of awareness. A reader can take whichever level helps.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna chs. 25–26 (G. V. Tagare’s translation, Motilal Banarsidass; the night battle and the fire rampart); the Tantrarāja Tantra (ed. Lakṣmaṇa Śāstrī, with Sir John Woodroffe’s introduction) and the Vāmakeśvara Tantra (Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava), as summarised in modern studies; the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā as quoted in those studies; Gopinath Kaviraj’s introduction to the Yoginīhṛdaya (1963); the Lalitā Sahasranāma with Bhāskararāya’s Saubhāgyabhāskara; Mike Magee’s summaries of the Nityā meditation verses (shivashakti.com), used for iconography only.

Sources differ / unverified: Descriptions of each Nityā’s form differ between the Tantrarāja and the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, and between modern summaries. The tradition says the arms of all fifteen total 108, but the counts in individual meditation verses as summarised do not add up exactly. Whether Kāmeśvarī belongs to the first day of the bright fortnight or of the dark one, and whether the order reverses in the other fortnight, varies by lineage and writer. Which corner of the central triangle the row of Nityās starts from also varies. Names of the demon generals follow Tagare, with his variant readings.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
