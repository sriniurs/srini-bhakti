VAHNIVĀSINĪ

“She who dwells in fire”

THE TITHI NITYĀS · 5 OF 15

FOREWORD

The fifth Nityā is the goddess of light. Vahnivāsinī, “she who dwells in fire”, is golden, dressed in yellow silk and rubies, and holds in her eight hands flowers, a conch, a horn, a bow and arrows, a citron, and the full moon itself.

In the night battle she has a job no one else can do. The demons attacked in darkness; Vahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī were sent to light the field so the Nityās could see whom they were fighting.

Śrī Vahnivāsinyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, is there a Nityā for light?

PROF. BOLT: There is, Zappy: the fifth. When the demons came in the dark, she switched the lights on.

PROF. BOLT: That’s the riddle of her. Fire and moon, hot and cool, in one goddess.

Her job in the storyTo be the light: she lights the dark battlefield

ZAPPY: Does she live in a volcano?

PROF. BOLT: In the fire of every offering, and in the light that drives back darkness. That’s where the story puts her to work.

Vahni is one of many Sanskrit words for fire, and its root suggests carrying: fire carries the offering up to the gods. Vāsinī means “she who dwells”. Together they make a name that tells us where to look for her: in the sacrificial fire, in the lamp before the shrine, in every flame.

The Lalitopākhyāna uses the shorter Vahnivāsā. The meaning is the same. Several Nityās have such short forms in the Purāṇa, which uses them freely to fit its verse.

Every Nityā’s name can also be read as a name of Lalitā herself, and several of them appear in her hymns as her own titles. The tradition does not see this as confusion: each Nityā is one ray of the queen, so the ray and the source may share a name.

HER FORM

From the meditation verse
ColourGolden; a young woman in yellow silk, adorned with rubies
Arms and eyesEight arms
HoldsLeft: a red lotus, a conch, a red sugarcane bow, the full moon. Right: a white water-lily, a golden horn, flower arrows, a citron
Seat or settingNot specified in the summary we used
AlsoSurrounded by countless attendant goddesses; her yantra has twelve petals, read as the twelve signs of the zodiac

ZAPPY: A full moon in one hand on day five?

PROF. BOLT: The moon isn’t full yet on her day. She holds the promise of the full moon, like a lamp held up ahead.

Her colours are fire colours: gold, yellow and ruby red. Yet much of what she holds is cool: the conch from the sea, the white water-lily that opens under the moon, and the full moon itself. The image is deliberately balanced. A goddess of fire who carried only fire would burn; this one also holds what cools.

The citron (mātuluṅga) is a fruit often held by goddesses and by Gaṇapati, a sign of the fruit of action, the reward that comes from effort. The golden horn may be a drinking horn or a sounding horn; summaries differ, and we leave it as “horn”.

Meditation verses (dhyānas) are meant to be pictured in detail, item by item, and the colours, hands and seats differ slightly between texts. We give the form as summarised from the Tantrarāja tradition, and note where another text differs.

ZAPPY: Day five: Pañcamī.

PROF. BOLT: Nāga Pañcamī, Vasanta Pañcamī for Sarasvatī: famous fifth days. And always Vahnivāsinī’s.

Pañcamī, the fifth lunar day, carries several festivals: Vasanta Pañcamī for Sarasvatī at the start of spring, and Nāga Pañcamī for the serpents in the rains. In the Śrīvidyā calendar every fifth day, in both fortnights, also belongs to Vahnivāsinī.

Indian calendars still run on tithis. A tithi is the time the moon takes to gain twelve degrees on the sun, so it can be longer or shorter than a solar day and may begin at any hour. That is why festival dates move from year to year, and why a tithi named in a calendar may start in the afternoon.

IN THE NIGHT BATTLE

Lalitopākhyāna, chapter 25
The attackBhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga creeps round to strike Lalitā’s army from the rear after dark
The NityāsAll fifteen ask Lalitā’s leave, dismount, and fight under Kāmeśvarī
DangerAn arrow shatters Lalitā’s royal fan; her guard goddesses are shaken
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light up the dark field
Her enemyKalkaśa (variant Karkaśa, “Harsh”), with sharp arrows
Her special taskWith Jvālāmālinī she is sent to light the field, so the hidden enemy can be seen
The endAfter three watches of the night, Viṣaṅga flees; Lalitā heals the Nityās with one glance

ZAPPY: She was the floodlight?

PROF. BOLT: Literally. You can’t fight what you can’t see. She lit up the night, then picked up her bow.

The Purāṇa makes the reason plain. The Nityās report to Lalitā that the demons are attacking in darkness, and ask that Vahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light the field. Lalitā agrees, and the two fire goddesses blaze up so that the enemy is exposed. Only then does the fighting begin in earnest.

Her own victim, Kalkaśa (Tagare’s variant reading is Karkaśa, “Harsh”), falls to her sharp arrows. Having lit the battle, she also fights in it.

The night battle is the only place in the Lalitopākhyāna where all fifteen Nityās fight together as a company. Earlier in the war they ride with Lalitā on the great chariot Cakrarāja; here they dismount, and the story gives each of them her own enemy, so the battle reads like a roll-call of the lunar days.

Her sideIn the usual arrangement she falls on the right-hand side, rising from the apex
At the centreMahānityā, Lalitā herself, the sixteenth, at the bindu
Before herNityā 4, Bheruṇḍā
After herNityā 6, Mahāvajreśvarī

ZAPPY: End of the first side!

PROF. BOLT: Fifth of five. The first side of the triangle closes with fire.

With her the first side of the central triangle is complete. The first five Nityās are, in order, desire, fortune, compassion, protection and light; the second side begins with Mahāvajreśvarī on her golden boat.

In Śrīvidyā thought time and space are mapped onto one another. The same fifteen goddesses who are the days of the fortnight are also placed on the innermost triangle of the Śrīcakra, so that the month itself is pictured as surrounding the Goddess at the centre.

ZAPPY: The zodiac in a Nityā’s yantra?

PROF. BOLT: The Tantrarāja loves mapping time onto everything. Fifteen days for the Nityās, twelve signs for her petals.

The twelve-petalled yantra is one of the clearest examples of how the Tantrarāja works. The Nityās already map the fifteen days of the fortnight; her yantra adds the twelve signs through which the sun moves in a year. Month and year meet in one diagram, and she sits at its centre.

Of all the Nityās she and Jvālāmālinī have the most obvious role in the story, because both are fire goddesses and the story needs fire. The Purāṇa uses her short name, Vahnivāsā, in both the lighting of the field and her kill.

A note on sources: the forms in this series come from modern summaries of the meditation verses, not from our own reading of the Sanskrit manuscripts, and the verses themselves differ between texts. Where two descriptions disagree we say so in the sources page at the end of each guide.

FIRE AND MOON

Agni and Soma in one goddess
AgniFire: the eater, the carrier of offerings
SomaThe moon’s nectar: what is eaten, what is offered
Vedic pairAgni and Soma are paired in Vedic ritual: the fire and the offering
In her handsShe dwells in fire, yet holds the full moon
In the bodyYoga texts speak of the inner fire and the inner moon
Our noteThe link with the Vedic pair is a reading, not a statement of her texts

ZAPPY: So she’s both the fire and the thing burned?

PROF. BOLT: She’s the meeting of the two. Sacrifice needs both: something to give, and a fire to carry it.

In Vedic ritual Agni and Soma are paired again and again: fire and the offering poured into it, the eater and the eaten. Soma was also the name of the moon. A goddess who dwells in fire and holds the full moon brings the pair together in a single image. We offer this as a reading; her own texts do not mention the Vedic pair.

Yoga traditions use a similar picture inside the body: a fire below and a moon above, whose nectar the fire receives. Śrīvidyā writers use this inner geography too. Again, it is a way of reading her, not a teaching we have found tied to her name.

The simplest meaning is the one the story gives. Fire is light; light shows what is hidden; once the hidden enemy is seen, he can be defeated. Every lamp lit at dusk in an Indian home does a small version of what Vahnivāsinī did on the battlefield.

ZAPPY: If Kāmeśvarī is wanting, what’s she?

PROF. BOLT: Some read her as clarity. After wanting and guarding comes light: seeing things as they are.

Her two flowers repeat the balance of fire and moon. The red lotus is the colour of passion and of the sun; the white water-lily opens only at night. She holds both, and so the commentators say she reconciles opposites, the way fire and moon are reconciled in her hands.

Commentators who read the Nityās as stages of awareness give her a natural place at the end of the first group. Desire, fullness, tenderness and protection need one more thing to be complete: the light to see clearly. The fifth day of the moon, bright enough to cast faint shadows, suits her.

Readings like these are offered as ways in, not as doctrine. Śrīvidyā commentators interpret the Nityās at several levels at once: as goddesses to be honoured, as days of the month, as powers within the body and as stages of awareness. A reader can take whichever level helps.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna chs. 25–26 (G. V. Tagare’s translation, Motilal Banarsidass; the night battle and the fire rampart); the Tantrarāja Tantra (ed. Lakṣmaṇa Śāstrī, with Sir John Woodroffe’s introduction) and the Vāmakeśvara Tantra (Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava), as summarised in modern studies; the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā as quoted in those studies; Gopinath Kaviraj’s introduction to the Yoginīhṛdaya (1963); the Lalitā Sahasranāma with Bhāskararāya’s Saubhāgyabhāskara; Mike Magee’s summaries of the Nityā meditation verses (shivashakti.com), used for iconography only.

Sources differ / unverified: Descriptions of each Nityā’s form differ between the Tantrarāja and the Dakṣiṇāmūrti Saṃhitā, and between modern summaries. The tradition says the arms of all fifteen total 108, but the counts in individual meditation verses as summarised do not add up exactly. Whether Kāmeśvarī belongs to the first day of the bright fortnight or of the dark one, and whether the order reverses in the other fortnight, varies by lineage and writer. Which corner of the central triangle the row of Nityās starts from also varies. Names of the demon generals follow Tagare, with his variant readings.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
