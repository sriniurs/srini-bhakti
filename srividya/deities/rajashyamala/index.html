ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

RĀJAŚYĀMALĀ

Mātaṅgī, minister of the Queen · explained and illustrated

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · DEITIES

HOW THIS WORKS: Rājaśyāmalā, also Mātaṅgī and Mantriṇī, Lalitā’s prime minister and goddess of music and speech: her names and green form with vīṇā and parrot, the Geyacakra chariot and the slaying of Viṣaṅga, Mātaṅgī among the Mahāvidyās, Kālidāsa’s Śyāmalā Daṇḍaka, Madurai and Māgha Navarātri.

FOREWORD

Beside Lalitā’s throne stands a green goddess with a vīṇā and a parrot. She is Rājaśyāmalā, also Mātaṅgī, and her office is Mantriṇī: the queen’s prime minister, the voice of her counsel.

She is also the goddess of music, speech and learning, beloved of poets and musicians, and in her Mahāvidyā form the goddess who sits with outcastes. This guide covers her names, form, war, hymn, temples, season and meaning.

Śrī Rājaśyāmalāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A minister who sings?

PROF. BOLT: And fights. And writes poetry. Busy lady.

ZAPPY: Prime minister AND goddess of music?

PROF. BOLT: Why not? A good minister knows how to speak, and music is speech at its best. She's the voice of the throne.

If Vārāhī is the strong arm of Lalitā’s kingdom, Śyāmalā is its voice and mind. She advises, speaks, sings and teaches. Her worship draws musicians, scholars, poets and students, and her best-known image, a green goddess playing the vīṇā with a parrot on her hand, is one of the loveliest in Indian art.

She has two lives, like Vārāhī. Outside Śrīvidyā she is Mātaṅgī, the ninth of the Ten Mahāvidyās, with a striking form associated with outcastes and leftovers. In Lalitā’s court she is Rājaśyāmalā, the royal minister. This guide shows how the two fit together.

As always in this series, her names, forms, stories, temples and meanings are described; her mantras and the rites of her worship are left to teachers.

The minister of the Queen

MātaṅgīDaughter of the sage Mātaṅga, in one line of stories
OfficeMantriṇī: the counsellor who holds the queen’s policy, mantra in both senses
PairWith Vārāhī the general, Lalitā’s two chief officers
DomainSpeech, music, learning, the arts, eloquence and attraction
KinRead as a tantric form of Sarasvatī

ZAPPY: Mantriṇī means minister? Like ‘mantri’ in Hindi?

PROF. BOLT: Same word. A mantri gives counsel, mantra. And Śyāmalā keeps the Goddess's counsel, and her mantras too.

In Indian political thought, the king rules with two chief helpers: the senāpati, who commands the army, and the mantrī, who gives counsel. The Lalitopākhyāna gives Lalitā exactly this pair. The idea is very old: a ruler without a wise minister is a danger to the kingdom.

Śyāmalā’s link with Sarasvatī runs deep. Both hold the vīṇā, both preside over speech and learning, and both are invoked by students. But Śyāmalā’s worship is tantric, her colour green rather than white, and her character more passionate. Many writers call her Sarasvatī’s esoteric form.

The story of the sage Mātaṅga, who did long penance to have the Goddess as his daughter, gives her the name Mātaṅgī. Some tellings make her the daughter of a hunter or outcaste instead, which leads to her Mahāvidyā form.

ZAPPY: Why does a music goddess have an elephant name?

PROF. BOLT: Mātaṅga means elephant, a symbol of royal majesty. Her other side is the sage Mātaṅga, her father in story. One word, two lineages.

Like most Śrīvidyā deities, Śyāmalā has a set of names recited by her devotees, and several forms with different emphases. Laghuśyāmalā, the “light” one, is gentler and more accessible; Rājaśyāmalā is the full royal form in Lalitā’s court; Ucchiṣṭa Mātaṅgī is the radical Mahāvidyā form.

The double meaning of Mātaṅga, sage and elephant, is typical of the tradition’s love of layered names. The elephant is a royal animal, so “Mātaṅgī” quietly marks her as queenly even in her lowest-born form.

Saṅgītayoginī, the yoginī of music, is a favourite name among musicians. It casts music not as entertainment but as yoga, a discipline that unites the performer with the Divine.

Her devotees also use short title-names when addressing her in song: Śyāmā, Ambā, Saṅgītamātṛkā, “mother of music”. Dīkṣitar and other composers weave such epithets into their lyrics, and listening for them is a good way to recognise a song to Śyāmalā even without knowing the rest of the text.

ZAPPY: Why is she green?

PROF. BOLT: Śyāma means dark, but for her it's the green of emeralds and parrots, the colour of fresh leaves. Growth, life, speech springing up.

māṇikyavīṇām upalālayantīṃ madālasāṃ mañjulavāgvilāsām
māhendranīladyutikomalāṅgīṃ mātaṅgakanyāṃ manasā smarāmi

I hold in my mind the daughter of Mātaṅga, caressing a ruby vīṇā, languid with delight, charming in speech, her tender body glowing like a sapphire. (The opening meditation verse of the Śyāmalā Daṇḍaka, ascribed to Kālidāsa)

The opening verse of the Śyāmalā Daṇḍaka is one of the best-known meditation verses in Sanskrit, recited by many who never read the rest of the hymn. It sums up her image: the ruby vīṇā, the languid eyes, the sweet speech and the body glowing like a blue-green sapphire.

Her parrot is more than decoration. Parrots repeat words; they were kept by poets and lovers and appear in Sanskrit poetry as messengers. On Śyāmalā’s hand the parrot becomes speech itself, perched on the Goddess’s finger.

In her four-armed form she holds Lalitā’s own weapons, noose, goad, sugarcane bow and arrows, showing that she acts with the queen’s full authority. The two-armed vīṇā-playing image shows her in her own domain of music.

THE MINISTER

Her place in Lalitā’s court
OfficeChief counsellor: advises, speaks for the queen, carries her authority
RankEqual with Vārāhī; together the queen’s right and left hands
In ŚrīpuraHer quarters are in the grove of kadamba trees, a place of music
InsigniaTradition says she bears the queen’s authority in council as Vārāhī does in war
In worshipŚrīvidyā lineages honour her alongside Vārāhī as Lalitā’s two great aṅga-devatās (attendant deities)

ZAPPY: Why does a queen need two officers?

PROF. BOLT: One to guard and one to guide. Force without wisdom is dangerous; wisdom without force is weak. Lalitā keeps both at her side.

The Lalitopākhyāna and the Lalitā Stavaratna describe Śrīpura in great detail, and Śyāmalā has her place in its geography: a grove of kadamba trees, golden with flowers, where music never stops. Some commentators connect this grove with the Sahasranāma name that calls Lalitā a dweller in the kadamba forest.

In Śrīvidyā practice, Śyāmalā and Vārāhī are honoured as Lalitā’s two principal attendant deities. Many lineages give each a place of special honour, and teachers often say that the student’s path is protected by Vārāhī and guided by Śyāmalā.

Royal imagery runs throughout. A minister in Indian courts carried the king’s seal and spoke with his authority. Śyāmalā’s Sahasranāma title, Mantriṇī, and the honour given to her in the hymn, make clear that her word carries Lalitā’s weight.

There is a quiet lesson about leadership in this arrangement. The minister does not rule; the queen does. But the queen does not act without counsel. Commentators who read the court as a picture of a well-governed mind make the same point: the heart decides, but it listens first.

IN LALITĀ’S WAR

Mantriṇī in the field
ChariotGeyacakra, “the wheel of song”: a towering chariot of many levels
Her foeViṣaṅga, Bhaṇḍa’s brother, whom she kills
PartnerVārāhī kills the other brother, Viśukra
Lalitā’s joyThe Sahasranāma says Lalitā is pleased by Mantriṇī’s slaying of Viṣaṅga
MoreTold in Part 4 of the Lalitopākhyāna

ZAPPY: The music goddess fights too?

PROF. BOLT: Fiercely. Her chariot is made of song, but its arrows are real. Wisdom has its battles too.

Geyacakra, Śyāmalā’s chariot, is described as made of song and music: its levels populated by deities of sound and melody. It rides beside Daṇḍanāthā’s Kiricakra and Lalitā’s own Cakrarāja as the three great chariots of the war.

Viṣaṅga, the brother she kills, is in some readings associated with sensory distraction, while Viśukra, killed by Vārāhī, is linked with a deeper pride or confusion. These moral readings are commentators’ interpretations; the Purāṇa tells it simply as battle.

The pairing of the two killings is deliberate. Bhaṇḍa’s two brothers fall to Lalitā’s two officers, mind and will, and only then does the queen herself face Bhaṇḍa. The order is a teaching: the mind and will must be won before the root ego can be overcome.

ZAPPY: Lalitā's own hymn calls Śyāmalā ‘Mother’?

PROF. BOLT: Ambā, yes. In this tradition every form of the Goddess is Mother, even the minister.

geyacakrarathārūḍhamantriṇīparisevitā
mantriṇyambāviracitaviṣaṅgavadhatoṣitā

She who is attended by Mantriṇī, riding the Geyacakra chariot. She who is pleased by the slaying of Viṣaṅga accomplished by Mother Mantriṇī. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

The two names for Mantriṇī stand beside the two for Daṇḍanāthā in the war section of the Sahasranāma. Chanting them, the devotee walks through the battle in order, saluting each officer as she appears.

Bhāskararāya explains these names with reference to the Lalitopākhyāna, and adds inner readings in which the chariots and officers stand for powers within the practitioner. Both readings are kept in the tradition.

Other names of the Sahasranāma touch Śyāmalā’s domain without naming her: names on the Goddess as the source of speech, music and the fine arts. Commentators often connect them to Śyāmalā’s worship.

StoryIn one telling she appears as an outcaste woman to Śiva and Pārvatī, and is honoured by them
MeaningThe Goddess is present even where society sees impurity

On this siteMātaṅgī has her own guide in the Mahāvidyā series

ZAPPY: An outcaste goddess and a royal minister? Same person?

PROF. BOLT: Two faces. The tradition loves that: the queen's highest counsellor is also the one who sits with those everyone else rejects.

On this site: Mātaṅgī among the Mahāvidyās

The Mahāvidyā Mātaṅgī is one of the most radical images of the Goddess. She is associated with ucchiṣṭa, leftover food that orthodox purity rules count as polluted, and with outcaste groups. Her worship deliberately overturns those rules, insisting that the Divine is present everywhere.

In a well-known story, Pārvatī takes the form of an outcaste woman and approaches Śiva, who is drawn to her; the form is later honoured as Mātaṅgī. Other tellings differ. The theme is consistent: the Goddess cannot be confined by social boundaries.

Śrīvidyā keeps this radical side mainly in the background and honours the royal form, Rājamātaṅgī. But the two are one goddess, and teachers often remind students that the queen’s minister also sits with the outcaste.

GODDESS OF MUSIC AND SPEECH

Her gifts
VīṇāThe instrument of Sarasvatī and Nārada: music as a path to the Divine
SpeechEloquence, poetry, the right word at the right moment
LearningStudents and scholars turn to her as to Sarasvatī
AttractionHer songs draw hearts: an old meaning of her worship
MusiciansCarnatic musicians often honour her before concerts and lessons

ZAPPY: Like Sarasvatī?

PROF. BOLT: Very like her. Some call Śyāmalā the Tantric Sarasvatī. Sarasvatī is white and serene; Śyāmalā is green and alive with music.

Carnatic music has always had a devotional heart, and many of its great composers were also devotees of the Goddess. For musicians, Śyāmalā is the patron who gives both skill and inspiration, and her picture with the vīṇā is a natural companion in a music room.

Her gift of speech is also prized by teachers, lawyers, writers and speakers. Students preparing for exams and interviews sometimes chant her names; the tradition presents this as asking for clarity and confidence, not for a magic shortcut.

The old idea of “attraction” in her worship is often misunderstood. In the texts it means the power of beautiful speech and art to draw hearts toward the good, the way a great singer can move a whole hall.

The link between music and liberation is old in India. The Saṅgītaratnākara and other musical treatises speak of nāda, sacred sound, as Brahman itself, and of music as a yoga. Śyāmalā gives that idea a face: a goddess who rules a kingdom from a vīṇā.

KĀLIDĀSA AND THE DAṆḌAKA

A poet’s hymn
The poetKālidāsa, greatest of Sanskrit poets, c. 4th–5th century
LegendA dull young man, married by trickery to a learned princess, who gained his genius from the Goddess
The hymnThe Śyāmalā Daṇḍaka: a hymn in a long, flowing prose-verse metre (daṇḍaka)
ContentsOpens with meditation verses, then a torrent of praise of her form, music and court
AttributionTraditional; many scholars doubt that the classical poet wrote it
Our approachWe describe it and quote only its opening verse

ZAPPY: Kālidāsa was dull?

PROF. BOLT: In the legend. A princess who loved learning was tricked into marrying a fool. The Goddess gave him the gift of speech, and he became Kālidāsa. Which Goddess varies: Kālī, Sarasvatī, or Śyāmalā.

Kālidāsa’s life is almost entirely legend. The best-known story makes him a handsome but simple man, chosen by scheming courtiers as a husband for a proud, learned princess who had humiliated them. When she discovered his ignorance, he turned to the Goddess, received the gift of language, and became the greatest of poets.

The Śyāmalā Daṇḍaka is one of several hymns credited to him by tradition. Its daṇḍaka metre, a long rhythmic flow without the usual verse breaks, makes it thrilling to recite. Scholars generally doubt that the author of the Raghuvaṃśa and Śākuntala wrote it; devotees cherish it regardless.

Following the house rule for hymns, we quote only its opening meditation verse. The rest is widely printed and recorded, and a full verse-by-verse treatment would require a supplied text.

WHERE SHE IS HONOURED

Temples and shrines
Madurai MīnākṣīGreen, holding a parrot: Śrīvidyā writers identify her with Rājamātaṅgī
Lalitā templesMany give Śyāmalā a shrine facing Vārāhī’s
Mātaṅgī shrinesScattered across India, including within Mahāvidyā temple groups
Music schoolsHer picture in many teaching rooms of Carnatic music
HomesStudents’ desks and musicians’ altars

ZAPPY: Mīnākṣī is Śyāmalā?

PROF. BOLT: Śrīvidyā writers say so: the green colour, the parrot, the queenly rule all fit. Madurai's own legend tells her story differently. Both are true in their own way.

Madurai’s Mīnākṣī is green, holds a parrot and rules as queen of the Pāṇḍya kingdom in her temple legend. Śrīvidyā writers naturally read her as Rājamātaṅgī, and some ritual details of the temple reflect Śrīvidyā influence. The temple’s own Tamil legend, however, tells her story as a warrior princess who married Śiva as Sundareśvara.

Mātaṅgī shrines appear within groups of Mahāvidyā temples, which have become more common in recent decades, and in older Śākta centres in Bengal, Assam and Odisha. In Lalitā temples of the South she often has her own sanctum.

The Madurai temple will have its own Śrīvidyā guide in the Temples section of this series.

Smaller Śyāmalā shrines are often found in the homes and schools of musical families, where an image or picture of her with the vīṇā receives a lamp before practice. Several music institutions in Chennai and elsewhere keep her image in their halls during the December music season.

HER DAYS

When she is honoured
Māgha NavarātriThe “hidden” Navarātri of January–February, kept as Śyāmalā Navarātri in the South
Vasanta PañcamīSarasvatī’s spring festival falls in Māgha too; many honour Śyāmalā then
ConcertsMusicians often begin seasons of performance with prayers to her
VijayadaśamīChildren begin learning (vidyārambha) under Sarasvatī and her forms
Our approachFestivals described; her mantras are for initiates

ZAPPY: Two hidden Navarātris, one for each officer?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Āṣāḍha for Vārāhī, Māgha for Śyāmalā. The great autumn one is for the Goddess herself.

The Māgha Navarātri, falling in January–February, is one of the two “hidden” Navarātris and is kept in many southern households and temples as Śyāmalā Navarātri. Students and musicians in particular observe it, with recitation, music and simple offerings.

Vasanta Pañcamī, the spring festival of Sarasvatī, falls in the same lunar month. In some households the two are blended, with Śyāmalā honoured as Sarasvatī’s tantric form.

As with Vārāhī, the inner worship of Śyāmalā belongs to initiation. The festivals, songs and recitations described here belong to everyone.

Navarātri in autumn also honours her indirectly. In many southern homes the ninth day is Sarasvatī Pūjā, when books and instruments are placed before the Goddess and not used for a day; the next morning, Vijayadaśamī, study and practice begin again. Musicians often treat this as Śyāmalā’s day too.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Counsel, beauty, voice
CounselThe wisdom that guides power
BeautyThe arts as a way to the Divine, not a distraction
VoiceSpeech as sacred: the right word heals or wounds
InclusionAs Mātaṅgī, the Goddess in what society rejects
Inner readingThe mind’s intelligence that serves the heart’s queen

ZAPPY: So she's the brains of the operation?

PROF. BOLT: And the voice. If Lalitā is the heart and Vārāhī the will, Śyāmalā is the mind that knows what to say.

Read inwardly, the three figures of Lalitā’s court map onto a person. Lalitā is the deep joy of the Self, Vārāhī the will that guards it, Śyāmalā the intelligence that guides it. A life in which the three are aligned is, in this picture, a well-ruled kingdom.

Śyāmalā also teaches that beauty and art are not luxuries. In her, music and poetry are offices of state, as necessary as the army. A culture that loses its arts, the image suggests, loses its counsel.

And as Mātaṅgī she teaches the widest compassion. The same goddess who counsels the queen sits among those society calls impure, and refuses to accept the distinction.

A last image: the parrot on her hand. A parrot can only repeat what it hears. Teachers sometimes use this to warn that speech without understanding is mere repetition, and to pray that the Goddess turn their parrot-speech into real knowledge.
