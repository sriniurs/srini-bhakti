TIRASKARIṆĪ

The veiling goddess

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · DEITIES

FOREWORD

On a chariot smeared with darkness, drawn by black horses, rides a goddess dark as the tamāla tree, in black armour, with a bow called Mohana. She is Tiraskariṇī, “she who veils”.

On the first day of Lalitā’s war she meets seven boastful demon brothers, blinds them, and destroys them. This guide follows her chapter in the Lalitopākhyāna and asks what her veil means.

ZAPPY: A goddess of hiding? Like an invisibility cloak?

PROF. BOLT: Better. She hides her own side and blinds the enemy. Darkness as a weapon, held by the right hand.

Tiraskariṇī is one of the less famous figures in Lalitā’s army, but her chapter in the Lalitopākhyāna is one of its most vivid. On the first day of the war, seven boastful demon brothers ride out; she meets them on a chariot of darkness, blinds them with a single weapon, and destroys them.

This guide follows that chapter closely, and then looks at what her name and power mean: the veil, the curtain, the hidden, and the protective side of māyā.

Her lineage worship belongs to teachers and is not described.

THE NAME

Veil · curtain · disappearance
TirasAcross, aside, out of sight
TiraskaraṇaHiding, veiling; also setting aside, overcoming
TiraskariṇīThe veiling one; the word also means a curtain or screen
AntardhiDisappearance, concealment: she is the “hidden goddess”
MahāmāyāThe great power of illusion: the Goddess’s veiling power itself
Double senseShe veils what should be hidden, and overcomes what stands in the way

ZAPPY: Her name means ‘curtain’?

PROF. BOLT: A curtain, a screen, a veil. In a theatre, the curtain hides the stage until the right moment. She's that curtain, for the Goddess's work.

Tiraskaraṇa is a common Sanskrit word for hiding, covering or setting aside. Tiraskariṇī, the feminine agent noun, is also an ordinary word for a curtain or screen, the kind used to divide a room or to hide a stage. The Goddess’s officer carries that everyday word as her name.

Her title Antardhi-devatā, “goddess of disappearance”, adds the idea of vanishing from sight. In epic warfare, the power to become invisible was a great advantage, attributed to the most skilled warriors and to the gods.

Mahāmāyā, her other title, connects her to the Goddess’s highest power. In the Devī Māhātmya, Mahāmāyā is the power that veils the world in sleep and delusion. Tiraskariṇī is that power deployed on the battlefield for the Goddess’s side.

HER FORM

How she is described

ArmourA black cuirass
BowNamed Mohana, “bewildering”
ArrowsMohanāstra, serpent-like arrows that confuse
Special weaponAndhāstra: the weapon of blindness
MoodSilent, swift, unseen until she strikes

ZAPPY: Black armour, black chariot, black horses?

PROF. BOLT: She's the night itself, on the Goddess's side. You don't see her coming. That's the point.

The Lalitopākhyāna describes her as dark like the tamāla, a tree with nearly black bark often used in poetry for the colour of Kṛṣṇa’s skin and of rain clouds. She wears black armour and carries a bow called Mohana, “the bewildering”.

Her arrows, Mohanāstra, are described as serpent-like, confusing those they strike. Her decisive weapon is the Andhāstra, the weapon of blindness, which takes away the enemy’s sight.

Images of her outside the text are rare. Lineage illustrations and modern paintings follow the description: a dark warrior-goddess with a bow, often wrapped in shadow.

The Lalitopākhyāna also links her bow Mohana with Vāsantī, a name connected with spring and with the god of love. The bewilderment it causes is the bewilderment of enchantment, the way a spring evening can make a person forget everything else. In her hands, enchantment becomes a weapon against Bhaṇḍa’s commanders.

TAMOLIPTĀ

The chariot of darkness
NameTamoliptā: “smeared with darkness”
KindA vimāna, a flying chariot, facing every direction
HorsesBlack, dark-yoked steeds
RiderShe mounts it as Mahāmāyā, the great veiling power
ContrastLalitā’s Cakrarāja blazes; Tamoliptā is darkness on the move
CompanyOne of many named chariots in the war: Kiricakra, Geyacakra, Cakrarāja

ZAPPY: A chariot made of darkness?

PROF. BOLT: Smeared with it. The ancient poets gave every chariot a character. Hers is night with wheels.

Every major figure in the Lalitopākhyāna war has a named chariot: Lalitā’s Cakrarāja, Mantriṇī’s Geyacakra, Daṇḍanāthā’s Kiricakra. Tiraskariṇī’s is Tamoliptā, “smeared with darkness”, a flying vimāna facing every direction and drawn by black horses.

The image is striking against the brilliance of the other chariots. Cakrarāja is described as blazing with jewels; Tamoliptā is a moving patch of night. The war uses both light and darkness.

The all-facing quality of the chariot suits a goddess of concealment: she can see in every direction while remaining unseen.

THE SEVEN BROTHERS

Her great deed
EnemySeven demon commanders, brothers, led by Balāhaka
Their namesBalāhaka, Sūcīmukha, Phālamukha, Vikarṇa, Vikaṭānana, Karālāyu, Karaṭaka
Their boastEver-victorious, sure of winning
Her moveShe looses the weapon of blindness; they cannot see
ResultTheir weapons fail, their army collapses, and she slays all seven
DayThe first day of the war, Lalitopākhyāna ch. 24

ZAPPY: She blinded them? Isn't that unfair?

PROF. BOLT: They came boasting they'd never lose. She took away the one thing a boastful fighter relies on: seeing the field. Fair in war, and a lesson besides.

After Aśvārūḍhā and Sampatkarī have killed Bhaṇḍa’s first generals, and Nakulī has defeated the next five, Bhaṇḍa sends seven brothers, sons of the demoness Kīkasā, led by Balāhaka. They are said to be always victorious and mutually supporting, and Bhaṇḍa is confident.

Tiraskariṇī rides out at the Goddess’s command. She releases the weapon of blindness, and the brothers cannot see; their weapons are stunned and their army falls apart. She then kills them one by one.

Commentators enjoy the fitness of the victory. The brothers’ name-leader, Balāhaka, means “cloud”. A goddess of darkness defeats an army of clouds by plunging them into a deeper darkness. Their confidence in always winning made them blind to danger long before her weapon struck.

IN THE ARMY

CirclePart of Vārāhī’s sphere: the guardian, protective side of the army
RoleConcealing the Goddess’s forces and bewildering the enemy
ComradesNakulī, Sarpiṇī’s foe, and other Śaktis who fight on the first day
Name in some lineagesSometimes honoured as Tiraskariṇī Vārāhī

ZAPPY: So she works for Vārāhī?

PROF. BOLT: Under her direction in the story. Some lineages even join their names. The general has her own special forces, and Tiraskariṇī is one.

The Lalitopākhyāna describes Tiraskariṇī as acting at the Goddess’s command and under the direction of Daṇḍanāthā, Vārāhī the general. Some lineages go further and honour her as Tiraskariṇī Vārāhī, joining the two names.

This places her in the guardian, protective side of the Goddess’s forces, alongside the many Śaktis who serve Vārāhī. Where Vārāhī commands with the rod, Tiraskariṇī protects with the veil.

Other goddesses fight on the same first day: Nakulī, whose name links her to the mongoose, defeats five generals and the serpent-demoness Sarpiṇī. The Nityā goddesses repel a night attack. The first day is a parade of the Goddess’s varied powers.

Vārāhī and Tiraskariṇī make a natural pair. The general keeps order by force and discipline; the veiling goddess protects by concealment and confusion of the enemy. Together they guard the outer edges of the Goddess’s army while Lalitā and Śyāmalā hold the centre.

THE POWER OF VEILING

Māyā as a protector
MāyāThe Goddess’s power to veil and to show
Usual senseMāyā hides the truth from us
Tiraskariṇī’s twistHere the veil hides the good from harm
Five actsCreating, keeping, dissolving, veiling, revealing: veiling is a divine act
LessonNot everything should be shown to everyone at every moment

ZAPPY: I thought māyā was a bad thing, the illusion that traps us?

PROF. BOLT: It's a power. Pointed one way, it confuses. Pointed the other, it protects. Tiraskariṇī is māyā on your side.

Māyā in Indian philosophy is usually discussed as a problem: the power that makes the one reality appear as many, and keeps beings from seeing the truth. In Śākta thought, māyā is the Goddess’s own power, and like all her powers it can be turned to grace.

Śaiva and Śākta theology lists five divine acts: creation, maintenance, dissolution, veiling and revealing. Veiling is a divine act, not a mistake. It lets the world exist as a world, and it lets seeds grow in the dark before they show themselves.

Tiraskariṇī is that veiling power on the side of the devotee. She hides the good from harm and blinds the forces that would destroy it.

There is also a gentle example from daily life. A parent does not tell a small child every danger and sorrow in the world; some things are veiled until the child is ready. That veiling is love, not deception. Tiraskariṇī’s māyā is of that kind.

VEILS IN WORSHIP

Curtains, screens and covered shrines
Temple curtainMany shrines draw a curtain while the deity is dressed or fed
PurposeThe sacred has its own privacy
DarśanaWhen the curtain opens, the sight of the deity is a gift
In practiceLineages honour Tiraskariṇī as the veil that guards worship
Our approachWe describe the idea; the rite belongs to teachers

ZAPPY: So when the temple priest closes the curtain, that's her idea?

PROF. BOLT: The same idea. The curtain isn't hiding something wrong. It's guarding something precious until it's ready to be seen.

Temples across India use curtains. The sanctum is screened while the deity is bathed, dressed or offered food, and opened for darśana. The opening of the curtain is a moment of great emotion for devotees.

Śrīvidyā lineages honour Tiraskariṇī as the veil that guards worship itself, and some include her in their practice for this purpose. The details are given by teachers and are not described here.

Even without those details, the idea is familiar to anyone who has stood before a closed shrine and waited. The veil makes the revealing precious.

In Kerala and Tamil Nadu, some temple deities are kept behind a curtain for most of the day and shown only at fixed times, which makes each darśana an event. Devotees who wait for the curtain to open experience exactly the tension between hiding and revealing that Tiraskariṇī embodies.

THE HIDDEN AND THE SECRET

Why some things are kept
Secret mantrasThe tradition’s guarded side follows the same logic
Not shameKept, not because they are wrong, but because they are tender
Seed imageA seed is buried in the dark to grow
ProtectionPractice is shielded from mockery, distraction and misuse
This seriesOur own rule, open and guarded, is a small act of Tiraskariṇī

ZAPPY: So the rule about not printing mantras is her rule?

PROF. BOLT: In a way. She'd approve. Some things grow better out of sight.

This series has a rule: it explains names, stories, philosophy, geometry and temples, and it does not reproduce initiatory mantras or procedures. That rule is, in a small way, Tiraskariṇī’s logic at work. Some things are kept, not because they are shameful, but because they are tender and need care.

The tradition often uses the image of a seed: buried in the dark, it grows; dug up repeatedly to be looked at, it dies. Practice, in this view, needs some shelter from the gaze of the curious and the mocking.

Openness has its own value, and most of this series is open. The guarded part is small, and Tiraskariṇī is its guardian.

Readers sometimes ask whether this makes the tradition secretive in a bad sense. The difference is in what is hidden and why. Śrīvidyā keeps a few specific practices for initiation, while its stories, philosophy, hymns and temples are open to all, as this whole series shows.

WHAT SHE MEANS

Darkness as a friend
ProtectionA veil around the good
HumilityThe hidden helper who wants no credit
Blindness for the proudThe boastful lose sight of the field
RestNight and darkness as shelter, not only fear
Inner readingThe discretion that guards one’s inner life from being spilled out

ZAPPY: Darkness isn't always bad?

PROF. BOLT: Not with her. Night lets you sleep, hides you from danger, lets seeds grow. She's the kind dark.

Darkness has a bad reputation in most religious imagery, standing for ignorance and evil. Tiraskariṇī offers another view: darkness as shelter, as night that brings rest, as shade that protects, as the soil where seeds grow.

She also teaches the virtue of the hidden helper. Her great deed comes in a short chapter, and she seeks no fame. Many of the most important helpers in any life are like that.

Inwardly, teachers read her as discretion: the capacity to guard one’s inner life, not to spill every experience and insight outward, and to let what is growing grow in peace.

Her chapter also carries a warning about pride. The seven brothers are described as always victorious, and their confidence makes them careless. Blindness comes to them in two stages: first the blindness of arrogance, then the blindness of her weapon. The second only completes the first.

WHAT SHE IS NOT

Clearing up mix-ups
Not a goddess of deceitShe veils to protect, not to cheat
Not for invisibility tricksOnline claims of “invisibility mantras” are outside the tradition
Not evil because darkDark colour marks depth and protection
Not separate from the GoddessMahāmāyā: the Goddess’s own veiling power
Not do-it-yourselfHer lineage worship is given by teachers

ZAPPY: Someone online says her mantra makes you invisible…

PROF. BOLT: It doesn't, and the tradition never says it does. Her ‘invisibility’ is protection for the worshipper's inner life.

Online sources sometimes present Tiraskariṇī as a source of “invisibility” for worldly ends. The Lalitopākhyāna presents her as a warrior-goddess of the Goddess’s army, and lineages as a guardian of worship. Neither offers tricks.

She is also sometimes painted as sinister because she is dark and works through concealment. In her own story she conceals the good and blinds the boastful forces of Bhaṇḍa. That is protection, not deceit.

As with all deities in this series, her lineage worship belongs to teachers.

A simple test for any account of her: does it lead to protecting what is good, or to hiding wrongdoing? The first is Tiraskariṇī; the second is the opposite of everything she stands for.
