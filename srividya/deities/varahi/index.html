ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

VĀRĀHĪ

Commander of the Goddess’s army

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · DEITIES

FOREWORD

At the head of Lalitā’s army rides a dark goddess with a boar’s face, holding a plough and a pestle. She is Vārāhī, called Daṇḍanāthā, “she who bears the rod”: the commander-in-chief of the Goddess’s forces.

She has two lives. Long before Śrīvidyā she was one of the seven Mothers who fight beside Durgā. In Śrīvidyā she becomes Lalitā’s general. This guide follows both: her names, form, war, festivals, temples and meaning.

Śrī Vārāhyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

PROF. BOLT: Only if you're Bhaṇḍa. To her devotees she's the fiercest mother there is.

ZAPPY: A boar-faced goddess? Isn't that strange next to beautiful Lalitā?

PROF. BOLT: Every queen needs a general who frightens the enemy. Vārāhī is that general. And she is fiercely loyal.

Vārāhī is one of the most powerful and most beloved of the fierce goddesses, and in Śrīvidyā she holds one of the two highest offices in Lalitā’s court. Where Lalitā is beauty enthroned, Vārāhī is that beauty’s armed guard: dark, tusked, riding at the head of the army.

She also has a life outside Śrīvidyā, much older, as one of the seven Mothers who appear in the Devī Māhātmya and in temple sculpture across India. This guide follows both lives and shows how they join.

As with every guide in this series, we describe her names, form, stories, temples and meaning. Her mantras and the rites of her worship belong to initiation and are left out.

WHO IS VĀRĀHĪ?

The Mother with the boar’s face
NameVārāhī, “of the boar” (varāha)
OriginThe power (śakti) of Varāha, Viṣṇu’s boar avatāra
In the PurāṇasOne of the Mātṛkās, the Mothers who fight beside Durgā
In ŚrīvidyāDaṇḍanāthā, commander of the army of Lalitā
RankWith Śyāmalā the minister, one of Lalitā’s two chief officers
CharacterFierce to enemies, protective to devotees

ZAPPY: So she started as Viṣṇu's boar?

PROF. BOLT: As his power. In the Mother stories, each god's śakti takes his shape and goes to war. Vārāhī is Varāha's.

The Mātṛkās, the Mothers, are a very old group. They appear in sculpture from the early centuries CE and in the Purāṇas as the powers of the great gods taking female form. Vārāhī, the boar-power, is among the most consistently present, and one of the few to grow an independent cult of her own.

In Śrīvidyā she becomes something more: not just one of a group but the general of the Goddess’s forces, second in rank only to Lalitā and standing level with Śyāmalā, the minister. The Lalitopākhyāna describes the two as Lalitā’s right and left hands.

Her character combines a terrifying exterior with intense loyalty and affection for her devotees. Many people who worship her describe her less as a frightening goddess than as a fiercely protective mother.

ZAPPY: Why twelve names?

PROF. BOLT: The Lalitopākhyāna gives them as a little hymn. Each name is a face of her: the boar, the general, the keeper of the code, the enemy-slayer.

The twelve names come from a passage in which the gods praise Daṇḍanāthā before the war. They are often recited by her devotees as a short hymn, and several of them appear in the names of her shrines and festivals.

The names fall into groups. Some describe her form (Vārāhī, Potriṇī). Some her office (Daṇḍanāthā, Mahāsenā, Arighnī). Some, like Saṅketā and Samayeśvarī, point to her role as keeper of the code of the tradition, the samaya, which an initiate promises to keep. Vārtālī is often linked by commentators to vārtā, news or livelihood, and so to agriculture.

Ājñācakreśvarī connects her to the subtle body. The ājñā cakra, between the eyebrows, is the place of command and inner sight, and naming the commander as its ruler makes her the guardian of the mind’s highest outpost.

A practical note: many temples and printed hymn books open her worship with these twelve names, and devotees who know nothing else about her often know them. Because they come from the Purāṇa and not from an initiatory text, they belong to the open side of her worship, and we give them in full for that reason.

HER FORM IN ŚRĪVIDYĀ

How she is pictured
FaceA boar’s, with tusks; three eyes
BodyDark, like a rain-cloud or deep blue
DressRed, with a crown and ornaments
HandsUsually eight: plough and pestle, noose and goad, conch and discus, fear-not and boon gestures
Plough and pestleHer signature weapons: the farmer’s tools turned to war
MountA lion, buffalo or boar; descriptions differ

ZAPPY: A plough? As a weapon?

PROF. BOLT: Balarāma uses one too. The plough digs up roots, the pestle crushes. She uproots and crushes everything that stands against her devotee.

The plough and pestle are unusual weapons for a goddess, and they mark Vārāhī out. The plough links her to Balarāma, Kṛṣṇa’s elder brother, who also carries one, and to agriculture more broadly. The pestle, used to pound grain, becomes a club.

Many images give her the conch and discus as well, the emblems of Viṣṇu, reminding the viewer that she is Varāha’s power. The noose and goad, Lalitā’s own weapons, show that she acts with her queen’s authority.

Her dark colour is often called “like a rain cloud” or “like a blue lotus”. In Indian imagery dark-blue gods, Kṛṣṇa, Rāma, Viṣṇu, are protectors, and the colour places her among them.

Some descriptions add other weapons in place of the conch and discus: a sword and shield, or a bow and arrows. The constants are the boar face, the dark body and, in the Śrīvidyā images, the plough and pestle. When you meet an eight-armed, boar-faced goddess holding farm tools, you can be sure it is Vārāhī.

AS ONE OF THE MOTHERS

Vārāhī in the Devī Māhātmya
The storyThe gods send their powers to help the Goddess fight Śumbha and Niśumbha
Her entryVārāhī comes in the form of Varāha, the sacrificial boar
In battleShe tears the asuras with her tusks and snout, against Raktabīja’s army
The sevenBrāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī, Indrāṇī, Cāmuṇḍā
ImagesMātṛkā panels in temples across India, often with Gaṇeśa and Vīrabhadra

ZAPPY: So she's in the Devī Māhātmya too?

PROF. BOLT: Chapter 8. She fights beside the Mothers. Śrīvidyā later gives her a promotion: commander of the whole army.

On this site: Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts

In the eighth chapter of the Devī Māhātmya, as the battle against Śumbha and Niśumbha grows desperate, the powers of the gods emerge from their bodies in forms like theirs. Vārāhī comes as the boar, and the Mothers fight beside Caṇḍikā against Raktabīja, whose every drop of blood becomes a new demon.

The list of seven Mothers is the most common, though eight and more appear in some traditions, with Nārasiṃhī or Mahālakṣmī added. In temple sculpture they usually sit in a row, each with her husband’s mount and emblems, flanked by Vīrabhadra and Gaṇeśa.

Many villages in South India still worship the Seven Mothers as protectors of the boundary. Vārāhī’s Śrīvidyā role as the army’s general grows naturally out of this old guardian function.

THE BOAR AND THE EARTH

Why a boar?
VarāhaViṣṇu as a boar dives into the cosmic ocean and lifts the Earth on his tusks
EarthSo Vārāhī carries the power that holds up the world
DiggingA boar roots up what is hidden: she uncovers and destroys hidden enemies
FarmingPlough and pestle are tools of the harvest: she also feeds and nourishes
SacrificeVarāha is called the “sacrifice-boar”, his body made of Vedic rites

ZAPPY: So she's about the earth as well as war?

PROF. BOLT: Both. The same tusks that lift the earth tear the demon. Protecting and nourishing are two sides of one strength.

Varāha is one of the ten avatāras of Viṣṇu. When a demon dragged the Earth into the depths of the cosmic ocean, Viṣṇu took the form of a vast boar, dived, killed the demon and lifted the Earth on his tusks. The Daśāvatāra guides on this site tell the story in full.

The Varāha myth carries old Vedic ideas: the boar is called yajña-varāha, the sacrifice-boar, because his body is made of the parts of the Vedic ritual. So Vārāhī carries a double heritage, the earth and the sacrifice, alongside her war role.

This helps explain her plough and pestle. A goddess of the earth naturally bears farming tools; a goddess of war turns them into weapons. In village worship she is still often asked for good harvests as well as protection.

Varāha himself has a famous Karnataka connection: the Varāha emblem was the royal seal of the Cāḷukyas and later of the Vijayanagara kings, stamped on their coins and grants. A kingdom that took the boar as its sign was claiming to hold up the earth as he did, and his śakti, Vārāhī, was a natural guardian for such kings.

Daṇḍinī in the field
RoleCommander-in-chief (senānāthā) of the Śakti army
ChariotKiricakra, a towering chariot with boar-emblems, said to be drawn by boars
Her foeViśukra, Bhaṇḍa’s brother, whom she kills
Her partnerMantriṇī (Śyāmalā) kills the other brother, Viṣaṅga
Lalitā’s joyThe Sahasranāma praises Lalitā as delighted by Vārāhī’s valour
MoreTold in Part 4 of the Lalitopākhyāna

ZAPPY: Does Lalitā fight too?

PROF. BOLT: At the very end, against Bhaṇḍa himself. Before that, her officers carry the war. Vārāhī leads from the front.

In the Lalitopākhyāna the war against Bhaṇḍa is described like an epic battle, with armies, generals, chariots and named weapons. Daṇḍanāthā leads the main force on her chariot Kiricakra, while Mantriṇī rides Geyacakra, and Lalitā herself rides Cakrarāja, the king of chariots.

Each chariot is described as having many levels, with deities stationed on each. Commentators have read the three chariots as related to the Śrīcakra itself, so the battle doubles as a map of the yantra in motion.

Vārāhī’s killing of Viśukra and Śyāmalā’s killing of Viṣaṅga form a pair. The two brothers of Bhaṇḍa fall to the Goddess’s two chief officers, leaving only Bhaṇḍa himself for Lalitā.

ZAPPY: Her name is in Lalitā's hymn?

PROF. BOLT: Twice. The Goddess's own thousand names stop to praise her general. That's how much the tradition honours her.

kiricakrarathārūḍhadaṇḍanāthāpuraskṛtā
viśukraprāṇaharaṇavārāhīvīryananditā

She who is honoured by Daṇḍanāthā riding the Kiricakra chariot. She who is delighted by the valour of Vārāhī, who took Viśukra’s life. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

The Lalitā Sahasranāma’s war section reads like a summary of the Lalitopākhyāna. In a few dozen names it tells the whole battle: the armies, the chariots, the fire-wall, Bālā’s victory over Bhaṇḍa’s sons, Gaṇeśa’s destruction of the obstacle-yantra, and the officers’ triumphs.

The names honouring Daṇḍanāthā and Vārāhī stand out because they praise someone other than Lalitā. The Goddess is described as pleased by her general, as a queen is proud of a loyal commander. Bhāskararāya explains these names with references to the Lalitopākhyāna.

For devotees who know the Sahasranāma by heart, these two names are a doorway to Vārāhī: chanting them, many say, is already a salute to her.

WHAT SHE MEANS

The rod, the code and the will
DaṇḍaThe rod: law, order, just punishment
SamayaTwo of her names call her keeper of the code: the discipline of the path
ĀjñācakreśvarīRuler of the ājñā cakra, the centre of command between the brows
ProtectionDevotees turn to her against enemies, fear and injustice
Inner readingThe will that guards the mind’s kingdom from its own demons

ZAPPY: Ājñā means command, right? Like a general's orders?

PROF. BOLT: Good catch. The cakra of command, ruled by the commander. The tradition loves a pun like that.

Daṇḍa, the rod, is a central idea of Indian political thought. The king’s rod maintains order; without it, the strong devour the weak, as big fish eat small ones. Making Vārāhī the bearer of the rod makes her the guarantee of order in the Goddess’s kingdom.

Samaya, the code, links her to the inner discipline of the tradition. A practitioner is bound by rules of conduct; Vārāhī is their guardian. This is why some teachers say she protects the student from their own lapses as much as from outer enemies.

Inwardly, many writers read her as the will. Lalitā is the heart’s joy; Śyāmalā is the mind’s wisdom; Vārāhī is the will that defends both. Without her, the mind’s kingdom is overrun.

HER DAYS AND SEASON

When she is honoured
Āṣāḍha NavarātriNine nights in Āṣāḍha (June–July), kept for Vārāhī in the South
ThanjavurThe Vārāhī shrine at the Bṛhadīśvara temple holds a great Āṣāḍha festival
PañcamīThe fifth lunar day, linked to her name Pañcamī
Time of dayTraditionally worshipped at night, a sign of her protective, guardian role
Our approachWe describe the festivals; her mantras and rites are for initiates

ZAPPY: A Navarātri just for her?

PROF. BOLT: A quieter one, in the monsoon month. In Thanjavur it's grand: the goddess is decorated with vegetables, fruits and flowers on different days.

There are four Navarātris in the year in some traditions: the well-known ones in Āśvina (autumn) and Caitra (spring), and two quieter “hidden” ones (gupta Navarātri) in Āṣāḍha and Māgha. In the South, the Āṣāḍha one is widely kept for Vārāhī.

At Thanjavur’s Bṛhadīśvara temple, the Āṣāḍha Navarātri for Vārāhī is a major festival, with the goddess decorated differently each day: with turmeric, sandal, vegetables, fruits, flowers and finally butter. Crowds come for the decorations as much as for the worship.

Her night worship and her rites are, in the tradition, matters for initiates. Ordinary devotees visit her shrines, offer lamps and flowers, chant her names, and ask for protection; that is the open side of her worship.

ZAPPY: Why can you only see her through a hole in Varanasi?

PROF. BOLT: That's how the temple keeps her: veiled, powerful, glimpsed rather than stared at. Many fierce goddesses are kept that way.

The Vārāhī Deula at Chaurasi, near Puri, is one of the finest goddess temples of Odisha. Its main image shows Vārāhī seated in royal ease, pot-bellied, holding a fish and a skull-cup, with her buffalo mount below. The fish links her to the coast and to local tantric traditions.

In Varanasi, Vārāhī’s shrine stands near the ghats. The image is in a chamber below ground; devotees view her through two openings in the floor of the upper room, one showing her face and one her feet, and only in the early morning hours.

At Thanjavur, her shrine stands within the great Bṛhadīśvara temple complex. A popular tradition holds that the Cōḻa king Rājarāja worshipped her before every campaign, and that no major work in the temple begins without honouring her first.

IN ART

How sculptors saw her
EarlyGupta-period and later Mātṛkā sets show her seated, boar-headed, often holding a child
OdishaChaurasi’s Vārāhī holds a fish, linking her to the sea and to tantric worship there
SouthCōḻa bronzes and stone images show her with plough and pestle
PaintingsTanjore and Mysore paintings show her in Lalitā’s court, on the Kiricakra
TodayCalendar prints show her dark, crowned, eight-armed and seated on a lion or buffalo

ZAPPY: A boar-headed mother holding a baby?

PROF. BOLT: In the oldest images, yes. Before she was a general, she was a Mother. The fierceness and the tenderness were always together.

The earliest Mātṛkā images, from the Kuṣāṇa and Gupta periods, show the Mothers as nurturing figures, often holding infants. Vārāhī in these sets is boar-headed but gentle, a reminder that her title “Mother” is not ironic.

Medieval temple sculpture develops her fiercer side. In Odisha she gains the fish and skull-cup; in the South, Cōḻa artists give her plough and pestle and set her among the Mothers in temple niches. Each region’s Vārāhī looks a little different.

Śrīvidyā paintings, especially from Tanjore and Mysore, place her in Lalitā’s court, standing or riding at the Goddess’s side. These are the images most modern calendar prints follow.

A good place to see a full set of Mothers is a museum gallery of early medieval sculpture, where Mātṛkā panels from ruined temples are often displayed in a row. Seeing Vārāhī beside Brāhmī, Vaiṣṇavī and the others makes clear how carefully sculptors gave each Mother her husband’s features, and how distinct Vārāhī’s boar head makes her among them.

WHAT VĀRĀHĪ IS NOT

Clearing up mix-ups
Not a demonessShe is fierce, never evil: fierceness aimed at what harms
Not separate from LalitāHer officer and, in the tradition’s view, her own power in a warrior form
Not for harming othersHer protection is invoked against injustice, not to attack people
Not only ŚāktaVaiṣṇavas honour her as Varāha’s power; Śaivas among the Mothers
Not a do-it-yourself cultHer deeper worship is learned in a lineage, not from social media

ZAPPY: People online say she gives power over enemies…

PROF. BOLT: She protects. But the tradition is firm: you pray for protection, not for someone else's ruin.

Because Vārāhī is fierce and worshipped at night, she attracts lurid stories, especially online. The tradition itself is clear: she protects devotees and punishes injustice, and her worship is not a tool for harming others. Texts that discuss such uses class them as outside the path.

She is also sometimes presented as a separate, rival goddess. In Śrīvidyā she is Lalitā’s officer and, in a deeper sense, Lalitā’s own power in martial form. To honour Vārāhī is to honour Lalitā.

Finally, a word on teachers. Vārāhī’s deeper worship is given within lineages. Anyone drawn to it should seek a teacher in a recognised line rather than a course sold online.

The best summary of her nature may be the image of a mother boar. In the wild, a sow with young is among the most dangerous animals in the forest: gentle with her litter, ferocious to anything that threatens it. Devotees who speak of Vārāhī as a mother describe her in exactly those terms.

Mātṛkā has two meanings in Śākta texts: the Mother goddesses, and the letters of the alphabet, which are also called “little mothers” because all words are born from them. The two meanings meet in the idea that the Mothers are the powers within speech itself.

Pronunciation, roughly: Vārāhī “vaa-raa-hee”; Daṇḍanāthā “dun-da-naa-thaa”, with the retroflex ḍ made with the tongue curled back; Kiricakra “ki-ri-chuk-ra”. Potriṇī is “po-tri-nee”, with a retroflex ṇ.
