NAVARĀTRI AND MYSURU DASARĀ

Nine nights of the Goddess, and a city that keeps them like no other

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Navarātri is the Goddess’s great festival, and in Śrīvidyā every goddess of the nine nights is Lalitā. This guide shows how Śrīvidyā devotees keep it, and then turns to Mysuru, where Dasarā has been kept as a royal festival since 1610.

We follow the festival from Vijayanagara’s Mahānavamī to Rāja Wodeyar, up Chamundi Hill to Cāmuṇḍeśvarī, into the palace for the golden throne and Āyudha Pūjā, and out with the Goddess on the Jamboo Savari. Along the way we meet a Maharaja who signed his songs “Śrīvidyā”.

Śrī Cāmuṇḍeśvaryai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why does Mysuru get a whole guide?

PROF. BOLT: Because nowhere else does a whole city, and a royal house, keep the nine nights like this.

ZAPPY: Mysuru Dasarā in a Śrīvidyā series?

PROF. BOLT: It belongs here, Zappy. The Goddess on the hill, the king as her servant, and a Maharaja who was a Śrīvidyā upāsaka.

Navarātri is one of the great festivals of the Goddess, kept all over India in different ways. For a Śrīvidyā devotee it is above all a time to worship Lalitā, the Goddess who is all goddesses.

In Karnataka, and above all in Mysuru, it is something more: a royal festival with an unbroken history of four centuries, centred on Cāmuṇḍeśvarī, the Goddess of Chamundi Hill. This guide covers both: the Śrīvidyā way of keeping Navarātri, and the story of Mysuru Dasarā from Vijayanagara to today.

Mysuru has a special link with this series. Its last ruling Maharaja, Jayacāmarājendra Wodeyar, was initiated into Śrīvidyā and signed every one of his songs with the word “Śrīvidyā”. Those songs are still played in the palace at Navarātri.

NINE NIGHTS

What Navarātri is
MeaningNava-rātri, nine nights; the tenth day is Vijayadaśamī
Four a yearSpring (Caitra), autumn (Āśvina) and two quieter ones in Āṣāḍha and Māgha; the autumn one is the great one
Core textThe Devī Māhātmya (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa): the Goddess kills Mahiṣa, Caṇḍa, Muṇḍa, Śumbha and Niśumbha
Night 9Mahānavamī: worship of weapons and tools (Āyudha Pūjā)
Day 10Vijayadaśamī: victory day; worship of the śamī tree; new learning begins
Also RāmaIn much of India the tenth day also marks Rāma’s victory over Rāvaṇa

ZAPPY: Why nights and not days?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess’s great battles are fought in the dark, and lamps and vigils belong to the night. The day of victory comes after.

The Devī Māhātmya, seven hundred verses from the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, is the core scripture of Navarātri across India. It tells three great battles: against Madhu and Kaiṭabha, against the buffalo demon Mahiṣa, and against Śumbha and Niśumbha with their generals.

Reciting it during the nine nights, in whole or in part, is one of the most widespread Navarātri practices. Many homes also set up a sacred pot (kalaśa) on the first day and sow grain that sprouts by the ninth.

The ninth day, Mahānavamī, gave its name to the Vijayanagara festival. The tenth, Vijayadaśamī, is the day of victory, and the day children are often started on their letters.

WHICH GODDESS?

Many names, one Mother
Common patternThree nights each for Durgā, Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī
North IndiaNine forms, Śailaputrī to Siddhidātrī, one per night
BengalDurgā Pūjā, the Goddess visiting her parents’ home
KarnatakaCāmuṇḍeśvarī at Mysuru, Śāradā at Śṛṅgeri, and many village goddesses
ŚrīvidyāAll of them are Lalitā; the Lalitā Sahasranāma itself names her Durgā (śloka 50)
Key ideaThe fierce and the gentle are one Goddess in different moods

ZAPPY: Lalitā fought Bhaṇḍa, not Mahiṣa. Does that matter?

PROF. BOLT: Not to a Śrīvidyā devotee. Two stories, one Goddess. Lalitā’s war is the inner one; Cāmuṇḍā’s is the same victory told another way.

Different regions give each night a different form of the Goddess, but the idea underneath is the same: one Mother appearing in many ways for the good of the world.

In Śrīvidyā this is a matter of doctrine. Lalitā Tripurasundarī is the supreme Goddess; Durgā, Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī, Kālī and Cāmuṇḍā are her forms or powers. The Lalitā Sahasranāma itself includes the name Durgā in its fiftieth śloka.

So a Śrīvidyā devotee can stand before Cāmuṇḍeśvarī on Chamundi Hill, killing the buffalo demon with her trident, and see in her the same Goddess who sits smiling on the five-Brahma couch in the Śrīcakra.

NAVARĀTRI THE ŚRĪVIDYĀ WAY

What devotees do
RecitationLalitā Sahasranāma, Triśatī and Khaḍgamālā, often every day of the nine
MusicDīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, one song for each enclosure of the Śrīcakra
Suvāsinī pūjāHonouring married women as the Goddess; done daily at Śṛṅgeri
Kanyā pūjāHonouring young girls as Bālā, the child form of Lalitā
Day 5Lalitā Pañcamī falls inside the nine nights
What we leave outInitiated rites of the Śrīcakra are taught by a guru; this guide only describes them

ZAPPY: Nine nights, nine enclosures. Coincidence?

PROF. BOLT: A happy one. Many singers take one Navāvaraṇa kṛti a night, walking inward from the square gate to the central point.

In Śrīvidyā homes, Navarātri is often a time of steady recitation: the Lalitā Sahasranāma each day, with the Triśatī and the Khaḍgamālā. Each of these has its own verse-by-verse guide in this series.

Dīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, nine songs on the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra with an opening and a closing song, are closely tied to Navarātri. In Bengaluru and Mysuru they are often sung in groups during Dasarā.

Honouring a married woman (suvāsinī) or a young girl (kanyā) as the Goddess is common in Navarātri. For Śrīvidyā, the girl recalls Bālā, Lalitā’s nine-year-old daughter-form, who fought in the war against Bhaṇḍa.

ŚṚṄGERI

Śāradā’s nine nights
StartMahābhiṣeka to Śāradā on the new-moon day before Navarātri
AlaṅkārasA new form each day: on the swan, the peacock, the bull, Garuḍa, Rājarājeśvarī, Vīṇā Śāradā and more
Jagat PrasūtikāOne form shows the world as a baby in Śāradā’s arms
The DurbarEach night the Jagadguru holds a durbar in royal dress
Its originThe maṭha dates the durbar to the founding of Vijayanagara in the 14th century
CloseMahābhiṣeka on the full moon, then a float festival on the river

ZAPPY: A monk on a throne?

PROF. BOLT: Only at Navarātri. The maṭha says the custom goes back to Vijayanagara, the same royal tradition that Mysuru inherited.

Navarātri is the greatest festival of the year at Śṛṅgeri, where the Goddess is Śāradā, Sarasvatī as the giver of knowledge. Each day her festival image is dressed in a new form, and devotees come to see the day’s alaṅkāra.

The forms include the Seven Mothers (Brāhmī on the swan, Kaumārī on the peacock, Māheśvarī on the bull, Vaiṣṇavī on Garuḍa and others), Rājarājeśvarī, Vīṇā Śāradā and Jagat Prasūtikā, the mother holding the universe as a baby.

Every night of the festival the Jagadguru holds a durbar in royal dress. The maṭha links this custom to the founding of Vijayanagara in the 14th century, which ties Śṛṅgeri’s Navarātri to the same royal line of tradition that Mysuru later inherited.

THE MAHĀNAVAMĪ OF VIJAYANAGARA

Where it all began
CapitalVijayanagara (Hampi), 14th–16th centuries
Abdur RazzaqPersian envoy, 1440s: hundreds of elephants on parade
Domingo PaesPortuguese visitor around 1520, under Kṛṣṇadevarāya: a “great feast of nine days”
House of VictoryPaes’s name for the platform now called the Mahānavamī Dibba
PurposeWorship of the Goddess, and a display of royal power before nobles from across the empire
TodayThe carved platform still stands in Hampi’s Royal Enclosure

ZAPPY: So Mysuru copied Hampi?

PROF. BOLT: Inherited is the better word. When Vijayanagara fell, its chiefs carried its customs with them, and the Wodeyars kept this one alive.

The great Navarātri of Vijayanagara is known to us mainly through foreign visitors. Abdur Razzaq, an envoy from Persia in the 1440s, described the crowds and the hundreds of elephants. Domingo Paes, a Portuguese visitor around 1520, described nine days of feasts, music, dance and parades.

Paes called the great platform from which the king watched the festival the “House of Victory”. Historians identify it with the Mahānavamī Dibba, a stepped stone platform covered with carvings of processions, hunters, dancers and musicians.

For the empire, the festival did two things at once. It was worship of the Goddess, and it was a review of the kingdom’s power, with nobles coming from distant provinces to pay homage. Mysuru Dasarā still carries both.

1610: THE WODEYARS BEGIN

From Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa to Mysuru
FounderRāja Wodeyar I (ruled 1578–1617)
1610He took Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa from the Vijayanagara governor and held Dasarā there
WhyTo mark the kingdom’s new independence, while still honouring the emperor
1610–1799Dasarā was held at Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa, the capital
1799After Tipu Sultan fell, the capital and the festival moved to Mysuru
First in MysuruPresided over by the boy king Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III, then about five

ZAPPY: A five-year-old ran Dasarā?

PROF. BOLT: With his grandmother and his minister beside him, yes. He grew into the king who shaped much of Mysuru’s culture.

Rāja Wodeyar I was a chief under Vijayanagara who made Mysuru independent as the empire weakened. In 1610 he took the fort of Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa on the Kāverī from the Vijayanagara governor and made it his capital.

That year he held Dasarā there in the Vijayanagara manner. The traditional account adds that he worshipped at a banni tree outside the town. From then on, the Wodeyars kept the festival every year, so that 2026 saw the 417th procession.

After Tipu Sultan’s death in 1799, the British restored the Wodeyars and the capital returned to Mysuru. The first Dasarā in Mysuru city was presided over by the child king Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III.

CĀMUṆḌEŚVARĪ AND MAHIṢA

Why Mysuru is Mysuru
The demonMahiṣāsura, the buffalo demon, ruled from this hill
The nameMahiṣūru, “Mahiṣa’s town”, became Mysuru
The battleThe Goddess fought him and killed him; Mysuru remembers it on this hill
CāmuṇḍāIn the Devī Māhātmya, Kālī kills Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa and is named Cāmuṇḍā
Śakti PīṭhaCounted among the 18 great pīṭhas as Krauñca Pīṭha, where Satī’s hair fell
On the hillA painted statue of Mahiṣāsura greets visitors near the top

ZAPPY: A city named after the demon?

PROF. BOLT: After the place of his defeat, really. Every time someone says “Mysuru”, the victory is remembered.

In the Devī Māhātmya, Mahiṣa is a buffalo demon who drives the gods from heaven. The gods’ combined anger takes the form of the Goddess, armed with all their weapons, who kills him. Mysuru tradition places this battle on Chamundi Hill.

The name Cāmuṇḍā comes from another episode of the same text. The Goddess, as Kālī, kills the generals Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa and is given the name Cāmuṇḍā. At Mysuru, Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is worshipped as Mahiṣāsuramardinī, the slayer of Mahiṣa.

The temple is also counted among the eighteen great Śakti Pīṭhas, in the traditional verse list ascribed to Śaṅkara. There it is called Krauñca Pīṭha, said to mark where the hair of Satī fell.

THE TEMPLE ON THE HILL

Chamundi Hill
HeightAbout 1,060 m, 13 km from the city
OriginsAn older shrine, often dated to the Hoysala period (12th century)
1659Dodda Devarāja Wodeyar built the stone stairway of about 1,000 steps
1664He installed the great granite Nandi on the way up
1827Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III renovated the temple and built the seven-tiered gopuram
InsideStatues of Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III and his queens stand before the Goddess

ZAPPY: Do people still climb all the steps?

PROF. BOLT: Many do, especially in Āṣāḍha month and at Dasarā. Some put turmeric and kuṅkuma on each step as they go.

The temple crowns the main peak of the Chamundi Hills, east of the city. Rulers of Mysuru have patronised it for centuries, and the name of the hill itself comes from the Goddess.

Dodda Devarāja Wodeyar built the stone stairway in 1659 and set up the huge granite Nandi, one of the largest in India, partway up the hill in 1664. The present gopuram, seven tiers high with golden finials, comes from the 1827 renovation under Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III.

Visitors’ accounts mention that a Śrīcakra is also worshipped at the temple. The hill has other old shrines too, including the Mahābaleśvara temple, which may be as old as the Goddess’s shrine itself.

THE KING AND THE GODDESS

Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III
Reign1799–1868, ruler and then titular king; called Mummaḍi, “the third”
DevotionA devotee of Cāmuṇḍeśvarī; gave the temple jewels, a lion vehicle and other vāhanas
Utsava mūrtiHe presented the festival image; its anniversary (Vardhanti) is kept on Revatī nakṣatra
ŚrītattvanidhiAn illustrated encyclopaedia made at his court, in nine “treasures”
ŚaktinidhiIts first treasure: the forms of the Goddess, drawn and described
DurbarHe began the palace durbar during Navarātri in 1805

ZAPPY: A book of nine treasures?

PROF. BOLT: The Śrītattvanidhi. Its Goddess section begins the whole work, a sign of where the king’s heart was.

Kṛṣṇarāja Wodeyar III is remembered as the cultural founder of modern Mysuru. Poets, musicians and painters worked at his court, and he wrote or sponsored many works in Kannada and Sanskrit.

The Śrītattvanidhi, made at his court, is an illustrated work in nine sections. Its first, the Śaktinidhi, describes and pictures the forms of the Goddess; others cover Viṣṇu, Śiva, Brahmā, the planets and more. It is one of the great iconographic manuscripts of India.

His devotion to Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is visible in the temple to this day, where his statue stands with his queens. He presented the festival image, whose anniversary, the Vardhanti, is still celebrated with the royal family taking part.

THE ŚRĪVIDYĀ MAHARAJA

Jayacāmarājendra Wodeyar
WhoThe 25th and last ruling Maharaja (1940–1950), later Governor of Mysore and Madras
InitiationInitiated into Śrīvidyā by his guru, the sculptor Siddhaliṅgasvāmī
SignatureHis songs carry the mudrā “Śrīvidyā”
Output94 kṛtis in 1945–47, each in a different rāga; 3 more released at his centenary
InfluenceMuttusvāmi Dīkṣitar, himself a Śrīvidyā upāsaka
TodayHis compositions are played at the palace’s private durbar each Navarātri

ZAPPY: A king who wrote in the Śrīvidyā voice?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The ruler of Dasarā signed his music with the name of this tradition.

Jayacāmarājendra Wodeyar trained in both Western and Indian music. He was a Licentiate of the Guildhall School of Music in London and a patron of composers in both traditions.

Initiated into Śrīvidyā by his guru, the sculptor Siddhaliṅgasvāmī, he composed his Carnatic songs in a burst of creativity between 1945 and 1947, nearly all addressed to the Goddess. The ninety-four kṛtis are each in a different rāga, some of them rare.

He also wrote books in English on philosophy and religion, including one on Dattātreya. Deepti Navaratna’s biography, The Maverick Maharaja, describes how his Śrīvidyā practice changed his life and work.

THE PRIVATE DURBAR

Khāsgi Darbār on the golden throne
ThroneGem-studded and golden; in 13 parts, kept in a secure vault, assembled only for Navarātri
AssemblyPalace staff and villagers of Gejjagalli put it together days before
Day 1The lion head is fixed to the throne (Siṃha Jodaṇe) at dawn
KaṅkaṇaA sacred thread is tied on the wrist of the head of the family, and on his wife
AscentHe circles the throne and sits for the durbar each of the nine nights
Since 1950Held as a private family rite since the end of princely rule

ZAPPY: Is it still a king’s court?

PROF. BOLT: A private one. There’s no kingdom, but the family keeps the vow to the Goddess.

The golden throne is the centre of the royal Navarātri. It is taken out only for the festival, assembled from its parts in the Durbar Hall, purified with homas, and completed on the first morning when the lion head is fixed in place.

The head of the family is bathed, a kaṅkaṇa is tied on his wrist at an auspicious time, and he ascends the throne. The palace band plays, including the Maharaja Jayacāmarājendra’s compositions. The durbar is repeated on each night of Navarātri.

The present head, Yaduveer Krishnadatta Chamaraja Wadiyar, has held the durbar since 2015. After descending the throne he is honoured by his family at the Savāri Toṭṭi, one of the palace courtyards.

MAHĀNAVAMĪ AND ĀYUDHA PŪJĀ

The ninth day at the palace
WhatWorship of arms, tools and vehicles
At the palaceThe state elephant, horse, cow and camel are brought in and honoured
WeaponsThe royal arms and the state sword (paṭṭada katti) are worshipped
HomaA Caṇḍī homa ends with the full offering (pūrṇāhuti)
At homePeople worship books, tools, vehicles and computers
MeaningEvery instrument of work is the Goddess’s power in our hands

ZAPPY: People worship their cars?

PROF. BOLT: And their laptops, Zappy. The tool you work with is treated as sacred for a day.

The ninth day is given to Āyudha Pūjā, the worship of weapons and tools. At the palace, the state elephant, horse and cow are brought in and decorated, and the royal arms, palanquins, chariots and even cars are worshipped.

The day also includes worship at the palace’s Koḍi Someśvara temple and the completion of the Caṇḍī homa. The weapons are later taken in procession to the Bhuvaneśvarī temple in the palace grounds.

Across Karnataka the same day sees shops, factories and homes worshipping their tools. The idea fits Śrīvidyā well: every power to act is a share of the Goddess’s power, called Kriyā Śakti.

VIJAYADAŚAMĪ AT THE PALACE

Victory day, morning
Vajramuṣṭi kāḷagaA ritual bout between jaṭṭi wrestlers wearing a knuckle-weapon
Vijaya yātreThe head of the family rides in a silver chariot around the palace
BhuvaneśvarīHe worships at the Bhuvaneśvarī temple inside the palace grounds
Banni pūjāThen the śamī (banni) tree is worshipped
Kaṅkaṇa offThe thread tied on the first day is ceremonially removed
AfternoonThe public procession, the Jamboo Savari, begins

ZAPPY: Why the banni tree?

PROF. BOLT: In the Mahābhārata the Pāṇḍavas hid their weapons in a śamī tree during their year in disguise. On victory day, they took them back.

Vijayadaśamī at the palace begins with the Vajramuṣṭi kāḷaga, a short and bloody traditional bout between jaṭṭi wrestlers, watched by the royal family. It is held only in the palace courtyard, and is occasionally cancelled.

Then comes the Vijaya yātre, a small procession in which the head of the family rides in a silver chariot to the Bhuvaneśvarī temple in the palace grounds. He worships there, and at the banni tree beside it.

This is the private victory ritual, and it is the root of the public procession. The Jamboo Savari of the afternoon is, at heart, the same ride to the banni tree, made by the whole city.

THE JAMBOO SAVARI

The procession of the Goddess
StartThe Chief Minister worships the Nandi flag and offers flowers to the Goddess at the palace
The howdahThe golden ambāri: a wooden core covered in about 85 kg of gold sheet; about 750 kg in all
In itThe image of Cāmuṇḍeśvarī, the Nāḍa Devate
RouteAbout 5 km from the palace to Bannimantap
With itElephants, bands, cavalry, folk troupes and district floats
2026Elephant Captain Prashanth carried the howdah for the first time, in the 417th procession

ZAPPY: Why is it called Jamboo?

PROF. BOLT: Probably from “Jambi” or Banni Savari: the ride to the banni tree. It has nothing to do with jumbo the elephant!

The public procession begins in the palace grounds. The Chief Minister first worships the Nandi flag, then climbs a platform and offers flowers to the Goddess seated in the golden howdah, at an auspicious time announced in advance. A gun salute follows.

The howdah is a wooden frame covered with filigreed gold sheet, made by a master craftsman named Singannācārya. The lead elephant has changed over the years: Arjuna from 2012, then Abhimanyu, and in 2026 Captain Prashanth, who had walked in the procession sixteen times before leading it.

Behind the Goddess come other elephants, mounted police, bands, folk performers and decorated floats from Karnataka’s districts. The procession moves slowly through the city, and takes some hours to reach Bannimantap.

FROM THE KING TO THE GODDESS

How the procession changed
BeforeThe Maharaja himself rode in the golden howdah
1969Jayacāmarājendra Wodeyar rode for the last time
1970–74When the palace stopped, citizens carried an image of Cāmuṇḍeśvarī in a wooden howdah
1975The State Government held the first official procession, under Chief Minister Devarāj Urs
SinceThe Goddess rides in the golden howdah; the elected government leads the honours
MeaningThe king was always her servant; now the people are

ZAPPY: Did people protest when the king stopped?

PROF. BOLT: Kannada activists did, and they started a people’s Dasarā. The Goddess replaced the king, and the festival became everyone’s.

The change came with the end of the princes’ privileges. After 1969, the Maharaja no longer rode in the procession, and for a while it looked as if the custom would end.

Kannada activists, among them the writer Na. Nāgaliṅgasvāmī, responded by placing a sandalwood image of Cāmuṇḍeśvarī in a wooden howdah borrowed from the palace and carrying it through the streets. These “people’s Dasarās” ran from 1970 to 1974.

In 1975 the State Government took the procession over, keeping the image of the Goddess in place of the king. That is the form seen today, with the royal family keeping its own rituals inside the palace.

BANNIMANTAP AND THE TORCHES

Victory night
Banni maṇṭapaThe pavilion of the śamī tree, the procession’s end
Pañjina kavāyatuThe torchlight parade of mounted police and troops
GuestThe Governor takes the salute
In royal timesThe Maharaja rode in on horseback for it
NowDrill, stunts, fireworks and, lately, drone shows
AfterThe palace lights stay on for days afterwards

ZAPPY: A parade after dark?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Victory day ends the way the nine nights began: in lamplight.

The procession ends at Bannimantap, named after the banni tree and its pavilion. In the evening the grounds host the Pañjina kavāyatu, the torchlight parade, with mounted police, drill displays and fireworks.

In the days of the Maharajas, the king rode into the parade ground on horseback in his riding clothes. Today the Governor of Karnataka takes the salute.

Recent years have added drone shows and an air display. The palace and city lights usually stay on for some days after Vijayadaśamī, so visitors who avoid the crowds can still see them.

GOMBE HABBA

The festival of dolls
WhatDolls arranged on steps at home for the nine nights
Pattada bombeA royal pair, king and queen, traditionally in sandalwood
PalaceThe Gombe Toṭṭi of Amba Vilas once displayed dolls to the public
Wedding giftFamilies gave pattada bombe to daughters at marriage
TreatsChildren visit homes to see the display and get a small snack
EndOn the tenth day a doll is laid down and the display packed away

ZAPPY: Why a king and queen?

PROF. BOLT: In old Mysuru homes, the royal pair honoured the reigning family, whose Dasarā the whole kingdom shared.

Gombe Habba, the doll festival, is the home side of Dasarā in old Mysuru, as Golu is in Tamil Nadu. Dolls are arranged on an odd number of steps, with gods, saints, scenes from the epics and everyday life.

The centre of the display is the pattada bombe, a royal couple carved in sandalwood. They honoured the Maharaja and Maharani, and were often given to a daughter when she married, so many families keep pairs handed down for generations.

Historians note that early Dasarā records at Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa do not mention dolls, so the custom may be later. The palace once had its own gallery for dolls, the Gombe Toṭṭi.

ZAPPY: Why 21 October in Mysuru when the rest of India had the 20th?

PROF. BOLT: The tenth lunar day began on the afternoon of the 20th and ran into the 21st. Mysuru’s programme placed the procession on the 21st. Always check the local calendar.

Mysuru Dasarā is now Karnataka’s state festival. It opens on the first day of Navarātri with worship on Chamundi Hill by an invited guest, chosen each year, at a time fixed by astrologers. In 2026 the festival ran from 11 to 21 October.

For eleven days the city fills with music in the palace grounds, the Yuva Dasarā concerts for young people, wrestling, food festivals and the Dasarā exhibition, which runs much longer.

Each evening the palace is lit with tens of thousands of bulbs, one of the best-loved sights in South India. The elephants arrive weeks before from the forest camps and practise carrying weights along the route.

ZAPPY: Why do the dates move every year?

PROF. BOLT: Navarātri follows the moon, so it moves against the solar calendar, between mid-September and late October.

Navarātri begins on the first day of the bright half of Āśvina and runs to the ninth, Mahānavamī, with Vijayadaśamī on the tenth. Because tithis begin and end at all hours, almanacs sometimes differ about which civil day to use.

In 2026 the all-India calendar placed Vijayadaśamī on 20 October, while Mysuru’s official programme placed the procession on 21 October. For the procession, follow the official Mysuru Dasarā schedule; for home worship, follow your own pañcāṅga.

For 2027, Drik Panchang gives the start of Navarātri as 30 September and Vijayadaśamī as 9 October. The State announces the Mysuru programme, including the opening and the procession times, a few weeks before.

ZAPPY: So what should I take from it?

PROF. BOLT: That power is held in trust. The king bowed to the Goddess; so can we, with whatever power we have.

Lalitā is called Rājarājeśvarī, queen of kings. In Mysuru the idea is acted out every year: the king takes a vow, sits on the throne in her name for nine nights, and then sends her out in his place on the golden howdah.

The demons of the two stories mean the same thing. Mahiṣa, the buffalo, stands for dullness and stubborn pride; Bhaṇḍa, born from the ash of Kāma, for the desires that remain when wisdom has burned them once. Both must be defeated within.

Vijayadaśamī is also a day to begin. Children start their letters; musicians and dancers begin new learning. The victory is not an end but a fresh start, which may be the most useful lesson of the whole festival.
