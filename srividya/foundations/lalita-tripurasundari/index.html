ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

LALITĀ TRIPURASUNDARĪ

The beautiful queen of the three cities

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · FOUNDATIONS 2

FOREWORD

Picture a queen, red as the dawn, smiling, seated on a throne whose legs are gods. In her four hands she holds a noose, a goad, a bow of sugarcane and five arrows made of flowers. This is Lalitā Tripurasundarī, the Goddess at the heart of Śrīvidyā.

This guide looks at her closely: her names and what they mean, how she is pictured, the four weapons and the couch of five Brahmas, her forms as the girl Bālā and the empress Rājarājeśvarī, her consort Kāmeśvara, and her place among the goddesses of India.

Śrī Lalitāmbikāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A goddess who shoots flowers?

PROF. BOLT: Flowers that never miss. Wait till you see where she aims them.

AT A GLANCE

Lalitā on one page
NamesLalitā, Tripurasundarī, Mahātripurasundarī, Rājarājeśvarī, Kāmeśvarī, Ṣoḍaśī, Śrīmātā
Meaning“She who plays”; “the beautiful one of the three cities”
ColourRed: dawn, kumkum, the rising sun
ArmsFour: noose, goad, sugarcane bow, five flower arrows
SeatThe couch of five Brahmas, in the Cintāmaṇi palace of Śrīpura
ConsortKāmeśvara, Śiva as the lord of desire
AgesBālā (girl), Lalitā (young queen), Rājarājeśvarī (empress)
YantraThe Śrīcakra; she sits in its central point, the bindu

ZAPPY: Why so many names for one goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Because people met her in many ways. A thousand names, in fact, and each is a doorway.

Lalitā is the centre of Śrīvidyā: the Śrīcakra is her palace, the Sahasranāma her portrait, the Lalitopākhyāna her story. This guide is about her alone: how she looks, what she holds, where she sits, the ages in which she appears and how she relates to the other great goddesses.

If the first guide answered “what is the tradition?”, this one answers “whom does it love?”. Many devotees never go further than this: they know her face, her names and her temples, and that is enough for a lifetime of devotion.

A practical note: images of Lalitā vary a great deal between regions and periods. A Tanjore painting, a Mysore-style print, a bronze in a Tamil temple and a modern calendar picture will differ in colour, posture and detail. The descriptions below follow the texts; the pictures you meet will follow local habit.

HER NAMES

What each name tells you
Lalitā“She who plays”: the world is her līlā, her sport
TripurasundarīBeauty of the three cities: three worlds, three bodies, three states of mind
RājarājeśvarīQueen over kings of kings: sovereign above all gods
KāmeśvarīLady of desire: desire itself, made sacred
Ṣoḍaśī“The sixteen-year-old”, ever young; also linked to the sixteenth digit of the moon
Śrīmātā“The auspicious Mother”: the very first of her thousand names

ZAPPY: Sixteen-year-old? Isn't she the mother of the universe?

PROF. BOLT: Both. She is called the eternal sixteen: never aging, always at the fresh peak of life. And the first name in her thousand is simply ‘Mother’.

śrīmātā śrīmahārājñī śrīmatsiṃhāsaneśvarī
cidagnikuṇḍasambhūtā devakāryasamudyatā

The auspicious Mother, the great Queen, ruler of the glorious lion-throne; born from the fire-pit of consciousness, risen to do the work of the gods.
(Lalitā Sahasranāma, the first five names)

The Lalitā Sahasranāma is less a list than a story told in names. It begins with her birth from the fire of consciousness and her readiness to help the gods, moves through her appearance from crown to feet, tells the war against Bhaṇḍa, maps the Śrīcakra and the subtle body, and ends in pure philosophy. The first five names already contain the arc of the whole Lalitopākhyāna.

Bhāskararāya’s commentary often gives several meanings for one name, drawn from grammar, ritual, philosophy and story. For “Śrīmātā”, for example, he notes that she is mother not only of the world but of Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī and the other goddesses, and that the word “mātā” also means “measurer”, the one who sets the measure of all things.

The name Ṣoḍaśī links her to the moon. The moon has fifteen phases, each ruled by one of the Tithi Nityās; the sixteenth, which never waxes or wanes, is the Goddess herself. The Nityās have their own guides in this series.

HER FORM

How she is pictured
BodyRed as vermilion, radiant like a thousand rising suns
FaceSmiling, with three eyes; the crescent moon in her crown
CrownSet with rubies; her hair braided with flowers
PostureSeated; usually the left leg folded, the right foot resting down
Two-armed formOne meditation verse gives her two hands, holding a jewelled cup and a red lotus
Four-armed formThe usual form: noose, goad, bow and arrows

ZAPPY: Two arms or four? Which is right?

PROF. BOLT: Both are traditional. Different meditation verses show her differently. The four-armed form is the one you will see most.

aruṇāṃ karuṇātaraṅgitākṣīṃ
dhṛtapāśāṅkuśapuṣpabāṇacāpām
aṇimādibhir āvṛtāṃ mayūkhair
aham ity eva vibhāvaye bhavānīm

Red as dawn, her eyes rippling with compassion, holding noose, goad, flower arrows and bow, ringed by rays that are the powers of yoga: I meditate on Bhavānī as my own “I”.
(A meditation verse recited before the Sahasranāma)

Meditation verses (dhyāna-ślokas) are how the tradition fixes an image in the mind before worship. They are short, dense and visual, almost like an artist’s brief. The Sahasranāma is usually preceded by several, each giving a slightly different picture, and devotees often recite all of them.

Her red colour is described again and again: like vermilion, the hibiscus, the rising sun, the ruby. Commentators read the red as compassion (anurāga, a word that also means love and redness) and as the energy that brings the world into being. Her smile is equally insisted on: she is never pictured angry.

The three eyes join her to Śiva; the crescent moon in her crown joins her to the moon’s cycle and to coolness and nectar. The jewelled crown and ornaments are not luxury but kingship: she is described as a ruling queen, and her ornaments are listed with the care of a royal inventory.

THE FOUR WEAPONS

What she holds, and why
Noose · pāśaAttachment (rāga): she holds it, so it does not hold her devotees
Goad · aṅkuśaAnger (krodha): turned into a tool that steers rather than wounds
Sugarcane bowThe mind: sweet, bending, ready to send out the arrows
Five flower arrowsThe five subtle elements of sense: sound, touch, form, taste, smell
The flowersLotus, aśoka, mango blossom, jasmine and blue lotus: the five arrows of Kāma, the god of love
HandsNoose and goad in the upper hands; bow and arrows in the lower

ZAPPY: How do you know the noose is attachment? Did someone just decide?

PROF. BOLT: Her own thousand names say so. Names eight to eleven explain all four weapons, one by one.

The four weapons appear in the Sahasranāma as names eight to eleven: she is adorned with a noose that is attachment itself, shining with a goad shaped as anger, holding a sugarcane bow that is the mind, with the five subtle elements as her arrows. The text spells out its own symbolism, which is rare and precious.

The bow and arrows are borrowed from Kāma, the god of love, who shoots flower arrows from a sugarcane bow strung with bees. In Lalitā’s hands they take on a different job. Kāma’s arrows stir desire; hers draw the mind toward the Divine. This links to her story, in which she restores Kāma to life after Śiva burns him.

Some commentators add a practical reading: the noose and goad are the two tools of an elephant-keeper. The mind is the elephant; the noose holds it in, the goad steers it. In Lalitā’s hands, both are used with love.

THE COUCH OF FIVE BRAHMAS

Pañcabrahmāsana
Four legsBrahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra and Īśvara
The plankSadāśiva, lying flat
Their tasksCreating, sustaining, dissolving, veiling: the work of the world
Names for itPañcabrahmāsanasthitā, “seated on the five Brahmas”; also pañcapretāsanāsīnā, “seated on five lifeless ones”
Lifeless?Without Śakti, the gods cannot move: they are her furniture until she gives them power
PicturesThe scene is common in Tanjore and Mysore paintings and in temple murals

ZAPPY: Isn't it rude to sit on gods?

PROF. BOLT: The picture is a teaching, not an insult. All their power is borrowed from her. Pull the Śakti out and even the great gods lie still.

The five figures correspond to the five great acts that Śaiva and Śākta theology assigns to the Divine: creating, maintaining, dissolving, concealing and revealing. Four of them, held by Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra and Īśvara, form the legs; Sadāśiva, the plank, stands for the fifth, the grace that reveals. Above all five sits the power that makes them act.

The name pañcapretāsanāsīnā, “seated on five corpses”, sounds harsh, but the commentators’ point is plain: without Śakti even Śiva is śava, a corpse. The difference between the two words is one short vowel, the letter i, and the tradition reads that letter as the Goddess.

In paintings the couch is often richly decorated, with Kāmeśvara seated on the plank and Lalitā on his lap, Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī fanning them, and the gods standing in attendance. The image compresses the whole theology into one royal scene.

BĀLĀ

The Goddess as a girl
NameBālā Tripurasundarī, “the young girl”
AgeTraditionally pictured as about nine years old
HandsBook and rosary, and the gestures of fearlessness and blessing
In the storyIn the Lalitopākhyāna, Bālā rides into battle and kills the thirty sons of the demon Bhaṇḍa
In practiceOften the first form of the Goddess a Śrīvidyā student is taught
TempleThe Śrī Bālā Pīṭham at Nemili, Tamil Nadu, among others

ZAPPY: A nine-year-old killing thirty demons?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point of the story. Innocence is not weakness. In her girl-form she is already the whole Goddess.

Bālā is sometimes described as Lalitā’s daughter and sometimes as Lalitā herself in childhood; texts and lineages differ. In the battle story she is a separate warrior, whom Lalitā is reluctant to send out because she is so young; Bālā insists, rides out, and destroys all thirty of Bhaṇḍa’s sons in one day.

In practice Bālā is often the first form a student of the tradition is introduced to, and her devotees treat her with the affection given to a child as well as the reverence due to the Goddess. Some families worship a young girl as Bālā at Navarātri in the kanyā pūjā.

Her book and rosary link her to learning and speech, and many students of Sanskrit, music and the arts turn to her before beginning study. Her gestures of fearlessness and blessing are the same as those of many deities: do not fear; ask, and receive.

RĀJARĀJEŚVARĪ

Empress of emperors
NameRājarājeśvarī: queen over the kings of kings, which means the gods themselves
FormThe full sovereign: crowned, enthroned, holding the four weapons, Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī fanning her
CourtHer prime minister is Śyāmalā (Mantriṇī); her commander is Vārāhī (Daṇḍinī)
Royal worshipKings in the South took her as protector; many royal houses had Śrīcakra shrines
TodayCountless shrines and towns carry the name; Navarātri is her great season

ZAPPY: Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī as her attendants?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, one of her names says Ramā and Vāṇī fan her from left and right. Wealth and learning wait on her.

As Rājarājeśvarī she is shown in full court. The Lalitopākhyāna describes her ministers, generals and attendants in detail, as a king’s court would be described in a royal chronicle. The deity guides in this series take up the most important of them, Śyāmalā the minister and Vārāhī the commander.

In South Indian history the image of the Goddess as empress fitted royal ideology well. Kings presented themselves as her servants and their kingdoms as her domain. The Mysore Wodeyars’ devotion to Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is one familiar example of a royal house binding itself to the Goddess, though Cāmuṇḍī is not Lalitā.

The name is so popular that it appears on schools, colleges and whole neighbourhoods. In many temples’ Navarātri decorations (alaṅkāras), the Goddess is dressed as Rājarājeśvarī, often on the last day.

Her court is also a map. The Lalitopākhyāna places her ministers and generals in the walled city of Śrīpura, ring within ring, much as the deities of the Śrīcakra stand in their nine enclosures. To know the court is to begin to know the yantra, which is why the deity guides and the Śrīcakra guides in this series cross-refer so often.

KĀMEŚVARA AND KĀMEŚVARĪ

The divine couple
Kāmeśvara“Lord of desire”: Śiva in the form that marries Lalitā
Not the burnerŚiva burned Kāma; as Kāmeśvara he becomes the master of desire, not its enemy
SeatShe sits on his left thigh on the couch of five Brahmas
OneŚiva and Śakti together, as light and its own reflection
The weddingTold in Part 2 of the Lalitopākhyāna; the gods give her gifts at the marriage

ZAPPY: So Śiva burned desire, then married the Lady of Desire?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Desire destroyed, then reborn as something holy. The tradition loves that twist.

Kāmeśvara is pictured almost as her mirror: the same colour, the same four weapons, the same crown with the crescent. This is deliberate. The Śrīvidyā view is that Śiva and Śakti are one reality seen two ways, and the texts often describe the couple as if one were the reflection of the other.

The Lalitopākhyāna says the gods, seeing the Goddess rise from the fire, worried that she had no husband. Brahmā arranged the marriage, and Kāmeśvara appeared, as handsome as she was beautiful. The wedding is described as a vast celebration, with each god bringing a gift.

The Foundations guide on Śiva and Śakti goes deeper into the philosophy of this pairing, and how it compares to Advaita Vedānta.

Some temples keep the pair together. In paintings of the five-Brahma couch, Kāmeśvara sits on the plank and the Goddess on his left thigh, so that the couple is one figure with two faces. In other images Lalitā sits alone and Kāmeśvara is understood to be present in the bindu of the Śrīcakra before her. Either way, the tradition never imagines her as a goddess without her lord, nor him as a god without his power.

WHERE SHE IS WORSHIPPED

Her great shrines
Kāñcī KāmākṣīSeated in yoga, holding the four weapons; the Śrīcakra before her
Madurai MīnākṣīGreen, holding a parrot, worshipped as a form of Śrīvidyā’s Goddess
Tiruvārūr KamalāmbāDīkṣitar’s Navāvaraṇa kṛtis are addressed to her

NaimiṣāraṇyaThe Lalitā Devī temple, counted by some as a Śakti Pīṭha
HomesMost often as a picture or a small Śrīcakra in the pūjā room

ZAPPY: Are all these really the same goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Each temple has its own story. The Śrīvidyā tradition reads all of them as faces of Lalitā. Temple tradition and Śrīvidyā overlap but are not identical.

See also: Kāmākṣī, Kāñcī · Ṣoḍaśī among the Mahāvidyās

Kāmākṣī of Kāñcī is perhaps the most important Śrīvidyā temple. The Goddess sits in a yoga posture holding the four weapons, and a Śrīcakra, said by tradition to have been installed by Śaṅkara, is worshipped before her. The temple has its own guide in the Śakti Pīṭha series and a Śrīvidyā guide later in this one.

Madurai Mīnākṣī is a different case. Her temple legend is Tamil: a princess born with three breasts, who conquered the world and married Śiva as Sundareśvara. Śrīvidyā writers read her as Lalitā, and her worship has Śrīvidyā elements, but the temple’s own story stands on its own.

Each of the seven temples has its own guide later in the series. Here it is enough to see how far the Goddess’s worship reaches: Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra, Kerala and the Gangetic plain.

LALITĀ AND OTHER GODDESSES

How she relates
DurgāDevī Māhātmya’s warrior; Lalitā also wars (against Bhaṇḍa), but is mainly pictured as queen at peace
ṢoḍaśīThe third Mahāvidyā is Tripurasundarī under another name
LakṣmīIn Śrīvidyā Lakṣmī serves Lalitā; Lakṣmī worshippers often read it the other way round
PārvatīLalitā is widely seen as Pārvatī in her sovereign form; her consort is a form of Śiva
Our stanceEach tradition puts its own Goddess at the top; we show how each reads the others

ZAPPY: So who's the highest?

PROF. BOLT: Every tradition gives the same answer: theirs. Śrīvidyā says Lalitā. Many devotees simply say there is one Goddess with many faces.

On this site: Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts · Aṣṭa Lakṣmī · 8 forms + overview

In the Devī Māhātmya, the great Goddess takes form to kill Mahiṣa, Śumbha and Niśumbha. In the Lalitopākhyāna, Lalitā takes form to kill Bhaṇḍa. The two stories run in parallel, and both are told in Purāṇas. Many devotees hold them as two tellings of one truth; others see different goddesses for different needs.

The ten Mahāvidyās include Tripurasundarī as the third and Kamalā (Lakṣmī) as the tenth, so within that system they are separate forms of one Devī. In Śrīvidyā proper, all goddesses are her attendants or her forms. In Śrīvaiṣṇava theology, Lakṣmī is the supreme Goddess. These views do not need to be reconciled to be understood.

What all these traditions share is a high view of the Goddess as the power behind the gods. The differences are about which face is placed in the centre.

WHAT SHE TEACHES

Beauty as a way in
BeautyNot a distraction from the Divine but one of its faces
DesireNot crushed but steered: noose and goad in her hands, not ours
PlayThe world is her play; life can be lived lightly and well
SovereigntyShe rules the gods; nothing in the world lies outside her
WithinHer palace is also the body, her throne the heart

ZAPPY: If she's so beautiful, why doesn't she just say ‘give up desire’ like the ascetics?

PROF. BOLT: Because desire is her own power. You don't throw out the fire; you learn to cook with it.

For many devotees Lalitā is simply the Mother: the one they chant to on Friday evenings and visit at Navarātri. For the philosophers she is consciousness itself. The two views do not compete. The tradition holds that the one who loves her face and the one who knows her nature are walking the same road from different ends.

Her teaching on desire is the most distinctive. Many Indian paths treat desire as the enemy. Śrīvidyā gives the weapons of desire to the Goddess, so that the devotee does not have to fight them alone. Surrender, not suppression, is the method.

And her beauty matters. A tradition that pictures the highest reality as a smiling queen is saying something about the world: it is fundamentally good and lovely, and joy is closer to truth than grimness.

The pronunciation of her names, roughly: Lalitā “la-li-taa”; Tripurasundarī “tri-pu-ra-sun-da-ree”; Rājarājeśvarī “raa-ja-raa-jesh-va-ree”; Kāmeśvarī “kaa-mesh-va-ree”; Ṣoḍaśī “sho-da-shee”. The long vowels (ā, ī) are held for twice the length.

Two names you will meet in later guides: Śrīpura, her city on the summit of Meru, and Cintāmaṇi-gṛha, the wish-jewel palace at its centre, where the five-Brahma couch stands. Both are described in Part 3 of the Lalitopākhyāna. Kāmakalā, a term from the philosophical texts, names the subtle union of Śiva and Śakti that the couple on the couch shows in picture form.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
