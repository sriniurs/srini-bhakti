ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

ŚIVA AND ŚAKTI

Light and its own awareness

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · FOUNDATIONS 5

FOREWORD

Every image in Śrīvidyā comes back to a pair: Kāmeśvara and Kāmeśvarī on one seat, four triangles and five in the Śrīcakra, the coiled power rising to meet the crown. Behind them all is one idea: Śiva and Śakti are not two.

This guide explains that idea in pictures and in arguments: a famous verse, the mirror, the alphabet, the three powers, the half-woman Lord, and how Śaiva, Śākta, Advaita and Vaiṣṇava schools each read the divine pair.

AT A GLANCE

The pair on one page
ŚivaPure awareness: still, unchanging, the light
ŚaktiThe power of that awareness: to know itself, to will, to act, to become the world
Their relationTwo names for one reality, like fire and its heat
In ŚrīvidyāKāmeśvara and Kāmeśvarī, the lord and lady of desire, seated as one
In the ŚrīcakraFour Śiva triangles and five Śakti triangles, meeting in the bindu
In the bodyŚakti rises from the base of the spine to meet Śiva at the crown
In VedāntaRead differently by Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita: shown side by side here

ZAPPY: Two gods or one?

PROF. BOLT: One reality, two faces. The tradition keeps saying it in new ways because it's easy to forget.

The first guide in this series said that Śiva and Śakti are, in Śrīvidyā, light and its self-awareness. This guide opens that idea up. It shows how the pair appear in a famous verse, in the alphabet, in the powers of will, knowing and action, in sculpture, in the Śrīcakra and in the body, and then sets Śrīvidyā’s view beside those of the great schools of Vedānta.

This is the most philosophical of the Foundations guides. It is meant to be readable without background; where an idea is technical, we give a picture or a story first.

A word on gender. The tradition uses male and female images because they make the point vividly, but its texts are clear that the reality behind them is beyond gender. The images are a way of saying “two in one”, not a statement about men and women.

THE FIRST VERSE

Śiva needs Śakti
SourceThe opening verse of the Saundaryalaharī
ClaimOnly joined with Śakti can Śiva create; without her he cannot even stir
ThereforeEven Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva worship her
TwistThe verse ends humbly: how can one without merit even bow to her?
Read asThe charter of the Śākta view, placed at the very start of the best-loved Śrīvidyā hymn

ZAPPY: Isn't that a bit rude to Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: He'd agree with it. In the tradition Śiva himself teaches that his power is the Goddess. It's a love-statement, not a put-down.

śivaḥ śaktyā yukto yadi bhavati śaktaḥ prabhavituṃ
na ced evaṃ devo na khalu kuśalaḥ spanditum api
atas tvām ārādhyāṃ hariharaviriñcādibhir api
praṇantuṃ stotuṃ vā katham akṛtapuṇyaḥ prabhavati

Only when Śiva is joined with Śakti can he create; otherwise the god cannot so much as stir. So how can one who has earned no merit bow to you or praise you, whom even Viṣṇu, Śiva and Brahmā worship? (Saundaryalaharī 1)

The Saundaryalaharī begins with a theological claim, not a prayer for blessings. Placing it first tells the reader what kind of hymn this is: a hymn whose praise of beauty rests on a view of reality in which Śakti is the source of all power.

Commentators enjoy the wordplay. Śaktaḥ, “able”, is formed from the same root as Śakti; without Śakti, Śiva is not śakta, not able. The structure of the language itself, they say, shows that power and the powerful belong together.

The humble last line matters too. After making the grandest claim, the poet turns to himself: if the gods worship her, what can a person like me do? It sets the tone for the whole hymn, which combines high thought with deep devotion.

LIGHT AND ITS MIRROR

Prakāśa and vimarśa
PrakāśaLight that shows everything but, by itself, does not know itself
VimarśaThe mirror-power: awareness turning back to know “I am”
Without vimarśaLight with no one to see it: pure, but inert
Without prakāśaA mirror in the dark: nothing to reflect
TogetherSelf-aware consciousness, which is the Goddess and Śiva as one
TextThe Kāmakalāvilāsa opens with this image

ZAPPY: So Śakti is Śiva's self-awareness?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Śiva is the light; Śakti is the light realising ‘I am light’. And that realising is what makes a world.

The image of light and mirror is the heart of Śrīvidyā’s philosophy. A mirror does not add anything to light; it lets light see itself. In the same way, Śakti adds nothing foreign to Śiva; she is his own capacity to know himself, and so to become many.

The Kāmakalāvilāsa develops this in a few dense verses: the pure light sees its own reflection in the mirror of its power, and from that self-seeing arise sound, letters, words, and finally the whole world. Creation is consciousness looking at itself.

This has a practical edge. If the world is consciousness seeing itself, then every act of real seeing, really attending to a flower, a face or a sound, is a small repetition of creation, and a door back to its source.

“I”: A TO HA

The alphabet as the pair
AhamThe Sanskrit word for “I”
AThe first letter: Śiva, the beginning
HaThe last letter: Śakti, the end of expression
MThe point (bindu) that joins them
So“I” contains the whole alphabet, the whole world of speech, from Śiva to Śakti
OriginAn idea shared with Kashmiri Śaiva philosophy, especially Abhinavagupta

ZAPPY: The word ‘I’ has all of creation in it?

PROF. BOLT: That's the claim. Every word lies between the first and last letters, and ‘aham’ spans them. Speaking ‘I’ honestly is speaking the whole world.

This idea comes from the grammarians and the Kashmiri Śaiva philosophers, and Śrīvidyā shares it. Sanskrit’s alphabet runs in a fixed order, from a to ha. The word aham, “I”, begins with the first and ends with the last, sealed with the nasal point m, written as a dot. So “I” contains, in principle, every sound between.

The teaching is that true “I”-consciousness, the I of the Divine, is not a small ego but the whole field of expression. When a person says “I” in the narrow sense, they claim a corner; the Goddess says “I” and means everything.

This is philosophy, not mantra. It explains how the tradition sees speech and selfhood; it does not touch any guarded formula, and we keep it at that level.

The same thought runs the other way too. Because every sound lies between a and ha, every word is a small piece of the divine “I”. Indian tradition has long treated the letters themselves as goddesses, the mātṛkās, and in the Lalitā Sahasranāma the Goddess is called the one whose form is the letters of the alphabet. Speech, in this view, is never merely human.

THE THREE POWERS

Will, knowing, doing
IcchāWill: the first stirring, “let me be many”
JñānaKnowing: the plan of what will be
KriyāAction: the doing that makes it real
As goddessesOften linked to the goddesses Vāmā, Jyeṣṭhā and Raudrī; pairings differ between texts
TripurāOne reading of her name: the one who holds these three as her “cities”
KāmaHer desire, kāma, is icchā: will itself, not lust

ZAPPY: Her desire is will?

PROF. BOLT: Desire in its first, pure sense: the wish to be, to know, to give. That's why the Goddess of creation is called the Lady of Desire.

The three powers appear in many Indian systems. In Śrīvidyā they map onto the many triads of Tripurā: the three cities, the three gods, the three states of mind, the three corners of the central triangle of the Śrīcakra. One triad becomes a key to many.

The order matters. First there is will, a pure wish without content; then knowing, which gives the wish a shape; then action, which makes it real. Every human act, from cooking a meal to writing a book, follows the same order, which is why the tradition sees human life as a small copy of divine creation.

Calling the Goddess the Lady of Desire, Kāmeśvarī, therefore means calling her the Lady of Will. The Kāma she rules is the first movement of the Divine toward expression, of which human desire is a faint and often confused echo.

HALF WOMAN, HALF LORD

Ardhanārīśvara
ImageOne body: left half the Goddess, right half Śiva
MeaningNeither exists apart; together they are whole
WhereAncient sculpture across India, notably at Elephanta and in Chola bronzes
In poetryKālidāsa opens the Raghuvaṃśa calling them joined like word and meaning
In ŚrīvidyāThe same truth shown as a couple on one seat, rather than one body

ZAPPY: Why half-and-half, not just side by side?

PROF. BOLT: Side by side, you might think there are two. Half-and-half, you can't. It's the most direct picture of ‘not two’.

vāgarthāv iva saṃpṛktau vāgarthapratipattaye
jagataḥ pitarau vande pārvatīparameśvarau

To understand word and meaning, I bow to the parents of the world, Pārvatī and Parameśvara, joined as word and meaning are. (Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa 1.1)

Ardhanārīśvara is one of the oldest images of the divine pair in Indian art, found from the Kuṣāṇa period onward. The sculptors take great care with the details: the female half with breast, anklet and earring; the male half with matted hair, serpent and trident. Nothing is shared except the one body.

Kālidāsa’s verse, the opening of his great poem on the solar dynasty, is perhaps the most quoted line on the subject. Word and meaning can be talked about separately, but in a real sentence they are one. So are the parents of the world.

Śrīvidyā prefers the image of the couple on the five-Brahma couch, but it fully shares this picture’s meaning. Some temples have both images, and devotees do not feel them as different.

WHO IS HIGHER?

How different schools answer
Śaiva SiddhāntaŚiva is supreme; Śakti is his power, his instrument, inseparable but subordinate
Kashmiri ŚaivismŚiva and Śakti are strictly one; Śiva is named first, but neither is higher
Śākta schoolsŚakti is supreme; Śiva is her support, the ground on which she sits
ŚrīvidyāŚākta in emphasis: the Goddess is worshipped; Kāmeśvara is her other half
Our stanceWe set out each view; we do not judge between them

ZAPPY: Which one is true?

PROF. BOLT: Each school has great thinkers and good arguments. They agree that the two are inseparable. They disagree about which name to say first.

In Śaiva Siddhānta, the dominant Śaiva theology of Tamil Nadu, Śiva is the supreme Lord and Śakti is his grace and power, through which he acts. She is honoured, but the system is centred on him. Many Tamil temples worship both, and many devotees hold both views lightly.

Kashmiri Śaivism insists on strict non-duality: Śiva and Śakti are not two, and to say “Śiva” already includes his power. The Śākta schools reverse the emphasis: Śakti is the dynamic Absolute, and Śiva is her ground or her consort. Śrīvidyā leans Śākta while using much Kashmiri language.

These differences can look large on paper and small in practice. A devotee who chants the Sahasranāma in a Śiva temple at Navarātri seldom asks which school is right.

Temple architecture shows the same range. In many Tamil Śiva temples the Goddess has her own shrine beside the Lord’s, and both receive full worship. In some great Goddess temples, Kāñcī and Madurai among them, she is the heart of the temple’s life and her consort, though honoured, stands slightly to the side. The buildings, like the schools, give different answers with the same respect.

ŚRĪVIDYĀ AND ADVAITA

Brahman with a face
Advaita (Śaṅkara)Brahman alone is real; the world appears through māyā, which is neither real nor unreal
Śākta readingMāyā is not a veil but Śakti, Brahman’s own real power
AgreementThe Self is one with the Absolute; liberation is knowing it
DifferenceAdvaita calls the world an appearance; Śrīvidyā calls it the Goddess’s real play
In practiceMany Advaita teachers practised Śrīvidyā, seeing no conflict
Our stanceBoth readings stated plainly; neither preferred

ZAPPY: If the world is illusion, why worship anyone?

PROF. BOLT: Advaitins answer: worship purifies the mind for knowledge. Śrīvidyā answers: the world isn't illusion, it's her. Same goal, different road.

Advaita Vedānta, as systematised by Śaṅkara, holds that Brahman alone is real, the individual self is that Brahman, and the world is an appearance, māyā, produced by ignorance. Liberation is the direct knowledge of one’s identity with Brahman.

Śrīvidyā shares the conclusion that the self is one with the Absolute, but reads māyā differently. For Śākta thinkers, māyā is the Goddess’s power: not an error but a real expression of the Absolute. The world is then the play of Brahman, not an illusion overlaid on it.

Many teachers held both: Advaita for the final understanding, Śrīvidyā as the devotional and meditative path that purifies the mind for it. The Śaṅkara maṭhas are the great example of this blend. The next guide looks at how that came about.

RĀMĀNUJA AND MADHVA

The Vaiṣṇava parallel
Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja)Viṣṇu and Lakṣmī are inseparable; Lakṣmī is the mediator, puruṣakāra
Śrīvaiṣṇava schoolsDebate whether Lakṣmī is an independent principle or one with the Lord
Dvaita (Madhva)Lakṣmī is eternally free and supreme among souls, yet wholly dependent on Viṣṇu
ParallelThe couple at the top of the universe, power and holder of power, appears here too
DifferenceHere the male partner is supreme; in Śrīvidyā the Goddess is
Our stanceShown for comparison; each tradition reads the pair its own way

ZAPPY: So Vaiṣṇavas have their own Śiva-Śakti?

PROF. BOLT: In a sense: Viṣṇu and Śrī. The shape is similar, the emphasis reversed. Lakṣmī's ‘Śrī’ and Lalitā's ‘Śrī’ are the same word.

The Vaiṣṇava schools are worth setting beside Śrīvidyā because they offer such a close parallel. In Śrīvaiṣṇava theology, Viṣṇu is never without Śrī; she lives on his chest, and every act of grace passes through her mediation.

Within Śrīvaiṣṇavism there has long been debate whether Lakṣmī shares the Lord’s supremacy or is the first of his dependents. The two great sub-schools, often called Vaḍakalai and Teṅkalai, give different answers. Madhva’s Dvaita makes Lakṣmī eternally free from bondage but dependent on Viṣṇu in all things.

These views put the male partner first, where Śrīvidyā puts the Goddess first. But all of them see the highest reality as a pair bound in love and power, which is why the parallel is illuminating rather than contradictory.

IN THE ŚRĪCAKRA

Nine triangles, one point
Śiva trianglesFour, pointing upward
Śakti trianglesFive, pointing downward
InterlockingThey cross to form 43 small triangles: the world as their meeting
BinduThe central point, where the two are wholly one
MovementFrom bindu outward: creation; from outside inward: return
MoreThe Śrīcakra section explains each enclosure

ZAPPY: Why five for her and four for him?

PROF. BOLT: Commentators say: her side is the side of unfolding, of more and more forms. His side is the stillness that holds them. Five and four make nine.

The Śrīcakra is, among other things, a diagram of Śiva and Śakti. Its four upward triangles stand for Śiva, its five downward ones for Śakti. Neither set alone makes the yantra; only their interlocking produces the many small triangles in which the deities of the inner enclosures live.

The bindu, the point at the centre, is where the distinction disappears. Kāmeśvara and Kāmeśvarī are said to reside there as one, and worship moves toward it from the outer square, enclosure by enclosure.

This guide only touches the Śrīcakra. Its own section, eleven guides long, will explain each enclosure, its goddesses and its meaning, and the Bhāvanopaniṣad’s reading of the whole diagram as the body.

Some traditions describe the two sets of triangles as fire and water, or as the sun and the moon, rather than as male and female. The images change, the logic stays: two kinds of movement, one rising and one descending, lock together to make a world. The Śrīcakra is that idea drawn with a ruler and compass.

IN THE BODY

Kuṇḍalinī and the crown
KuṇḍalinīŚakti, coiled at the base of the spine (mūlādhāra)
SahasrāraThe thousand-petalled lotus at the crown, where Śiva dwells
UnionHer rising to meet him is liberation in this picture
SahasranāmaSeveral names describe her rising through the cakras to the crown
SaundaryalaharīVerses 9–10 picture the same journey
CautionWe describe the image; any practice belongs with a teacher

ZAPPY: Is this like yoga class kuṇḍalinī?

PROF. BOLT: Same word, older and more careful idea. The texts describe it as grace, not a technique to force. We describe it; we don't teach it.

The kuṇḍalinī image is shared by many Indian traditions. In Śrīvidyā it gives the Śiva-Śakti relation an inner geography: the Goddess lies coiled at the base of the spine, Śiva dwells in the thousand-petalled lotus at the crown, and the spiritual journey is her rising to meet him.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma includes a sequence of names describing her presence in each of the subtle centres, and the Saundaryalaharī, in two famous verses, pictures her piercing the centres and rejoicing with her lord in the crown before returning. These are read as devotional descriptions, not instructions.

Popular modern “kuṇḍalinī” teaching often makes it a technique. The older texts are more cautious: they treat it as the Goddess’s grace, best approached through devotion and a teacher’s care. We follow them.

WHAT IT MEANS

For an ordinary devotee
The worldReal and sacred, not a trap to escape
The bodyA temple of Śiva and Śakti
MarriageAn image of the divine couple; many families pray to Kāmeśvara-Kāmeśvarī for harmony
OppositesStillness and action, inner and outer, need each other
LoveUnion, not domination, is the shape of the highest reality

ZAPPY: So it's a philosophy, but also about how to live?

PROF. BOLT: Always both. If the highest truth is a loving couple, then love, work and stillness all have a place.

For a devotee who will never read a Tantra, the Śiva-Śakti teaching still says something clear. The world is not an obstacle to God but God’s own expression; one does not have to leave life to find the sacred.

It also offers an image of balance. Stillness without action is lifeless; action without stillness is blind. Most of us lean one way or the other; the divine couple shows the two held together.

And it puts love at the top of the universe. Not power over, but union with. That is perhaps why the image of Kāmeśvara and Kāmeśvarī has meant so much to married couples, artists and philosophers alike.

There is a quieter teaching too. If Śakti is Śiva’s own self-awareness, then a person’s capacity to notice, to reflect and to love is not a by-product of the brain but a spark of the Goddess. Paying attention, in this view, is already a kind of worship.

GLOSSARY

Words used in this guide

VimarśaSelf-reflection; awareness of awareness
Aham“I”
Icchā · Jñāna · KriyāWill · knowing · action
ArdhanārīśvaraThe Lord who is half woman
MāyāAppearance; in Śākta reading, power
PuruṣakāraMediator (Lakṣmī’s role)

SahasrāraThousand-petalled crown lotus
BinduPoint of union

Two pairs of terms often confused: śakti (power, the Goddess) and śakta (able, empowered); Śākta (a worshipper or tradition of the Goddess) and Śaiva (of Śiva). The long ā in Śākta changes the meaning from “able” to “of Śakti”.

Two more terms that appear in commentaries on this subject: spanda, “vibration”, the subtle pulse by which stillness becomes movement, a key word in Kashmiri Śaivism; and sāmarasya, “one taste”, the state in which Śiva and Śakti, or the seeker and the sought, can no longer be told apart.
