THE TEXTS OF ŚRĪVIDYĀ

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · FOUNDATIONS 4

FOREWORD

Śrīvidyā has a library: Purāṇic stories, thousand-name hymns, Tantras, a ritual rulebook, Upaniṣads, poems and philosophy. Each was written for a different reader and a different purpose.

This guide is a map. For each major text it tells you what kind of book it is, what is in it, who explained it, and whether it is open to all or studied with a teacher. Then it suggests where to start.

AT A GLANCE

The library on one page
StoryLalitopākhyāna (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa): Lalitā’s birth, wedding, city and war
NamesLalitā Sahasranāma (1,000 names) and Lalitā Triśatī (300 names)
Core TantrasVāmakeśvara Tantra (Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava + Yoginīhṛdaya); Tantrarāja Tantra
ManualParaśurāma Kalpasūtra: the practice code of many lineages
UpaniṣadsTripurā, Bhāvanā, Bahvṛca, Devī and Tripurātāpinī Upaniṣads
HymnsSaundaryalaharī; Lalitā Pañcaratnam; Durvāsā’s Lalitā Stavaratna
PhilosophyKāmakalāvilāsa; Varivasyārahasya; Tripurā Rahasya

ZAPPY: Do I have to read all of these?

PROF. BOLT: Nobody reads all of them. But knowing what each one is for stops you getting lost.

Śrīvidyā has a library that grew over many centuries and in several genres. Some texts tell stories, some list names, some lay down rules, some argue philosophy, some praise. A newcomer often meets a single title, the Sahasranāma or the Saundaryalaharī, and has no idea how it fits with the rest.

This guide is a map of that library. For each major text it says what kind of text it is, what it contains, who commented on it, and whether it belongs to the open side of the tradition or to the side studied with a teacher. The History guide placed these texts in time; this one opens their covers.

Several texts listed here have, or will have, their own guides in this series. Where that is so, we say what each guide will cover, so you can see where you are going.

KINDS OF TEXT

Six shelves
UpaniṣadClaims Vedic authority; short and dense
PurāṇaStory and praise, for everyone
TantraScripture given by Śiva to the Goddess, or the reverse; for initiates
KalpasūtraA compact rulebook of practice
StotraA hymn: praise, prayer and teaching in verse
Śāstra · BhāṣyaTreatise and commentary: philosophy, explanation, debate

ZAPPY: Which kind is most important?

PROF. BOLT: Depends who you ask. Devotees live by the stotras and the Sahasranāma. Initiates by the Tantras. Scholars by the commentaries.

Indian religious literature sorts itself by authority and audience. Śruti, the Veda including the Upaniṣads, carries the highest authority for orthodox Hindus. Purāṇas and epics are smṛti, remembered tradition, open to all. Tantras claim their own revelation, addressed to initiates. Śrīvidyā draws on all three.

Kalpasūtras are an old Vedic genre: compact, numbered rules for ritual. That Śrīvidyā produced its own kalpasūtra in the name of Paraśurāma is a sign of how much it wished to sit beside the Vedic schools as an equal.

Commentaries deserve a shelf of their own because in Indian tradition the commentator is often as important as the text. A sūtra or verse may be nearly unreadable alone; the commentary opens it up. Much of what we know about Śrīvidyā’s meaning comes through commentators like Bhāskararāya.

THE LALITOPĀKHYĀNA

Her story
WhereThe last part (Uttara Khaṇḍa) of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa
FrameThe horse-headed god Hayagrīva tells it to the sage Agastya at Kāñcī
LengthAbout forty chapters
ContentsKāma burned; Bhaṇḍa born; Lalitā rises from the fire; wedding; Śrīpura; the great war; Kāma revived
Ends withThe teaching of the Sahasranāma, and later the Triśatī
In this seriesRetold in six parts in the Lalitopākhyāna section

ZAPPY: A horse-headed god telling stories?

PROF. BOLT: Hayagrīva is a form of Viṣṇu and the god of learning. Agastya asks; he answers. That's the frame of the whole Lalitā literature.

The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa is one of the eighteen major Purāṇas. Its last section, containing the Lalitopākhyāna, is generally thought by scholars to be a later addition, but within the tradition it is the authoritative telling of Lalitā’s story.

Agastya, on pilgrimage at Kāñcī, is troubled by the suffering of the world and asks Viṣṇu for help. Viṣṇu sends Hayagrīva, who tells him the story of Lalitā as the remedy. The frame matters: the whole Lalitā literature is presented as compassion for a suffering world.

The story is vivid, full of battles, palaces and long lists of attendants. Its war chapters introduce many of the deities who later appear in the Śrīcakra and in the deity guides of this series: Vārāhī, Śyāmalā, Aśvārūḍhā, Sampatkarī, Jvālāmālinī and more.

SAHASRANĀMA AND TRIŚATĪ

The two great name-hymns
Sahasranāma1,000 names in about 183 verses; composed, the text says, by the eight Vāgdevīs, goddesses of speech
OrderBirth, form head to foot, the war, the Śrīcakra, the subtle body, philosophy
Who may chantOpenly chanted by millions; the text praises its recitation
Triśatī300 names in about sixty verses; the names follow the order of an initiatory mantra
CommentariesBhāskararāya on the Sahasranāma; a commentary on the Triśatī traditionally ascribed to Śaṅkara
On this siteTriśatī done; Sahasranāma coming in this series

ZAPPY: Why is the Triśatī treated as more private?

PROF. BOLT: Because its names are arranged on the pattern of the guru-given mantra. Some teachers keep it for initiates; others teach it openly. We say the pattern exists and leave it there.

On this site: Lalitā Triśatī, verse by verse

The Sahasranāma is unusual among thousand-name hymns in two ways. First, it uses no filler words: each name is a full name, and none is repeated. Second, its order is meaningful: it tells a story and builds a philosophy as it goes. Bhāskararāya treats this structure as proof of its divine authorship.

According to the text, Lalitā asked the eight Vāgdevīs, the goddesses of speech who preside over the letters of the alphabet, to compose a hymn of her secret names. They recited it in her court before all the gods, and she declared that anyone chanting it would please her.

The Triśatī is much shorter but considered by many to be more intimate. Its names are grouped so that they follow, in order, the syllables of a mantra the tradition keeps for initiates. Our existing verse-by-verse guide to the Triśatī explains the names without spelling out that order.

The two hymns are often printed together, with the Lalitopākhyāna’s closing chapters as their frame. In that frame Hayagrīva at first holds back the Triśatī, and gives it to Agastya only after the sage presses him, which is the text’s own way of marking it as the more inward of the two.

THE VĀMAKEŚVARA TANTRA

The first core scripture
Two halvesNityāṣoḍaśikārṇava (“ocean of the sixteen Nityās”) and Yoginīhṛdaya (“heart of the yoginīs”)
First halfThe Śrīcakra, the Nityā goddesses of the lunar days, and practice
YoginīhṛdayaThree sections: on the cakra, on the mantra, on worship; read mostly for its philosophy
VoiceŚiva teaching the Goddess
CommentariesJayaratha and Śivānanda on the first half; Amṛtānanda and Bhāskararāya (Setubandha) too

ZAPPY: Why is it called the ‘heart of the yoginīs’?

PROF. BOLT: Because it claims to give the inner meaning, the heart, that even the yoginīs guard. Its first chapter on the cakra is the deepest account of the Śrīcakra we have.

The Vāmakeśvara Tantra is the scripture historians place earliest among the core texts. Its two halves were sometimes transmitted separately, and the Yoginīhṛdaya in particular became the favourite of philosophers. The first half is more concerned with the goddesses and their worship.

The Yoginīhṛdaya presents the Śrīcakra as the unfolding of consciousness from a single point into the world. Each triangle and enclosure becomes a stage in that unfolding, and the worshipper, by reversing the movement in meditation, travels back to the source. This reading underlies the Śrīcakra section of this series.

Its commentators span centuries and regions, from Kashmir to the Tamil country, which makes the text a thread through Śrīvidyā’s history. Bhāskararāya’s Setubandha, “the bridge”, is the most widely used in the South.

THE TANTRARĀJA TANTRA

King of Tantras
Name“King of the Tantras”
SchoolThe great scripture of the Kādi school
SizeThirty-six chapters
ContentsThe Nityā goddesses in detail; the Śrīcakra; the body, time and the cosmos mapped onto the Goddess
CommentaryManoramā, begun by Subhagānanda and completed by Prakāśānanda
UseOur main source for the Tithi Nityā guides, on form, colour and meaning only

ZAPPY: Thirty-six chapters on fifteen goddesses?

PROF. BOLT: And their whole world: time, the body, the stars. The Nityās are the moon's phases, so the Tantrarāja ends up being a book about time.

The Tantrarāja is vast and detailed, and among its most characteristic features is its treatment of the Nityā goddesses. Each is given her form, colour, number of arms, weapons and associations, so that the lunar month becomes a cycle of goddesses with Lalitā at the centre.

It also maps the Goddess onto time and space: the days, the months, the planets and the body. This cosmological side made it important to astrologers and scholars as well as practitioners.

For this series it is the key source for the Nityā guides. We draw from it what is descriptive (form, colour, symbol, meaning) and leave out its mantras and procedures.

THE PARAŚURĀMA KALPASŪTRA

The practice code
FormShort prose rules (sūtras) in ten sections
TraditionTaught by Dattātreya to Paraśurāma; condensed by his disciple Sumedhā
ContentsRules for initiation, daily worship and the worship of related deities
CommentarySaubhāgyodaya of Rāmeśvara
InfluenceUmānandanātha’s Nityotsava expands it into the manual many lineages follow
Our approachWe name it and describe its scope; we do not summarise its procedures

ZAPPY: So that's the rulebook?

PROF. BOLT: For many lineages, yes. Which is exactly why it is learned with a teacher, not from a website.

The Kalpasūtra is short, terse and technical, written for people who already know the practice and need a compact reference. Without commentary it is nearly unreadable; with commentary it becomes a full manual.

The tradition’s attribution links it to two great figures: Dattātreya, the teacher of teachers, and Paraśurāma, the warrior-sage. The same pair appear in the Tripurā Rahasya, where Dattātreya teaches Paraśurāma the highest knowledge. Both have guides in the Teachers section.

We include it on this map because no account of the tradition’s texts is complete without it. Its contents, however, are exactly the part this series does not reproduce.

It was not the only manual. Later works such as the Śrīvidyārṇava Tantra, traditionally linked to the Vidyāraṇya circle at Śṛṅgeri, and numerous lineage handbooks in Sanskrit and the regional languages served the same purpose. Each lineage tends to treat its own manual as authoritative, which is one reason details of practice differ from teacher to teacher.

THE TRIPURĀ UPANIṢAD

Vedic seal of approval
LengthSixteen verses
VedaCounted with the Ṛgveda
ClaimThat Tripurā is the supreme reality, praised in Vedic language
Hidden layerIts verses are read by commentators as containing the vidyā in coded form
CommentaryBhāskararāya’s, among others
Why it mattersIt gives Śrīvidyā a place inside the Vedic canon, not only outside it

ZAPPY: A whole tradition hidden in sixteen verses?

PROF. BOLT: That's how commentators read it. On the surface it is a hymn; underneath, a map. We stay on the surface.

The Tripurā Upaniṣad presents itself as a Vedic text and uses Vedic diction, but scholars generally see it as a medieval composition that draws Śrīvidyā into the Vedic fold. Within the tradition it is treated as śruti, a source of authority above any Tantra.

On the surface it praises Tripurā as the three cities, the three paths and the one light beyond them, and celebrates her worship as leading to the highest goal. Commentators find under this surface a whole system of mantra and yantra. That layer is where we stop.

Its importance is partly polemical. In debates about whether Śrīvidyā was a respectable, Vedic practice, the existence of an Upaniṣad devoted to Tripurā was a strong argument.

Its sixteen verses are themselves a nod to the number sixteen that runs through Śrīvidyā: sixteen Nityās, the sixteen-petalled lotus of the Śrīcakra, the Goddess as the eternal sixteen-year-old Ṣoḍaśī. Whether the count was planned or later admired, commentators enjoy pointing it out.

THE BHĀVANOPANIṢAD

The body as the Śrīcakra
VedaCounted with the Atharvaveda
ThemeInner worship: each part of the Śrīcakra is matched to the human body and mind
ExampleThe guru is said to be the source of all power; the nine enclosures are the body’s own layers
UseRead as the charter of samaya, inner worship
CommentaryBhāskararāya’s, among others
In this seriesA verse-by-verse guide in the Śrīcakra section, once we have the text

ZAPPY: So the palace is inside me?

PROF. BOLT: That's the claim. The Śrīcakra on paper is a picture of what is already in you. This short text draws the map.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad is a short, almost list-like text. It takes the whole outer worship of the Śrīcakra and turns it inward, matching each element to a part of the body or a power of the mind. Bhāvanā means “making real in the mind”: worship by contemplation.

It is beloved of samaya practitioners because it shows that every outer rite has an inner meaning, and that a person who understands the inner meaning has the substance of the worship wherever they are.

We plan a verse-by-verse guide in the Śrīcakra section. Following the house rule, it will be built from a supplied text rather than from memory.

Its method is simple to state and hard to practise: wherever the outer worship uses an object, the inner worship uses a state of mind. Flowers become the senses offered back, the lamp becomes awareness, the offering of food becomes contentment. This is why some teachers call it the most important text for householders who cannot perform long rituals.

OTHER ŚĀKTA UPANIṢADS

A small family
BahvṛcaṚgvedic: the Goddess alone existed before creation; she is Brahman
Devī UpaniṣadThe Goddess declares her own nature to the gods; recited with the Devī Māhātmya
TripurātāpinīLong and technical, on Tripurā and her worship
SaubhāgyalakṣmīOn the Goddess as Śrī and inner yoga
DateLate Upaniṣads, mostly medieval; the tradition counts them as Veda

ZAPPY: Are these as old as the Kaṭha or the Īśa?

PROF. BOLT: No. Scholars place them much later. The tradition treats them as śruti anyway, which shows how much it wanted the Vedas on its side.

These Upaniṣads are short and were mostly composed in the medieval period. They are grouped as the Śākta Upaniṣads in later collections of 108 Upaniṣads, where they sit beside the ancient ones. That collection gave them a wide readership.

The Bahvṛca is only a few lines long, but it states the Śākta view with great force: she alone was before creation, and she is the Self. The Devī Upaniṣad is better known because it is recited with the Devī Māhātmya, and its opening, in which the gods ask the Goddess who she is, is a favourite.

A reader of the Upaniṣads series planned for this site will meet the ancient Upaniṣads there. These later ones belong here, with the tradition that made them.

One practical consequence: because these texts are counted as Upaniṣads, they are often recited in Vedic style, with the Vedic accents, by priests trained in the Vedas. Hearing the Devī Upaniṣad chanted in that manner at a Navarātri pūjā is one of the clearest signs of how fully Śrīvidyā entered orthodox ritual life in the South.

THE HYMNS

Praise that teaches
Saundaryalaharī100 verses; first 41 the Ānandalaharī (wave of bliss), the rest the Saundaryalaharī (wave of beauty); ascribed to Śaṅkara
Lalitā StavaratnaAlso called Āryādviśatī: about 200 verses describing her city and court; ascribed to Durvāsā
Lalitā PañcaratnamFive morning verses, ascribed to Śaṅkara
Navāvaraṇa kṛtisDīkṣitar’s eleven songs on the Śrīcakra
TamilAbhirāmi Antāti and many temple hymns

ZAPPY: Hymns as textbooks?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The Saundaryalaharī has verses on philosophy, the Śrīcakra and yoga, all in the shape of praise.

On this site: Lalitā Pañcaratnam · Saundaryalaharī and Navāvaraṇa kṛtis coming in this series

The hymns are where Śrīvidyā meets ordinary devotion. The Saundaryalaharī is sung in homes and temples across the South, often with the belief that each verse brings a particular benefit. It is also a dense philosophical and yogic text, as its commentators show.

The Lalitā Stavaratna takes the reader on a tour of the Goddess’s city, ring by ring, describing the guardians, gardens and palaces. It closely parallels the Śrīpura chapters of the Lalitopākhyāna and is a favourite of those who love vivid imagery.

Dīkṣitar’s songs are the modern summit of this genre. They will be given their own guide, describing each kṛti’s content and the Śrīcakra enclosure it sings.

PHILOSOPHY BOOKS

For the thinking reader
KāmakalāvilāsaPuṇyānanda; about fifty-five verses: Śiva and Śakti as light and reflection, the Śrīcakra as their unfolding
VarivasyārahasyaBhāskararāya: the meanings of the vidyā, discussed at the level of philosophy
Tripurā RahasyaDattātreya teaches Paraśurāma; the Jñāna Khaṇḍa is a classic of non-dual thought told through stories
SubhagodayaShort work traditionally ascribed to Gauḍapāda
SaubhāgyabhāskaraBhāskararāya’s commentary on the Sahasranāma is also a philosophy book in disguise

ZAPPY: Is there a book I could just read?

PROF. BOLT: The Tripurā Rahasya. It's stories, conversations and ideas, with nothing guarded. Ramaṇa Maharṣi used to recommend it.

The Kāmakalāvilāsa is the classic short account of Śrīvidyā’s philosophy: how the one light becomes self-aware, how that awareness becomes sound, and how sound becomes the world. It is technical but brief, and modern translations make it accessible.

The Tripurā Rahasya is different in kind. It is narrative philosophy: stories of a prince, Hemacūḍa, taught by his wife Hemalekhā; of sages and kings; of people waking up from the dream of ordinary life. Its teaching is close to Advaita, and it has been admired by teachers far outside Śrīvidyā.

Ramaṇa Maharṣi’s fondness for the Tripurā Rahasya is well recorded and helped make it popular in the 20th century. Its Jñāna Khaṇḍa will have its own guides in the Teachers section.

WHERE TO START

A suggested reading order
1Lalitopākhyāna, for the story (our six-part retelling)
2Lalitā Sahasranāma, with a good translation of Bhāskararāya
3Saundaryalaharī, for beauty and imagery
4Kāmakalāvilāsa or a modern study, for the philosophy
5Tripurā Rahasya (Jñāna Khaṇḍa), for the goal
Not firstTantras and the Kalpasūtra: these are for study with a teacher

ZAPPY: Why story first?

PROF. BOLT: Because the names make sense once you know who Bhaṇḍa was and what the war was about. Philosophy lands better after a story.

This order follows the tradition’s own advice that the outer, open texts prepare the mind for the inner ones. Story first, then names, then praise, then philosophy. The Tantras and the Kalpasūtra are for those who go further, with a teacher.

For the Sahasranāma, a translation that includes Bhāskararāya’s explanations makes all the difference: many names are opaque without them. Several good English editions exist, and our own verse-by-verse guide will draw on the commentarial tradition.

Above all, the tradition says: read slowly. These texts were meant to be recited, memorised and reflected on, not skimmed.

If you prefer to listen first, music is another door. Dīkṣitar’s Navāvaraṇa kṛtis and recordings of the Saundaryalaharī sung in the South introduce the imagery of the tradition through the ear. Many devotees knew the songs for years before they ever opened a text.

On titles: many Tantric titles use water images. Arṇava (ocean), laharī (wave), and kalpa (sometimes compared to a rule as fixed as the shore). The image is of a vast body of knowledge, of which any text is a wave or a drop.

One more distinction worth keeping: mūla, the root text, and ṭīkā or vyākhyā, the commentary on it. When a scholar cites “the Setubandha on the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava”, the first title is the commentary, the second the mūla. Many Indian books are printed with both on the same page, root text above, commentary below.
