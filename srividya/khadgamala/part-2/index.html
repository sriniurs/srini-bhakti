THE SIXTEEN NITYĀS

Set 3 · the eternal ones of the lunar days

KHAḌGAMĀLĀ · 2 OF 13

FOREWORD

After calling the Mother and naming her limbs, the garland greets her moon days. The fifteen Tithi Nityās are the fifteen lunar days, the phases of the Goddess as the moon; the sixteenth, Mahānityā, is Lalitā herself.

On the Śrīcakra they sit along the three sides of the central triangle, five to a side, with Mahānityā at the bindu. Each has her own guide in our Tithi Nityā series; this part shows them as the hymn names them, in order and in place.

Śrī Lalitāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, are these the same Nityās from the moon series?

PROF. BOLT: The very same, Zappy. Here they’re part of the walk: sixteen names, sixteen places on the triangle.

SET 3 ON THE ŚRĪCAKRA

Where the Nityās sit
WhereOn the three sides of the central triangle, five on each side
OrderKāmeśvarī near the west corner, then anticlockwise
Right sideNityās 1–5, rising from the west corner
Top sideNityās 6–10, from right to left
Left sideNityās 11–15, coming down to the west corner
The sixteenthMahānityā at the bindu
Names16

ZAPPY: So the triangle is a clock of the month?

PROF. BOLT: A good picture: fifteen marks round the edge, and the one that never moves at the centre.

Srini’s diagram numbers the Nityās in chanting order. Starting beside the west corner of the downward-pointing triangle, the row rises up the right side, crosses the top from right to left, and comes down the left side, so that Citrā, the fifteenth, sits beside the same corner where Kāmeśvarī began. Mahānityā shines at the bindu.

This placement belongs to the eighth enclosure of the Śrīcakra, the central triangle, whose three corners are held by the “great” forms of three Nityās: Mahākāmeśvarī, Mahāvajreśvarī and Mahābhagamālinī. Those three appear later in the garland, in Part 11.

ZAPPY: Some names end in -e, some in -ī. Why?

PROF. BOLT: The -e is the vocative, the ‘O …!’ form: Nityaklinne is ‘O Nityaklinnā’. Names in -ī stay the same when called.

Each of these names has a guide of its own in the Tithi Nityā series, with her form from the meditation verse, her part in the night battle, and her meaning. Here they are simply called, one after another, as the hymn calls them.

In the garland the names come without pause. The meanings above are for understanding; in chanting, attention rests on each devī in her place on the triangle.

ZAPPY: Mahānitye is the sixteenth?

PROF. BOLT: Lalitā herself, the digit of the moon that never wanes. The whole cycle returns to her.

The second half of the row moves from learning and the body (Kulasundarī, Nityā) to the banner and victory (Nīlapatākā, Vijayā), to blessing and purifying fire (Sarvamaṅgalā, Jvālāmālinī), and to the full moon (Citrā). Then comes the sixteenth, who is not a day at all.

The Khaḍgamālā names sixteen here, not fifteen. That is the teaching of the whole Nityā system in a single list: the fifteen changing days are her rays, and she is the sixteenth that does not change.

ZAPPY: Can I use this as a monthly practice?

PROF. BOLT: Many people greet the Nityā of the day. The overview guide explains how the days are counted.

CHANT THIS SET

kāmeśvarī, bhagamālinī, nityaklinne, bheruṇḍe, vahnivāsinī, mahāvajreśvarī, śivadūtī, tvarite, kulasundarī, nitye, nīlapatāke, vijaye, sarvamaṅgale, jvālāmālinī, citre, mahānitye

Said aloud at a steady pace, the sixteen names take well under a minute. Many who recite the Khaḍgamālā daily pause briefly on the Nityā of that day’s tithi, so that the hymn and the calendar meet. That is a devotional habit, not a rule of the text.

Readers who want to explore further can find each Nityā’s guide from the Tithi Nityā overview on this site. Each guide gives her lunar day, her meditation verse, her story and her meaning.

From the Lalitopākhyāna
Night battleWhen Bhaṇḍa’s brother attacks Lalitā’s camp by night, all fifteen Nityās fight under Kāmeśvarī
LightVahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī light up the dark field
Fire wallNext day Jvālāmālinī becomes a ring of fire round the camp
HealingLalitā heals the wounded Nityās with one glance
SahasranāmaCalls Lalitā “Nityāṣoḍaśikārūpā”: whose form is the sixteen Nityās

ZAPPY: So the names I chant are warriors too?

PROF. BOLT: Warriors, moon days and rays of the queen, all at once.

The booklet places the Nityās on the triangle; the Purāṇa places them on the battlefield; the calendar places them in the month. All three pictures say the same thing: the Goddess’s closest companions are the days of time, and she is always at their centre.

The Nityās’ great scene is the night battle of the Lalitopākhyāna. Bhaṇḍa’s brother Viṣaṅga creeps round to attack Lalitā’s rear after dark; the Nityās ask leave to fight, and under Kāmeśvarī they destroy fifteen generals in three watches of the night.

The Sahasranāma keeps this memory in two names: Lalitā is “eager to watch the valour of the Nityās”, and she “stands within the fire-rampart thrown up by Jvālāmālinī”. A recitation of these sixteen names carries the whole story with it.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Names, devī positions and diagrams from Srini’s own Śrī Devī Khaḍgamālā booklet. Readings of the enclosures in the body follow the Bhāvanopaniṣad as summarised in modern studies; the Lalitā Sahasranāma; the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna (G. V. Tagare’s translation); Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis (described).

Sources differ / unverified: Editions of the Khaḍgamālā differ slightly in their names and their order; we follow the booklet. Lists of the siddhis usually have ten and leave out Garimā; this edition has eleven. Placement of the devīs within each enclosure follows the common anticlockwise order from the west, as the booklet says; where a teacher’s tradition differs, follow the teacher. The gems linked with each enclosure are the booklet’s scheme; other traditions give other correspondences. The booklet also gives seed-syllables and practice instructions for each set; we leave those out.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
