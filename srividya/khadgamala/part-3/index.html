ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

THE THREE LINES OF GURUS

Set 4 · divine, perfected and human teachers

KHAḌGAMĀLĀ · 3 OF 13

FOREWORD

Before entering the city of the Mother, the garland bows to the teachers who carry Śrīvidyā. Twenty are named, in three streams: eight divine (divyaugha), four perfected (siddhaugha) and eight human (mānavaugha).

On the Śrīcakra they are placed in three rows inside the central triangle, above the bindu: the teachers sit closest to the Goddess. Almost every name after the first two ends in -mayī, “you who are present as…”: the Goddess herself is greeted in the form of each teacher.

Śrī Lalitāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, why bow to teachers before the Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Because the teaching reached us through them, Zappy. And the hymn says she is in each of them.

SET 4 ON THE ŚRĪCAKRA

Three rows above the bindu
WhereThree rows inside the central triangle, above the bindu
Row 1Divyaugha, the divine stream: names 1–8
Row 2Siddhaugha, the perfected stream: names 9–12
Row 3Mānavaugha, the human stream: names 13–20
Names8 + 4 + 8 = 20
DirectionFrom the divine teachers down to the human ones

ZAPPY: Why inside the central triangle?

PROF. BOLT: The teachers are placed nearest the centre. In Śrīvidyā the guru and the Goddess are not two.

The booklet’s diagram shows the three rows as rings of numbered circles in the upper part of the central triangle, with the bindu below them. The divine stream is widest, at the top; the perfected stream is a short row of four; the human stream, at the bottom, sits just above the bindu.

The order of the hymn runs from Śiva and Śakti downward through the streams to the human teachers who passed the teaching into the world. Chanting it is a way of tracing one’s own teaching back to its source.

ZAPPY: Lopāmudrā and Agastya are here!

PROF. BOLT: The same pair who heard Lalitā’s story from Hayagrīva. Their guide in the teachers series tells it.

After Parameśvara and Parameśvarī come four masters, Mitreśa, Ṣaṣṭhīśa, Uḍḍīśa and Caryānātha, who are honoured in several Kaula traditions as early founders. Different accounts link them with sacred seats or ages of the world; we do not choose between those accounts.

The divine stream ends with Lopāmudrā and Agastya. The first is a woman seer of the Ṛgveda, after whom a whole school of Śrīvidyā is named; the second heard the Lalitopākhyāna and the Sahasranāma from Hayagrīva. Both have a guide in our teachers series.

ZAPPY: Who were these four?

PROF. BOLT: Perfected masters whose names survive in lineage lists like this one. History records almost nothing else about them.

Kālatāpaśa may be read as “he who burns away time”, Muktakeśīśvara as “lord of the loose-haired goddess”, a name of Kālī-like forms of the Goddess. These are readings of the words, not histories of the men; the lineage lists give the names and nothing more.

The siddhas stand between the divine and the human. In Indian religious thought a siddha is one who has reached perfection and can pass between worlds; in lineage lists they are the bridge by which teaching came down.

Some of the names are suggestive, “teacher of dharma”, “lord of the lamp’s digit”, and commentators sometimes read them as the qualities a teacher should have. As names, they are all that remains of these masters.

ZAPPY: Are these the only human gurus?

PROF. BOLT: They’re the ones this hymn names. In worship, many lineages add the names of their own teachers after them.

The human stream brings the teaching into the world of people. Eight are named here, ending with Śrī Rāmānanda. Lists of this human line vary between traditions, and in actual worship many lineages continue it with their own gurus, ending with the worshipper’s own teacher.

With the twentieth name the preliminaries end. The garland has called the Mother, named her limbs, greeted her moon days and bowed to her teachers. It now goes to the outer gate of her city.

“PRESENT AS”

What -mayī means
-mayī“Made of, consisting of, present as”
Mitreśamayī“You who are present as Mitreśa”
Who is calledThe Goddess, in the form of the teacher
TeachingThe guru is not separate from the Goddess
In practiceRespect for the teacher is respect for her
First twoParameśvara and Parameśvarī: Śiva and Śakti themselves, the first teachers

ZAPPY: So when I say ‘Agastyamayī’, I’m calling her, not him?

PROF. BOLT: Both, in one name: her, present as him. That’s the whole idea of the guru in Śrīvidyā.

CHANT THIS SET

parameśvara, parameśvarī, mitreśamayī, ṣaṣṭhīśamayī, uḍḍīśamayī, caryānāthamayī, lopāmudrāmayī, agastyamayī, kālatāpaśamayī, dharmācāryamayī, muktakeśīśvaramayī, dīpakalānāthamayī, viṣṇudevamayī, prabhākaradevamayī, tejodevamayī, manojadevamayī, kalyāṇadevamayī, vāsudevamayī, ratnadevamayī, śrīrāmānandamayī

The suffix -maya (feminine -mayī) means “consisting of” or “full of”. In the Khaḍgamālā it turns each teacher’s name into a name of the Goddess. The worshipper does not bow to the teachers instead of her, but to her as them.

This is one of the clearest expressions of a central Śrīvidyā teaching: guru, mantra, deity and worshipper are ultimately one. The hymn puts it simply, in a single suffix repeated eighteen times.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Names, devī positions and diagrams from Srini’s own Śrī Devī Khaḍgamālā booklet. Readings of the enclosures in the body follow the Bhāvanopaniṣad as summarised in modern studies; the Lalitā Sahasranāma; the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna (G. V. Tagare’s translation); Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis (described).

Sources differ / unverified: Editions of the Khaḍgamālā differ slightly in their names and their order; we follow the booklet. Lists of the siddhis usually have ten and leave out Garimā; this edition has eleven. Placement of the devīs within each enclosure follows the common anticlockwise order from the west, as the booklet says; where a teacher’s tradition differs, follow the teacher. The gems linked with each enclosure are the booklet’s scheme; other traditions give other correspondences. The booklet also gives seed-syllables and practice instructions for each set; we leave those out.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
