ĀVARAṆA 1 · TRAILOKYAMOHANA

Set 5 · the circuit that enchants the three worlds · explained and illustrated

KHAḌGAMĀLĀ · 4 OF 13

HOW THIS WORKS: Devī Khaḍgamālā, Part 4: the first enclosure of the Śrīcakra, the outer square. Its three lines, the eleven siddhis, the eight Mothers and the ten mudrā devīs, each with her direction on Srini’s diagrams, and the enclosure’s queen and yoginī.

FOREWORD

The garland now enters the Śrīcakra at its outermost enclosure, the bhūpura: the square with four gates, called Trailokyamohana, “the circuit that enchants the three worlds”. It holds more devīs than any other enclosure, 31 names, arranged in three lines from the outside in.

The outer line holds the siddhis, the powers of yoga. The middle line holds the eight Mothers. The inner line holds the ten mudrā devīs. Then come the queen and the yoginī of the enclosure. Each line has its own diagram.

Śrī Lalitāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, why so many at the gate?

PROF. BOLT: A city needs strong gates, Zappy. Here are the powers, the Mothers and the seals, all guarding the way in.

ZAPPY: Three lines, one square?

PROF. BOLT: The square is drawn as three nested lines. Each line has its own family of devīs.

The first enclosure is the boundary between the world and the Goddess’s city. Its square shape, with a gate on each side, recalls a temple compound. In Śrīvidyā’s own terms its presiding form of the Goddess is Tripurā, and its yoginī is called manifest, because what is outermost is what everyone can see.

This is the busiest enclosure: three lines and thirty-one names. The guide to Āvaraṇa 1 in our Śrīcakra series describes the square and its meaning; this part follows the hymn and places each devī on the booklet’s diagrams.

ZAPPY: Eleven powers at the gate?

PROF. BOLT: Lists of ten leave out Garimā; this edition has her beside Laghimā in the north. Nadir and zenith are below and above the square.

The siddhis are the classical powers of yoga: becoming minute or vast, light or heavy, ruling and mastering, getting whatever is willed. They glow like molten gold in the booklet’s description and hold the goad and the noose.

Indian tradition has always been cautious about them. They are real attainments, but not the goal; a seeker who stops for them stops at the gate. Placing them on the outermost line of the Śrīcakra says exactly that.

ZAPPY: Vārāhī is here!

PROF. BOLT: Lalitā’s commander, Daṇḍanāthā. She has her own guide in the deities series.

The eight Mothers are the powers of the great gods given form as goddesses: Brāhmī of Brahmā, Māheśvarī of Śiva, Kaumārī of Skanda, Vaiṣṇavī of Viṣṇu, Vārāhī of the Boar, Māhendrī of Indra, Cāmuṇḍā, and Mahālakṣmī. Many of them fight in the Devī Māhātmya.

Each is said to master an inner enemy. Adorned with every ornament, they grant what is wished for, the booklet says; inwardly they ask the worshipper to face desire, anger and the rest before going deeper.

ZAPPY: What are the mudrā devīs?

PROF. BOLT: Ten devīs named after the ten sacred hand-seals of the worship. We name them; the seals are learned from a teacher.

The ten mudrā devīs are named after the mudrās used in the Navāvaraṇa worship: one for each of the nine enclosures, and one for the whole. Their names describe what grace does: it stirs, melts, attracts, controls, intoxicates, drives, lifts into the sky, plants a seed, gives birth, and holds all three worlds.

They are red in colour, the booklet says. We name them and give their meanings; the hand-seals themselves belong to practice under a teacher.

ZAPPY: Why does the yoginī get more secret?

PROF. BOLT: Because the walk goes inward. What’s outside is plain to see; what’s at the centre is the most hidden.

The cakrasvāminī of each enclosure is a form of Lalitā herself, ruling that layer. In this hymn she is named by the enclosure: here, the queen of the Trailokyamohana cakra. The nine queens are named again together at the end of the garland, in Part 13.

The yoginīs grow more hidden at each step inward: manifest, hidden, more hidden, of the tradition, beyond the clan, inmost, secret, most secret, and supreme secret. Following them is like following a single thread to the centre.

READING THE OUTER SQUARE

What the three lines mean

MothersThe Bhāvanopaniṣad links them to desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride, envy, merit and sin
Mudrā devīsGlow red; each rules one of the ten seals of the worship
The whole squareThe world of the senses, through which every journey begins
MoreThe Āvaraṇa 1 guide in the Śrīcakra series gives the enclosure’s Dīkṣitar song and story links

ZAPPY: So the gate tests what I’m looking for?

PROF. BOLT: That’s one reading. Powers glitter at the gate; the Goddess is inside.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad, a short Upaniṣad that reads the Śrīcakra as the human body, links the outer square’s devīs to the forces that rule ordinary life. In its reading, the worship of the first enclosure is the mastering of those forces.

Readers who want more on this enclosure, its Tripurā and its Dīkṣitar song, Kamalāmbā saṃrakṣatu in rāga Ānandabhairavī, will find them in the Āvaraṇa 1 guide.

THE PRESIDING TRIPURĀ

Tripurā, mistress of Āvaraṇa 1

Who rules here. Each of the nine enclosures has a presiding form of the Goddess, one of the nine Tripurās. Over Āvaraṇa 1, Trailokyamohana, presides Tripurā.

The yoginī. With her is the yoginī of the enclosure, Prakaṭa: the manifest. The yoginīs grow more hidden as the garland moves inward: the manifest, the hidden, the more hidden, and so on to the supreme secret at the bindu.

In the garland. The Khaḍgamālā names the enclosure’s queen, its cakrasvāminī, and its yoginī at the end of each set; the Tripurā herself is honoured in the full Navāvaraṇa worship. Our Śrīcakra series gives each enclosure its own guide.

ZAPPY: Nine Tripurās for nine enclosures?

PROF. BOLT: One Goddess, Zappy, wearing nine names as you walk in. The names change; she doesn’t.

Tripurā rules Āvaraṇa 1.

THE MUDRĀ AND THE SIDDHI

Sarvasaṅkṣobhiṇī · Aṇimā

The mudrā. Each enclosure has a mudrā-devī, a power shown by a hand gesture in the worship. Here it is Sarvasaṅkṣobhiṇī, “she who stirs all”. The gestures themselves are taught within the tradition; we name them only.

The siddhi. Each enclosure also has a siddhi, an attainment. Here it is Aṇimā: becoming as small as an atom. The ten siddhi-devīs stand at the outer square; each enclosure is linked with one of them.

A reading. Many teachers read the mudrā as the first shaking awake of a sleeping mind, and the siddhi not as a magic power but as an inner capacity ripening on the way to the centre. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: So I get a superpower in each enclosure?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition warns against chasing them, Zappy. They’re signposts, not the goal.

Sarvasaṅkṣobhiṇī and Aṇimā.

DĪKṢITAR’S SONG

The Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛti

The composer. Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar (1775–1835), one of the three great composers of Karnatak music, sang the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra in nine songs to Kamalāmbā, the Goddess of Tiruvārūr in Tamil Nadu.

This enclosure’s song. For Āvaraṇa 1 he composed Kamalāmbike āśrita-kalpalatike, in rāga Toḍi. The songs, with the dhyāna and maṅgala songs, run through the cases of Sanskrit grammar one by one.

How to use it. Hearing the song after chanting the set is a lovely way to stay with the enclosure. Many recordings exist; listen with the names of this set in mind.

ZAPPY: A song for every enclosure?

PROF. BOLT: Nine of them, Zappy, plus an opening and a closing one. A whole Śrīcakra in music.

Kamalāmbike āśrita-kalpalatike · rāga Toḍi.

CHANTING THIS SET

Slowly, name by name

Aloud and steady. Say each name of this set clearly, at an even pace, letting attention rest on each devī in her place on the diagram, starting from the west in front of you and moving anticlockwise.

Without the seeds. In printed editions the names come with seed-syllables given at initiation. We do not print them; the names alone, said with attention, are how most householders recite.

With a teacher. The full Navāvaraṇa worship, with mudrās and offerings, is learned from a teacher. Follow your own teacher’s order where it differs from ours.

ZAPPY: Can I just read the names?

PROF. BOLT: With attention and respect, Zappy, yes. The rest comes with a teacher.

Name by name, with attention.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Names, devī positions and diagrams from Srini’s own Śrī Devī Khaḍgamālā booklet. Readings of the enclosures in the body follow the Bhāvanopaniṣad as summarised in modern studies; the Lalitā Sahasranāma; the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna (G. V. Tagare’s translation); Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis (described). See also the guide to Āvaraṇa 1 in the Śrīcakra series.

Sources differ / unverified: Editions of the Khaḍgamālā differ slightly in their names and their order; we follow the booklet. Lists of the siddhis usually have ten and leave out Garimā; this edition has eleven. Placement of the devīs within each enclosure follows the common anticlockwise order from the west, as the booklet says; where a teacher’s tradition differs, follow the teacher. The gems linked with each enclosure are the booklet’s scheme; other traditions give other correspondences. The booklet also gives seed-syllables and practice instructions for each set; we leave those out.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
