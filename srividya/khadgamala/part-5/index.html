ĀVARAṆA 2 · SARVĀŚĀPARIPŪRAKA

Set 6 · the circuit that fulfils every hope · explained and illustrated

KHAḌGAMĀLĀ · 5 OF 13

HOW THIS WORKS: Devī Khaḍgamālā, Part 5: the second enclosure of the Śrīcakra, the sixteen-petalled lotus, and its sixteen Ākarṣiṇīs, “the attracting ones”, each in her petal on Srini’s diagram, with the enclosure’s queen and its hidden yoginī.

FOREWORD

Past the square, the garland enters the sixteen-petalled lotus, Sarvāśāparipūraka, “the circuit that fulfils every hope”. In each petal sits an Ākarṣiṇī, an “attracting one”.

One by one they draw back the scattered powers of the person: desire, intellect and the sense of “I”; the five senses; mind, courage, memory and name; seed, self, nectar and body. Each is offered back to the Mother. They are linked with the sixteen vowels.

Śrī Lalitāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Prof, what do they attract?

PROF. BOLT: You, Zappy, piece by piece. Everything scattered out in the world is gathered back to her.

ZAPPY: Why ‘fulfils every hope’?

PROF. BOLT: Because what you hope for is gathered and offered to her, and she fulfils it.

The second enclosure is a ring of sixteen lotus petals just inside the three circles of the outer square. Its presiding form of the Goddess is Tripureśī, and its yoginī is called gupta, hidden: the first step from what is plain into what is not.

The booklet describes the Ākarṣiṇīs as drawing back the scattered powers of the person one by one and offering them to the Mother. That is the simplest way to understand this enclosure.

SET 6 ON THE ŚRĪCAKRA

Where the devīs of Āvaraṇa 2 sit
WhereThe sixteen petals of the outer lotus
OrderPetal 1 at the west, in front of you, then anticlockwise
Names16 + queen and yoginī
VowelsThe sixteen petals are linked with the sixteen Sanskrit vowels

ZAPPY: Petal 1 is right in front of me?

PROF. BOLT: At the west, where you sit. Then round anticlockwise, petal by petal.

Srini’s diagram numbers the petals from the west, in front of the worshipper, and goes round anticlockwise. As the names are chanted, attention moves from petal to petal round the ring.

The petals sit on the outer of the two lotus rings of the Śrīcakra. The inner ring, of eight petals, is the next enclosure.

ZAPPY: The first ones attract senses?

PROF. BOLT: After desire, intellect and ego come sound, touch, form, taste and smell: the five sense-objects.

The first eight draw back the deepest drives first: desire, intellect, and the sense of “I”. Then the five sense-objects, sound, touch, form, taste and smell, through which attention runs out into the world.

Each name has the same shape: something, then ākarṣiṇī, “she who attracts”. The repetition is part of the effect: one thing after another is gathered.

ZAPPY: Name and body at the end?

PROF. BOLT: The last things we let go of. Even they are drawn back and offered.

The second eight go deeper and closer: mind, courage, memory, name, the seed or subtle cause, the self, the nectar of life, and the body. The last two, nectar and body, are what we hold onto most.

With all sixteen drawn back, nothing of the person is left scattered. In the booklet’s words, they are offered to the Mother.

The queen of this enclosure is the cakrasvāminī of Sarvāśāparipūraka. Its yoginī is Gupta, the hidden one, the first of the yoginīs who are not visible from outside.

READING THE SIXTEEN

Gathering the self
The imagePowers scattered through the senses are drawn back, like threads to a centre
BhāvanopaniṣadReads the sixteen as the five elements, the ten organs and the mind
VowelsSixteen petals, sixteen vowels: the sounds that give every word its life
MoonSome link them also with the sixteen digits of the moon
MoreThe Āvaraṇa 2 guide gives Tripureśī, the story links and Dīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re

ZAPPY: So this enclosure is about gathering yourself?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the reading. Before going further in, the scattered self is gathered.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad reads the sixteen as the five elements, the ten organs of sense and action, and the mind, the sixteen “transformations” that make up a person. The worship of this enclosure, in its reading, is the recognition that all of these belong to the Goddess.

Dīkṣitar’s song for this enclosure, Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re in rāga Kalyāṇī, names these Ākarṣiṇīs too. The Āvaraṇa 2 guide describes the song.

THE PRESIDING TRIPURĀ

Tripureśī, mistress of Āvaraṇa 2

Who rules here. Each of the nine enclosures has a presiding form of the Goddess, one of the nine Tripurās. Over Āvaraṇa 2, Sarvāśāparipūraka, presides Tripureśī.

The yoginī. With her is the yoginī of the enclosure, Gupta (the hidden). The yoginīs grow more hidden as the garland moves inward: the manifest, the hidden, the more hidden, and so on to the supreme secret at the bindu.

In the garland. The Khaḍgamālā names the enclosure’s queen, its cakrasvāminī, and its yoginī at the end of each set; the Tripurā herself is honoured in the full Navāvaraṇa worship. Our Śrīcakra series gives each enclosure its own guide.

ZAPPY: Nine Tripurās for nine enclosures?

PROF. BOLT: One Goddess, Zappy, wearing nine names as you walk in. The names change; she doesn’t.

Tripureśī rules Āvaraṇa 2.

THE MUDRĀ AND THE SIDDHI

Sarvavidrāviṇī · Laghimā

The mudrā. Each enclosure has a mudrā-devī, a power shown by a hand gesture in the worship. Here it is Sarvavidrāviṇī, “she who melts all”. The gestures themselves are taught within the tradition; we name them only.

The siddhi. Each enclosure also has a siddhi, an attainment. Here it is Laghimā: becoming light. The ten siddhi-devīs stand at the outer square; each enclosure is linked with one of them.

A reading. Many teachers read the mudrā as hardness and resistance melting away, and the siddhi not as a magic power but as an inner capacity ripening on the way to the centre. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: So I get a superpower in each enclosure?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition warns against chasing them, Zappy. They’re signposts, not the goal.

Sarvavidrāviṇī and Laghimā.

DĪKṢITAR’S SONG

The Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛti

The composer. Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar (1775–1835), one of the three great composers of Karnatak music, sang the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra in nine songs to Kamalāmbā, the Goddess of Tiruvārūr in Tamil Nadu.

This enclosure’s song. For Āvaraṇa 2 he composed Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re, in rāga Kalyāṇī. Its text addresses the Goddess in the accusative (dvitīyā) case: the nine songs, with the dhyāna and maṅgala songs, run through the cases of Sanskrit grammar one by one.

How to use it. Hearing the song after chanting the set is a lovely way to stay with the enclosure. Many recordings exist; listen with the names of this set in mind.

ZAPPY: A song for every enclosure?

PROF. BOLT: Nine of them, Zappy, plus an opening and a closing one. A whole Śrīcakra in music.

Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re · rāga Kalyāṇī.

CHANTING THIS SET

Slowly, name by name

Aloud and steady. Say each name of this set clearly, at an even pace, letting attention rest on each devī in her place on the diagram, starting from the west in front of you and moving anticlockwise.

Without the seeds. In printed editions the names come with seed-syllables given at initiation. We do not print them; the names alone, said with attention, are how most householders recite.

With a teacher. The full Navāvaraṇa worship, with mudrās and offerings, is learned from a teacher. Follow your own teacher’s order where it differs from ours.

ZAPPY: Can I just read the names?

PROF. BOLT: With attention and respect, Zappy, yes. The rest comes with a teacher.

Name by name, with attention.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Names, devī positions and diagrams from Srini’s own Śrī Devī Khaḍgamālā booklet. Readings of the enclosures in the body follow the Bhāvanopaniṣad as summarised in modern studies; the Lalitā Sahasranāma; the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, Lalitopākhyāna (G. V. Tagare’s translation); Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar’s Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis (described). See also the guide to Āvaraṇa 2 in the Śrīcakra series.

Sources differ / unverified: Editions of the Khaḍgamālā differ slightly in their names and their order; we follow the booklet. Lists of the siddhis usually have ten and leave out Garimā; this edition has eleven. Placement of the devīs within each enclosure follows the common anticlockwise order from the west, as the booklet says; where a teacher’s tradition differs, follow the teacher. The gems linked with each enclosure are the booklet’s scheme; other traditions give other correspondences. The booklet also gives seed-syllables and practice instructions for each set; we leave those out.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
