HOW THIS WORKS: The dhyāna and the ślokas in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and every name in the word table; then a deep dive.

WHERE WE ARE

The Lalitā Sahasranāma, the thousand names of Lalitā, is part of the Lalitopākhyāna of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa. Hayagrīva tells the sage Agastya that the eight Vāgdevīs, goddesses of speech, composed it at Lalitā’s command. It has 183 ślokas and, by tradition, exactly a thousand names, with no word repeated as filler. Its great commentary is Bhāskararāya’s Saubhāgyabhāskara.

This series takes it in ten parts. Part 1 gives the four dhyāna verses and ślokas 1–21, names 1–54: the Mother rising from the fire of consciousness, her four weapons, and her form from her hair to her feet, ending with her seated on Kāmeśvara’s lap.

Printed editions, including the Vaidika Vignanam text we follow, begin with a viniyoga, a karanyāsa and aṅganyāsa with seed-syllables, and five offerings. Those belong to initiated practice; we leave them out and say so. The guru-verse and the dhyāna are included.

DHYĀNA 1 · DEEP DIVE

THE RED GODDESS

🔎 In context

The Sahasranāma is usually recited after a dhyāna, a set of meditation verses that bring her form before the mind. The edition we follow gives four. This first one gives the essentials: her colour, her compassion, her four weapons and the ring of powers around her, the siddhis of the Śrīcakra’s outer square.

The four dhyāna verses are not counted among the thousand names. Each gives a picture to hold in the mind before chanting, and traditions choose one or recite all four.

📖 What the commentary adds

The last words of this verse, aham ity eva vibhāvaye, “I meditate on her as myself”, state the rule of Śrīvidyā meditation: the worshipper does not stand outside the deity but identifies with her. The Tripurā Rahasya and the Bhāvanopaniṣad both build on this.

The four weapons named here are explained in names 8–11. Bhāskararāya maps them onto the three powers of the Goddess (Yoginīhṛdaya 1.53): the noose is will, the goad is knowledge, the bow and arrows are action.

📜 Looking deeper

The last line is the most important: “I meditate on Bhavānī as I.” The worshipper is asked to see the Goddess not as someone else but as their own deepest self. That is the highest teaching of Śrīvidyā, placed at the very start. Before the dhyāna, editions also print a viniyoga and nyāsa with seed-syllables; we leave those out.

The last line is the boldest in the dhyāna: not “I worship her” but “I meditate that I am she”. This is the non-dual heart of Śrīvidyā, echoed by Śaṅkara’s tradition and by the Tripurā Rahasya.

DHYĀNA 2 · DEEP DIVE

THE GOLDEN LOTUS FORM

🔎 In context

The second dhyāna verse shows a gentler, golden form, seated on a lotus and holding one. It is the form many households keep in their shrines: calm, smiling, bending towards those who come to her. She is called Śrīvidyā itself, the knowledge that the whole tradition is named for.

📖 What the commentary adds

This verse is the only one of the four that calls her Śrīvidyā outright, the name of the knowledge itself. In Bhāskararāya’s terms (name 25) śuddhavidyā, pure knowledge, is Śrīvidyā, and the Goddess and her mantra are not two.

The open right hand (abhaya) giving fearlessness is the gesture name 121, bhayāpahā, describes. Bhāskararāya supports that name with Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.9: one who knows the bliss of Brahman fears nothing.

📜 Looking deeper

Notice “bending towards her devotees”, bhaktanamrā. The Goddess, worshipped by all the gods, leans towards the worshipper. It is the same tenderness that the Saundaryalaharī describes again and again: the queen who comes to meet those who call her.

Different dhyānas suit different temperaments. The red ones stress compassion and power; this golden one stresses peace and prosperity, and is often chosen by householders.

Calm is a form of power too.

DHYĀNA 3 · DEEP DIVE

FRAGRANT WITH KUṄKUMA

🔎 In context

The third verse is fragrant and red: kuṅkuma, musk, red garlands, the red hibiscus. It names the moment of meditation precisely: japavidhau, at the time of japa, the repetition of her name or mantra.

📖 What the commentary adds

Kuṅkuma and musk are the cosmetics of the married woman, and of the Goddess in temple worship. Sastry notes in his translation that red stands for the Goddess and white for Śiva throughout the hymn.

Japā, the hibiscus, is the flower most offered to the Goddess; its very name sounds like japa, recitation. The verse links the flower offered with the hand and the name offered with the voice.

📜 Looking deeper

There is a pun at the end. Japā is the hibiscus; japa is repetition. The poet remembers the hibiscus-red Mother at the time of japa. The verse is a practical instruction: picture her like this when you begin to recite.

The verse is full of scents and colours. Commentators note that such detailed dhyānas train the mind to stay in one place: a vivid image holds attention better than an abstract one.

DHYĀNA 4 · DEEP DIVE

THE SUPREME MOTHER

🔎 In context

The fourth dhyāna is the form most often printed at the head of the Sahasranāma. She is red, three-eyed like Śiva, crowned with the moon, holding a cup and a red lotus, her feet on a jewelled pot. Some editions place this verse first; ours places it fourth.

This verse is the one most often recited before the Sahasranāma in South India, and is printed first in many editions. Our text gives it last.

📖 What the commentary adds

The jewelled cup of honey-wine in her hand is read by commentators of the samaya school as the overflowing bliss of the Self. Bhāskararāya himself, on name 330, records the Tripurā Upaniṣad’s offerings but advises that such outer offerings be reduced over time.

Her red feet resting on a jewelled pot of plenty make the point of the whole verse: everything is gathered at her feet. In the hymn the feet are the place of refuge (names 44–46).

📜 Looking deeper

After the dhyāna, editions print a set of five offerings tied to the five elements, and then the guru-verse, “gurur brahmā gurur viṣṇuḥ…”, bowing to the teacher as Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Maheśvara and the supreme Brahman. We give the guru-verse’s meaning here and leave the offerings out. Then, with “hariḥ oṃ”, the thousand names begin.

Her crown shines with the crescent moon, as Śiva’s does. The hymn often gives her his marks, the third eye and the moon, to show they are one.

ŚLOKA 1 · DEEP DIVE

THE MOTHER, THE QUEEN

🔎 In context

The thousand names begin with three titles that sum up the whole hymn: Mother, Queen, Empress on the throne. The Sahasranāma was composed by the eight Vāgdevīs at Lalitā’s command, Hayagrīva tells Agastya in the Lalitopākhyāna, and these opening names set its tone: love first, then sovereignty.

The notes draw on Bhāskararāya’s Saubhāgyabhāskara (1728), the classic commentary, in R. Ananthakrishna Sastry’s 1925 translation.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāskararāya refuses to give Śrīmātā a single meaning. Śrī can be Lakṣmī or Sarasvatī, which makes her the mother of the goddesses of wealth and of speech. Śrī can be the Veda, which makes her the one who taught the Vedas to Brahmā, as the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (6.18) says of the Lord. And mā can mean “measure”, which makes her the immeasurable. He grounds the word “mother” itself in the Taittirīya Upaniṣad’s definition of Brahman: that from which beings are born (3.2).

The lion throne gets three readings: the lion she rode against Mahiṣa (Devī Purāṇa); the five thrones the Jñānārṇava builds from eight throne-mantras; and a letter game, siṃha read backwards as hiṃsā, so she is the one who destroys harm. On her birth from the fire he is careful: consciousness is a fire that burns up the world, and she abides in it rather than being born from it. He quotes the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa to the same effect: the eternal Goddess is called “born” only when she appears to do the gods’ work.

📜 Looking deeper

Names 4 and 5 tell the Lalitopākhyāna’s story in a line. The gods, oppressed by Bhaṇḍa, offered themselves in a great fire-sacrifice, and Lalitā rose from the fire to do their work. Commentators read cidagni, the fire of consciousness, as pure awareness: she rises from the fire in which all limitations are burned. Our Lalitopākhyāna guides tell the whole story.

🌱 For today

Before she is a queen, she is a mother.

ŚLOKA 2 · DEEP DIVE

THE NOOSE AND THE GOAD

🔎 In context

Her four weapons are now explained, two in this śloka and two in the next. The noose is rāga, attachment or desire; the goad is krodha, anger. They are not weapons against the devotee but powers she holds and rules.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāskararāya reads the red of a thousand rising suns as her vimarśa, the self-aware side of consciousness, citing the Svacchanda and Vāmakeśvara Tantras. He then sets out her three forms, gross, subtle and supreme, which the next names describe: the weapons are held (gross), they are mantras (subtle), and they are powers (supreme).

The Yoginīhṛdaya (1.53) gives the key he uses: the noose is icchā-śakti, the power of will; the goad is jñāna-śakti, the power of knowing; the bow and arrows are kriyā-śakti, the power of action. He cites the Tantrarāja and the Pārvacatuḥśatikā for the goad as both wrath and knowledge.

📜 Looking deeper

The reading is beautiful in its simplicity. Desire binds like a noose; anger drives like a goad. In her hands, the noose draws the devotee towards her and the goad drives away what blocks the way. The same reading appears in the Bhāvanopaniṣad for the four weapons around the Śrīcakra’s central triangle.

So the four weapons map onto the three powers: noose = will, goad = knowledge, bow and arrows = action. Holding them, she holds the three powers by which anything is ever done.

🌱 For today

What binds and what drives you can both be placed in her hands.

ŚLOKA 3 · DEEP DIVE

THE BOW AND THE ARROWS

🔎 In context

The bow is the mind, made of sweet sugarcane; the arrows are the five tanmātras, the subtle elements of sound, touch, form, taste and smell. With mind and senses as her weapons she draws every creature towards herself.

📖 What the commentary adds

The five arrows are the tanmātras: sound, touch, form, taste and smell. Bhāskararāya notes that the Kālikā Purāṇa, the Jñānārṇava and the Tantrarāja give each arrow a name (“exciting”, “maddening” and so on), and the Kādimata names the five flowers.

Name 12, the universe drowned in her rosy light, is not only poetry. Bhāskararāya identifies it with the morning contemplation in the Yoginīhṛdaya (3.48 onwards): at dawn the practitioner sees everything suffused with her red light, and so sees the world as her own body.

📜 Looking deeper

Name 12 is one of the longest and most visual in the hymn: the whole universe drowning in a flood of red light from her body. Red is her colour throughout, the colour of compassion in action. The Saundaryalaharī (verse 18) offers almost the same meditation: sky and earth plunged in her redness.

Name 12 is thus not only poetry: it is a meditation instruction. At dawn one imagines everything bathed in her colour, and so sees the world as her body.

Mind and senses are not enemies; in her hands they are a bow and arrows.

🔎 In context

From here to śloka 21 the names describe her form from head to foot (keśādi-pāda), as the Saundaryalaharī does in its second half. It begins, naturally, with her hair and her crown.

📖 What the commentary adds

The four flowers are all fragrant and loved in temple worship. Commentators, following the same idea as the Saundaryalaharī (verse 43), say the flowers come to her hair to gain fragrance, not to give it. Kuruvinda is a kind of ruby: her crown is red, like everything about her.

FOREHEAD AND TILAKA

🔎 In context

The half-moon of the eighth lunar day, Aṣṭamī, has a perfectly straight edge and a curved one, like a forehead. Her face is the full moon, and the dark musk tilaka on it is the moon’s own mark.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāskararāya keeps these two names brief; they are images. Musk (kastūrī) tilaka was a classical cosmetic and is still offered in Devī worship.

Aṣṭamī is a weighty day in the Goddess’s calendar: Durgāṣṭamī of Navarātri falls on it. The hymn quietly marks the midpoint of the waxing moon on her brow.

📜 Looking deeper

The eighth day of the moon is Tvaritā’s day among the Nityās, and the full moon is Citrā’s. The Sahasranāma does not make that link, but readers of our Nityā guides will enjoy it: even her face holds the phases of the moon.

Aṣṭamī matters in Śrīvidyā: in the Tithi Nityā sequence the eighth day belongs to Tvaritā, and Navarātri’s Durgāṣṭamī falls on it. The forehead image quietly marks the midpoint of the moon’s waxing.

BROWS AND EYES

🔎 In context

Indian homes hang a toraṇa, a decorative arch, over the doorway on festive days. Her face is Kāma’s house, and her brows are its festive arch. Her eyes are fish swimming in the stream that flows from the beauty of her face.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāskararāya gives names 33 and 34 no separate commentary. Within the tradition the Goddess is the universal Mother, and her breasts stand for the nourishment of all beings.

Classical Indian aesthetics treats such descriptions as śṛṅgāra rasa offered to the divine. The commentators read past the surface to the image of the mother who feeds the world.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators read name 33 inwardly as the exchange between Śiva and Śakti in which neither is greater: love given and received. The Saundaryalaharī has the same creeper image in verse 78. We render these verses plainly, in the devotional spirit in which they are recited.

Classical Indian aesthetics treats such descriptions as śṛṅgāra rasa offered to the divine, not as private desire. The commentators move past these names quickly.

🌱 For today

Love is an exchange in which both give everything.

ŚLOKA 15 · DEEP DIVE

HER WAIST

🔎 In context

Her waist is so slender it cannot be seen, only inferred: since the line of hair must rest on something, there must be a waist there. Commentators enjoy the logic, which mimics a philosopher’s inference.

📖 What the commentary adds

No separate commentary. The joke is philosophical: anumāna, inference, is the means of knowledge Indian logic illustrates with fire known from smoke. The waist is known from the line of hair resting on it.

The three folds (vali-traya) look like a belt tied to keep that waist from breaking. Even in a beauty passage the hymn uses the vocabulary of the schools.

📜 Looking deeper

The three folds, trivalī, are a belt that keeps the waist from breaking. The Saundaryalaharī (verse 80) tells the same idea as a story: Kāma tied three creepers round her waist to keep it safe.

Inference (anumāna) is one of the means of knowledge in Indian logic, with the classic example of fire inferred from smoke. The poet borrows the logician’s term with a smile.

Some things are known by what they support.

HER DRESS AND GIRDLE

🔎 In context

Aruṇāruṇa, “red of reds”: her garment is the deepest red, dyed with kusumbha, safflower. Around her hips is a girdle hung with jewelled bells that chime as she moves.

📖 What the commentary adds

No separate commentary on either name. Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius) was traded across Asia for its dye; its red fades easily, so a “bright” safflower silk is a small marvel in itself.

Bhāskararāya’s silence over names 31–48 is itself telling: the tradition reads the body names as meditation imagery, and reserves commentary for doctrine.

📜 Looking deeper

The repeated redness of the hymn is deliberate. Lalitā is the red Goddess, and the Sahasranāma keeps returning to it: her body, her dress, her flowers, her light. Commentators link red to rajas, the quality of creative activity, and to compassion moving outward.

Safflower dye was traded across Asia for centuries; its red is fugitive, so the hymn’s “ever-bright” red silk is itself a small marvel.

Wear your colours with joy.

THIGHS AND KNEES

🔎 In context

Name 39 is discreet: the beauty of her thighs is known only to her husband. The hymn stays modest here, as Sanskrit devotional poetry usually does. Her knees are round and red like ruby crowns.

📖 What the commentary adds

No separate commentary on names 39 and 40. The reticence is deliberate: the hymn says only that Kāmeśa knows, leaving the union of Śiva and Śakti beyond description.

Bhāskararāya passes over the body names 31–48 almost without comment; his interest is in the names that carry doctrine. The silence itself tells us how the tradition reads them.

ŚLOKA 18 · DEEP DIVE

SHINS, ANKLES, FEET

🔎 In context

Her shins are Kāma’s quivers, and the red indragopa insects that appear in the rains are the jewels round them. Her ankles are gūḍha, hidden, a mark of beauty in Indian description. Her insteps are arched, more lovely than a tortoise’s shell.

📖 What the commentary adds

No separate commentary on names 41–43. Indragopa, “Indra’s cowherd”, is Trombidium, the scarlet mite of the rains; poets used it for tiny points of red.

The arch of her foot outdoes a tortoise’s back, a stock image of a beautiful instep, and a quiet echo of Kūrma, who bore the world on his back.

📜 Looking deeper

The Saundaryalaharī uses both images. Verse 83 makes her shins quivers for Kāma, and verse 88 protests that her foot is far too soft to be compared to a tortoise shell. The poets argue, the Goddess remains.

The indragopa, “Indra’s cowherd”, is a real creature (Trombidium), bright scarlet, emerging with the rains; Sanskrit poets used it for tiny points of red.

🌱 For today

Even the foot of the Goddess is praised: no part of her is ordinary.

ŚLOKA 19 · DEEP DIVE

THE LIGHT OF HER FEET

🔎 In context

A devotee bows and touches her feet; the light from her toenails falls on him and drives away his tamas, the quality of darkness and dullness. Bowing at her feet is itself the cure.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāskararāya tells a story from the Matsya and Padma Purāṇas. Nārada, reading the marks of the infant Pārvatī for her parents, says her feet “err by their own shadow”. He explains the riddle: the shadow is the colour of the jewelled crowns of gods and demons bowed at her feet, which drive off the light of her nails.

In the same reading Nārada says no husband is yet born for her: a riddle resolved when Śiva, the unborn, becomes her husband. Meditation on her feet, says Bhāskararāya, removes the darkness of ignorance.

📜 Looking deeper

Her feet outshine the lotus, the usual comparison for feet. The Saundaryalaharī (verse 87) explains why point by point: the lotus dies in snow, sleeps at night and only holds Lakṣmī, while her feet do the opposite.

Nārada’s reading of Pārvatī’s marks also says no husband is yet born for her: a riddle resolved when Śiva, the unborn, becomes her husband. Bhāskararāya likes such riddling stories.

🌱 For today

Bow, and let the light fall on you.

The description reaches her feet, with anklets that chime. Her gait is slow and graceful, like a swan’s. Then a summary: she is the treasure-house, the store, of great beauty.

The commentary gives names 46–48 no separate remarks. The head-to-foot meditation ends with the sound of her anklets and the swan’s walk.

Mahālāvaṇyaśevadhi, “treasure-house of great beauty”, sums up names 13–47: the body has been a treasury, and each part a jewel to dwell on in meditation.

ŚLOKA 21 · DEEP DIVE

ON HER LORD’S LAP

🔎 In context

The description of her form, which began at śloka 4 with her hair, ends here with a summary: red all over, flawless, fully adorned, and seated on the lap of Kāmeśvara. The Śrīcakra’s bindu is this scene.

Name 51’s forty ornaments are a real ritual list: the Kalpasūtra of Paraśurāma prescribes them for full worship, and temples dress the Goddess accordingly on festival days.

📖 What the commentary adds

On name 51 he cites the Kālikā Purāṇa’s list of forty ornaments, from crest-jewel to toe-ring, and more from Paraśurāma’s Kalpasūtra; temples dress the Goddess by such lists on festival days. On name 52 he argues that Kāma means the Lord who desires to create (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.17) and prajñāna, knowledge (Aitareya Upaniṣad).

Śivā (53) comes from the root meaning “to desire”: she is Paramaśiva’s will; the Devī Purāṇa makes Śivā liberation itself. Svādhīnavallabhā (54): though she is his will, she is never subject to him. He cites two Devī Bhāgavata stories: Sukanyā winning back her husband, and Śacī recovering Indra, both by the Goddess’s grace.

📜 Looking deeper

Name 54 is striking: svādhīnavallabhā, she whose beloved is in her control. In classical poetics the svādhīnapatikā is the heroine whose lover is devoted to her. Commentators read it theologically: Śiva acts only through Śakti. Śivā, name 53, is both “the auspicious one” and “Śiva’s own”. Part 2 moves from her form to her city.

Bhāskararāya refers in name 53 to his own commentary on the Śiva Aṣṭottaraśata, one of his many works. His learning ran across Śaiva and Śākta texts alike.

🌱 For today

Love that is mutual needs no force.
