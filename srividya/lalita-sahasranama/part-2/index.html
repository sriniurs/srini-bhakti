The thousand names · her city, her war, her mantra

HYMNS OF THE GODDESS

HOW THIS WORKS: All the ślokas in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: the śloka, a picture, its meaning and every name in the word table; then a deep dive.

WHERE WE ARE

Part 2 covers ślokas 22–40, names 55–111. It moves from Lalitā’s form to her city, Śrīpura on Mount Meru (55–63), then tells the whole war with Bhaṇḍa of the Lalitopākhyāna in twenty names (64–84), names the three parts of the root mantra as her body (85–98), and describes her rising as kuṇḍalinī through the centres of the body (99–111).

Almost every name here links to another guide on this site: the Lalitopākhyāna, the deities Sampatkarī, Aśvārūḍhā, Rājaśyāmalā, Vārāhī, Bālā and Mahāgaṇapati, the Nityās, and the Saundaryalaharī.

Names 85–91 refer to the three kūṭas of the root mantra of Śrīvidyā. We give their names and what the hymn says of them, and do not give the mantra itself.

ŚLOKA 22 · DEEP DIVE

HER CITY

🔎 In context

From her form the hymn turns to her home. Śrīpura, the city of the Goddess, stands on the central peak of Mount Meru, ring within ring. At its heart is the Cintāmaṇi palace, the palace of wish-fulfilling gems. The Śrīcakra is a map of this city.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāskararāya counts Meru’s middle peak as the fourth, citing Durvāsas’s Lalitāstavaratna: three peaks for Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva, and a central one, 400 yojanas high, for her. The Vidyāratna commentary gives her city 25 walls; the Rudrayāmala places another city on Ratnadvīpa in the nectar ocean, its 25 walls standing for the 25 tattvas.

Her house is of cintāmaṇi, the wish-granting gem; one commentary on Gauḍapāda’s sūtras calls it the source of all wish-granting mantras. Her couch, in the Bahurūpāṣṭaka and Bhairavayāmala Tantras, has Brahmā, Hari, Rudra and Īśāna as legs and Sadāśiva as plank; Indra, they add, holds her spittoon.

📜 Looking deeper

Her throne is made of the five Brahmās: Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra and Īśvara as its four legs and Sadāśiva as its seat. The Saundaryalaharī (verse 92) describes the same couch. The five gods who perform the five acts of creation are, here, her furniture.

Bhāskararāya keeps three levels for every place: the Purāṇic geography, the Tantric yantra, and the body. City, house and couch are Meru, the Śrīcakra’s enclosures, and the centres of the subtle body all at once.

Every journey inward has a home at its centre.

ZAPPY: Three oceans of nectar?

PROF. BOLT: Bhāskararāya finds one in heaven, one in the moon at the crown of the head, and one in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad: two lakes in Brahmā’s world.

Kāmākṣī, giver of desires.

ŚLOKA 23 · DEEP DIVE

THE GROVE AND THE OCEAN

🔎 In context

More of her home: the great lotus forest, the kadamba grove, the island in the ocean of nectar. The Saundaryalaharī (verse 8) builds the same picture: Maṇidvīpa, the island of gems.

📖 What the commentary adds

The lotus forest, says Bhāskararāya, is beyond the worlds (Rudrayāmala), on Meru (Lalitāstavaratna), and within: the Svacchanda Tantra calls the thousand-petalled lotus at the crown padmāṭavī. The Bhairavayāmala gives the order inward: nectar ocean, the nine triangles, kadamba trees, a gem gallery, the palace.

For the nectar ocean he cites the Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa (1.6.2), the moon at the centre of the crown lotus, and the two lakes of Chāndogya 8.5.3 discussed in the Brahma Sūtras. Kāmākṣī: “whose eyes are Kāma (Śiva)”, or whose two eyes are Ka and Ma, Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī; the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa says Brahmā gave her the name.

📜 Looking deeper

Kāmākṣī, name 62, is also the Goddess of Kāñcīpuram, the great Śrīvidyā temple where, in the Lalitopākhyāna, Agastya heard this very hymn. Readers in South India will hear the name of a living temple here. Kāñcī has its own guide in the temples section of this series.

The Chāndogya’s two lakes in Brahmā’s world are a striking Upaniṣadic image of the afterlife. Bhāskararāya finds the Goddess’s nectar ocean already there in the Veda.

Home can be a place of nectar.

ŚLOKA 24 · DEEP DIVE

THE ARMY OF ŚAKTIS

🔎 In context

Now the hymn tells the war of the Lalitopākhyāna in a sequence of names, 64 to 84. Bhaṇḍa, the demon born from Kāma’s ashes, oppresses the gods; Lalitā goes to war with an army of goddesses. Our six-part Lalitopākhyāna series tells the whole story.

From name 64 Bhāskararāya runs two readings side by side: the Purāṇic war, and a reading through the Śiva Sūtras and Kṣemarāja’s commentary, in which every event of the war is a stage in the soul’s recognition of itself.

📖 What the commentary adds

From name 64 Bhāskararāya runs two readings side by side: the Purāṇic war, and an inner reading through the Śiva Sūtras and Kṣemarāja’s commentary, in which every event of the war is a step in the soul recognising itself. The praise of gods and sages is, inwardly, the Self being recognised (Śiva Sūtras 1.1–2).

Bhaṇḍa is the bound soul; his army is the host of dualistic ideas. He lists the Agni Purāṇa’s classes of gods and the Rudrayāmala’s guardians who praise her, and recalls Nārada going to her for help.

📜 Looking deeper

The Sahasranāma compresses chapters of the Purāṇa into a few long names. Reciting them is retelling the story. Each name in this sequence is like a scene: the army, the elephants, the cavalry, the chariots, the fire wall, the battles.

The Śiva Sūtras are a short Kashmir Śaiva text attributed to Vasugupta (9th century). Bhāskararāya’s use of them shows how closely Śrīvidyā and Kashmir Śaivism were woven together.

🌱 For today

Even the gentlest power can take up arms when needed.

ŚLOKA 25 · DEEP DIVE

ELEPHANTS AND HORSES

🔎 In context

Two of her generals: Sampatkarī leads the elephants, Aśvārūḍhā the cavalry. In the Lalitopākhyāna, Sampatkarī rises from Lalitā’s goad and Aśvārūḍhā from her noose. Both have guides in our deities series.

📖 What the commentary adds

Sampatkarī is named in the Svatantra Tantra; the Lalitopākhyāna has her spring from the goad and ride the elephant Raṇakolāhala. Aśvārūḍhā springs from the noose and rides Aparājitā (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa), and has a mantra of her own.

The Kādimata reads elephants and horses as the senses; the rider is the one who controls them. Bhāskararāya sets this against Śiva Sūtras 1.12–14, which speak of wonder as the first step of yoga.

📜 Looking deeper

An Indian army of the epics had four arms: elephants, horses, chariots and infantry. The hymn lists them in turn. The elephants and horses come first; the chariots follow in the next śloka.

The two goddesses spring from the goad and the noose: knowledge and will, by the scheme of name 8–9. Her army is literally made from her own powers.

🌱 For today

A great task needs many hands, each with its own strength.

ŚLOKA 26 · DEEP DIVE

THE THREE CHARIOTS

🔎 In context

Lalitā rides Cakrarāja, “king of wheels”, a chariot in nine tiers like the Śrīcakra itself. Mantriṇī, her minister, also called Rājaśyāmalā, rides Geyacakra, the “wheel of song”. The next śloka gives the third chariot.

📖 What the commentary adds

Cakrarāja, nine-storeyed and ten yojanas high, is the Śrīcakra (Lalitopākhyāna). Inwardly Bhāskararāya ties it to the three means to realisation, āṇava, śākta and śāmbhava, citing the Yoga Sūtras, Gītā 4.33 and Śiva Sūtra 1.22.

Geyacakra is read as the sun’s chariot (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1) or the Śrīcakra again; its riders are yogis who gain mantra-power (Śiva Sūtra 1.23). Mantriṇī is Śyāmalā.

📜 Looking deeper

The chariot Cakrarāja is the Śrīcakra in motion: the yantra becomes a war-chariot. Our Lalitopākhyāna Part 3 describes all three chariots tier by tier, and Rājaśyāmalā has her own guide.

Three chariots for three commanders: the Śrīcakra for Lalitā, the Geyacakra for her minister, the Kiricakra for her general. Each is also a yantra worshipped in the tradition.

🌱 For today

Wise counsel rides beside power.

ŚLOKA 27 · DEEP DIVE

THE COMMANDER AND THE FIRE WALL

🔎 In context

Daṇḍanāthā, the commander, is Vārāhī, the boar-faced goddess, who rides Kiricakra at the front of the army. Then the fire rampart: after the night attack, Lalitā has Jvālāmālinī, the fourteenth Nityā, raise a wall of fire round the camp.

📖 What the commentary adds

Kiri is the boar, or the rays of creation; her wheel is the cycle of creation, preservation and dissolution, through which the yogi keeps his self-awareness (Śiva Sūtra 3.33). Jvālāmālinī built the wall of fire at Lalitā’s order (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa).

Inwardly the knower stands in the fire of consciousness as seer, and among its sparks as creator; Bhāskararāya cites the Spanda Kārikā and Śiva Sūtras 3.26 and 3.31. As a yantra, the fire is the five Śakti and four Śiva triangles with the bindu at the centre (Yoginīhṛdaya 1.8).

📜 Looking deeper

Our Jvālāmālinī guide tells the scene from chapter 26 of the Lalitopākhyāna: a ring of flame a hundred yojanas round, with a single opening. Vārāhī has her own guide in the deities series. These two names appear together in the hymn as they do in the story.

Bhāskararāya also gives a yantra reading of the fire wall: the five Śakti and four Śiva triangles of the Śrīcakra with the bindu at the centre (Yoginīhṛdaya 1.8).

🌱 For today

Strong protection lets the work go on safely inside.

ŚLOKA 28 · DEEP DIVE

DELIGHTING IN HER ŚAKTIS

🔎 In context

Lalitā watches her goddesses fight and is pleased. Name 73 remembers the Nityās’ night battle: when Bhaṇḍa’s brother attacked by night, the fifteen Nityās fought under Kāmeśvarī, and Lalitā watched them with eager pride.

📖 What the commentary adds

He quotes a Śakti Sūtra: one’s own powers, unrecognised, bewilder and bind one in saṃsāra. Bhaṇḍa’s army is that bewilderment; the śaktis, goddesses like Nakulī, are the same powers recognised.

The Nityās defeated generals such as Damanaka and Citragupta. He notes that their mantras differ between the Jñānārṇava and the Tantrarāja, and quotes the Yogavāsiṣṭha: skills unused perish, but the seed of wisdom, once risen, never dies.

📜 Looking deeper

Our Tithi Nityā series tells that battle Nityā by Nityā. The Sahasranāma keeps it in a single name. It is a rare picture of the Goddess as a proud leader, delighting in her companions’ courage.

Bhāskararāya notes that the Nityās’ mantras differ between the Jñānārṇava and the Tantrarāja. He records variant traditions rather than pretending there is one.

🌱 For today

Take pride in the courage of those who work with you.

🔎 In context

📖 What the commentary adds

Matsya came from the right thumb, Kalki from the left little finger; they matched demons such as Somaka, Bali and Hiraṇyākṣa. In another reading the ten forms are the soul’s five states and Īśvara’s five acts.

Bhāskararāya distinguishes the Mahāpāśupata missile of Sadāśiva from the six-syllabled Pāśupata of Īśvara, citing the Liṅga Purāṇa on the name Paśupati. Inwardly the fire is non-dual awareness growing with practice.

📜 Looking deeper

For a reader who knows the avatāras from our Daśāvatāra guide, this is a remarkable claim: in the Śākta view, Viṣṇu’s ten forms come from the Goddess’s fingertips. Vaiṣṇava tradition tells their origin differently; the Lalitopākhyāna tells it this way.

Matching each revived demon with the avatāra who first killed it, Somaka with Matsya, Hiraṇyākṣa with Varāha, gives the Lalitopākhyāna its logic: the Goddess commands every past victory again.

📖 What the commentary adds

Kāma’s revival marks the turn of the hymn from story to doctrine. Desire, burned by asceticism, is restored by the Goddess in a purified form: love that serves creation.

ŚLOKA 37 · DEEP DIVE

KULA AND SAMAYA

🔎 In context

Two great approaches to Śrīvidyā are named: Kaula, which includes outer ritual, and Samaya, which stresses inner worship in the heart. The Goddess belongs to both. She is in the kula, of it, its yoginī, and also akulā, beyond it.

📖 What the commentary adds

Kulāṅganā: the Kulārṇava contrasts ordinary learning, public like a courtesan, with this vidyā, guarded like a lady of good family; Paraśurāma’s Kalpasūtra (1.30) says the same. Kaula, says Bhāskararāya, is the union of kula and akula, Śakti and Śiva; the Svacchanda calls the lotus at the base of suṣumnā akula.

Samaya is the worship of the cakra in the heart’s space, which yogis hold supreme; it also names five Saṃhitās (Vasiṣṭha, Śuka, Sanaka, Sanandana, Sanatkumāra), and the fivefold equality of Śiva and Devī in place, function, action, name and form. He describes samaya worship moving through the centres to union with Sadāśiva at the crown.

📜 Looking deeper

Lakṣmīdhara, the famous commentator on the Saundaryalaharī, followed the Samaya way and favoured purely inner worship. Bhāskararāya, the Sahasranāma’s great commentator, followed a Kaula line. The hymn keeps both, and so does this series: we describe them without choosing.

The fivefold equality of Śiva and Śakti is the hallmark of the samaya school, set out by Lakṣmīdhara in his commentary on the Saundaryalaharī. Bhāskararāya includes it without taking sides.

The sahasrāra sits at the lower part of the brahmarandhra, the crown aperture. The nectar comes from the moon in the pericarp of the crown lotus; he cites Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 3.12.3 on “feet” that make nectar flow.

📜 Looking deeper

The Saundaryalaharī’s verse 10 describes the nectar flowing from her feet as she returns. Both hymns end the ascent the same way: not in withdrawal from the world, but in nectar flowing back into it.

Ājñā as the seat of the guru’s command is still taught in yoga traditions: the place between the brows is where the teacher’s instruction is held in meditation.

🌱 For today

What rises to the top comes back as nourishment.

ŚLOKA 40 · DEEP DIVE

KUṆḌALINĪ

🔎 In context

The sequence ends by naming her: Kuṇḍalinī, the coiled one, the great Power, thin as a lotus fibre and bright as lightning, dwelling above all six centres. The Saundaryalaharī (verse 21) uses the same lightning image.

📖 What the commentary adds

He cites the Yogavāsiṣṭha’s story of Queen Cūḍālā, who recognised this power in herself and taught her husband. “Bright as lightning” and “fine as a lotus fibre” come from the Mahānārāyaṇa (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 10.13), which describes the flame in the heart.

He reads the Aruṇa Upaniṣad (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1.27) as rousing kuṇḍalinī, and maps a girl, a marriageable young woman and a faithful wife onto her stages: risen from the root, ripened at the heart, united with Kāmeśvara at the crown. Then nectar fills the 72,000 channels (Śuka Saṃhitā).

📜 Looking deeper

This completes Part 2: her city, her war and her victory, the mantra that is her body, and her rising within the body. Part 3 turns to devotion: Bhavānī, who is reached by meditation, and the long sequence of names describing her beyond all qualities.

Bhāskararāya maps a girl, a marriageable young woman and a faithful wife onto kuṇḍalinī’s stages: newly risen from the root, ripened at the heart, and united with Kāmeśvara at the crown. The 72,000 channels are then filled with nectar (Śuka Saṃhitā).

The greatest power can be the finest thread.
