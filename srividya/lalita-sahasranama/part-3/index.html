LALITĀ SAHASRANĀMA

The thousand names · devotion, and the Goddess beyond qualities

HYMNS OF THE GODDESS

HOW THIS WORKS: All the ślokas in one go for chanting, then every śloka on two pages: the śloka, a picture, its meaning and every name in the word table; then a deep dive.

WHERE WE ARE

Part 3 covers ślokas 41–60, names 112–248. It turns from the war and the mantra to devotion: Bhavānī, reached by meditation and won by love (112–131).

Then comes the famous nirguṇa sequence, names 132 to about 195: the Goddess described by what she is not, without form, without qualities, without ego, beyond death, often in pairs, “without passion, destroyer of passion”. After it, the hymn turns round and calls her everything (sarva-) and great in everything (mahā-), ending with Pārvatī at home in the Śrīcakra.

The movement from saguṇa (with qualities) to nirguṇa (beyond qualities) and back is one of the deepest features of the Sahasranāma. The same Goddess is both.

ZAPPY: Bhavānī means “Śiva’s wife”, doesn’t it?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and more. Bhāskararāya says bhava can mean Śiva, latent impressions, or Kāma; she gives life to all three. The Padma Purāṇa makes her the Goddess of Thanesar.

ŚLOKA 41 · DEEP DIVE

BHAVĀNĪ

🔎 In context

With the war and the inner ascent behind it, the hymn turns to devotion. The next stretch of names, from here to about name 131, describes her as the one reached by bhakti and by meditation, and as the giver of every good to her devotees.

📖 What the commentary adds

Bhāvanāgamyā gets a careful note: meditation is on words or on their meanings; the Kūrma Purāṇa gives three kinds (Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Akṣarā), and the Yoginīhṛdaya three stages: with form, with and without form, and formless.

On name 117 he cites the Agni Purāṇa’s definition of bhaga, fortune, and the Padma Purāṇa’s eight auspicious items (saubhāgyāṣṭaka) given to devotees. Bhadra in 115 can also name a noble class of elephant.

📜 Looking deeper

Bhavānī is a name heard across India, and especially beloved in Maharashtra and Karnataka. Bhāvanāgamyā, “reached through meditation”, uses the word bhāvanā, which also gives the Bhāvanopaniṣad its name: the meditation in which the Śrīcakra is seen as one’s own body.

Sthāṇeśvara (Thanesar, near Kurukshetra) is an ancient Śaiva centre. The Padma Purāṇa’s list of 108 seats of the Goddess, each with its own name of her, is one source for how names like Bhavānī became local deities.

🌱 For today

The forest of worries can be cleared, one stroke at a time.

ŚLOKA 42 · DEEP DIVE

WON BY DEVOTION

🔎 In context

Three names in a row say that devotion is the way to her: she loves it, she is reached by it, she is won over by it. The Goddess who ruled armies in Part 2 can be won by love.

📖 What the commentary adds

Primary devotion is longing for the Lord; secondary is service. He cites the Śāṇḍilya Bhakti Sūtra (1.2), Kaṭha Upaniṣad 4.1, Yoga Sūtra 1.23 and Gītā 11.54, “by devotion alone can I be known”. For bhayāpahā he quotes Taittirīya 2.9: knowing the bliss of Brahman, one fears nothing; the Vāyu Purāṇa advises repeating her names in danger.

Śāmbhavī is a mudrā (Haṭhayoga Pradīpikā 4.36), an initiation (Kalpasūtra 1.32), and, in the Devī Bhāgavata, the name of a girl of eight worshipped as the Goddess. Śarvāṇī: Śarva is Śiva’s earth form, whose wife is Sukeśī and son Mars (Liṅga and Vāyu Purāṇas).

📜 Looking deeper

Name 123 has two readings. Śāradā is autumn, the season of the great Navarātri; it is also a name of Sarasvatī. So she is “worshipped in autumn” or “worshipped by Sarasvatī”. Both are true to the tradition. Śṛṅgeri’s Śāradā has a guide later in this series.

The eight forms of Śiva in the Purāṇas each have a wife and son: Śarva (earth) with Sukeśī and Mars, Bhava (water) with Uṣā and Śukra. Bhāskararāya uses this scheme to explain names 112 and 124.

Love is the simplest key.

ŚLOKA 43 · DEEP DIVE

ŚAṄKARĪ, BEYOND SUPPORT

🔎 In context

The śloka starts with gentle names, Śāṅkarī, prosperity-giver, moon-faced, and then turns: nirādhārā, without support; nirañjanā, stainless. From here, for nine ślokas, almost every name begins with nir- or niṣ-, “without”.

📖 What the commentary adds

Śāṅkarī: the Kālikā Purāṇa says she accompanies Śambhu at the start of every cycle as Dakṣa’s daughter, as māyā and as yoga-sleep. Śrīkarī is the consort of Śrīkara, a name of Viṣṇu in his own Sahasranāma. Śātodarī can be read śatodarī, “of a hundred caves”: the Himālaya, whose daughter she is.

Nirādhārā: she needs no support; he cites the Sūta Saṃhitā on worship with a support (an image) and without (pure inner worship). Nirañjanā: free of the three impurities; he explains the three classes of bound souls, referring to his own Setubandha, and cites Śvetāśvatara 6.19.

📜 Looking deeper

This is the famous nirguṇa section of the Sahasranāma: the Goddess described by what she is not. Commentators say it moves from her saguṇa form, with qualities, to her nirguṇa nature, beyond qualities. The two are not opposed: the same Goddess is both.

The three classes of bound souls come from Śaiva Siddhānta and Kashmir Śaivism: those with only the impurity of limitation, those with that and karma, and those with all three. Names 133–135 remove them one by one.

🌱 For today

What needs no support is the support of everything.

PROF. BOLT: Bhāskararāya reads them as answers to rival schools. “Eternal” answers the Buddhists, “formless” the Vijñānavādins, “qualityless” the logicians, “all-pervading” the Jainas.

ŚLOKA 44 · DEEP DIVE

WITHOUT QUALITIES

🔎 In context

Ten names, almost all negative: no taint, no stain, no form, no disturbance, no qualities, no parts, no desire, no destruction. Among them two positive ones: nityā, eternal, and śāntā, peaceful.

📖 What the commentary adds

Nirlepā: free of the stain of karma (Gītā 4.14 and 5.10). Nirmalā: free of avidyā. Nityā refutes the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness (he cites Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.5.14). Nirākārā refutes the Vijñānavāda view that all has form in consciousness. Nirākulā rejects nihilism.

Nirguṇā answers the Nyāya view that the Self has qualities (Śvetāśvatara 6.11). Niṣkalā reconciles Brahma Sūtra 2.3.43 with Gītā 15.7. Śāntā, all-pervading, answers the Jaina self limited to the body. Nirupaplavā, split as nir-upa-plava, is the nectar flooding the 72,000 channels (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1).

📜 Looking deeper

This is the language of the Upaniṣads applied to the Goddess. Readers of our Bhagavad Gītā chapters will recognise it: the Self is described the same way. The Sahasranāma says the Goddess of the red form and the four weapons is also this formless reality.

🌱 For today

Some truths are best approached by saying what they are not.

ŚLOKA 45 · DEEP DIVE

EVER FREE

🔎 In context

The nirguṇa names continue. Nityamuktā, nityaśuddhā, nityabuddhā: ever free, ever pure, ever awake. These three are a well-known Vedāntic description of the Self, used here of the Goddess.

📖 What the commentary adds

Nirvikārā: the vikāras are Sāṅkhya’s 23 evolutes from mahat down (he cites the Sāṅkhya Kārikā). Niṣprapañcā: beyond the world, the Fourth of Māṇḍūkya 7. Nirāśrayā refutes the Cārvāka view that consciousness depends on the body.

Nityabuddhā: knowledge cannot be separated from the knower (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.30). He records a curious alternative, two Jaina tīrthaṅkaras called Śuddha and Buddha made immortal by her. Niravadyā: Avadya is also a hell (Kūrma and Liṅga Purāṇas) from which she saves. Nirantarā: Taittirīya 2.7, whoever makes the least separation has fear.

📜 Looking deeper

Nirantarā is lovely: without antara, without an interval or gap. There is no place where she is not, and no moment when she is absent. It answers the question a devotee might ask after the long list of negatives: where is she? Everywhere, without a break.

Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.30 says the seer’s seeing is never lost, because it is imperishable; there is nothing other than it to see. Bhāskararāya uses it to show that knowing is her nature, not an act.

🌱 For today

Freedom is not something to gain but something to recognise.

ŚLOKA 46 · DEEP DIVE

WITHOUT PASSION, DESTROYING PASSION

🔎 In context

Now the names fall into pairs: without passion, and destroyer of passion; without pride, and destroyer of pride. She is free of a fault, and she frees her devotees of it too. The pattern runs for four ślokas.

📖 What the commentary adds

Niṣkāraṇā: the cause of all, herself uncaused (Śvetāśvatara 6.9). Niṣkalaṅkā: Īśa Upaniṣad 8, “bright, bodiless, unscarred”. Nirupādhi: free of limiting adjuncts, the red flower and the crystal. Nirīśvarā: both Mīmāṃsā and Sāṅkhya, which deny a separate creator, belong to her.

Nīrāgā: he cites the Śāṇḍilya Sūtra on renunciation overcoming the six inner enemies. Rāgamathanī: Yoga Sūtra 2.3 on the five afflictions. From here the names run in pairs: without X, and destroyer of X in others.

📜 Looking deeper

Name 155, nirīśvarā, “with no lord above her”, is a strong Śākta statement: there is no higher god than the Goddess. Name 152, niṣkāraṇā, without cause, places her before all causes: she causes everything and nothing causes her.

The crystal and the red flower (sphaṭika and japā) is one of Vedānta’s best-known images: the crystal seems red but is not. Bhāskararāya uses it to explain how the one Goddess appears as many.

🌱 For today

What you are free of, you can help others be free of.

ŚLOKA 47 · DEEP DIVE

WITHOUT EGO, DESTROYING DELUSION

🔎 In context

Ahaṅkāra is the sense of “I”; mamatā is the sense of “mine”. These two together, says Indian philosophy, bind the soul. The Goddess has neither, and destroys both in her devotees.

📖 What the commentary adds

Nirahaṅkārā: he names Sāṅkhya’s three kinds of ego, vaikārika, taijasa and bhūtādi. Mohanāśinī: she ends delusion by giving the sense of unity (Īśa 7). Nirmamā: “mine” needs separateness, which she lacks.

Pāpanāśinī: she destroys sin through her mantra; he cites Chāndogya 5.24.3, as a reed’s tuft burns in fire, so the knower’s sins burn, and the Padma Purāṇa on Kātyāyanī.

📜 Looking deeper

The Bhagavad Gītā praises the person who is nirmamo nirahaṅkāraḥ, “without mine and without I”. The Sahasranāma uses the same words of the Goddess. The devotee who chants them is reminded of the goal as well as the Goddess.

The cintā–citā proverb is a Sanskrit classic quoted in many forms. Bhāskararāya puts it next to a name, giving devotees something to remember when worry starts.

🌱 For today

Loosen “I” and “mine”, and much worry goes with them.

Silver in mother-of-pearl, like the rope and the snake, is a standard Vedānta example of error: something seen that was never there. Bhāskararāya uses it for the world’s hold on the mind.

ŚLOKA 58 · DEEP DIVE

SIXTY-FOUR

🔎 In context

Sixty-four is a sacred number in Śrīvidyā. Full worship offers sixty-four services (upacāras). Indian tradition counts sixty-four arts (kalās), from music and dance to cooking and archery. And sixty-four crores of yoginīs serve her.

📖 What the commentary adds

Sixty-four offerings: Paraśurāma’s Kalpasūtra (4.5) lists them; other Tantras add eight more. He refers to his own Varivasyārahasya. Sixty-four arts: lists differ (Śārṅgadhara and others).

Sixty-four crore yoginīs: Brāhmī and the seven Mothers each have eight śaktis, making 64; the Tantrarāja distributes 64 crore yoginīs across the nine cakras of the Śrīcakra.

📜 Looking deeper

The Saundaryalaharī (verse 31) speaks of sixty-four tantras. The repetition of the number across texts is deliberate: sixty-four is eight times eight, a number of completeness.

Gambling and alchemy among the 64 arts surprise readers. The traditional lists include every skill of a cultured life; the Goddess is all of them.

🌱 For today

Every art can be an offering.

ŚLOKA 59 · DEEP DIVE

THE VIDYĀ OF MANU AND THE MOON

🔎 In context

Tradition names twelve great worshippers of Lalitā, each with a form of her vidyā; Manu and Candra, the moon, are two of them, along with Lopāmudrā, Agastya, Kāma and others. Our Agastya and Lopāmudrā guide names all twelve.

📖 What the commentary adds

Candramaṇḍalamadhyagā: kuṇḍalinī breaks through the moon in the crown lotus; the moon’s disc is also the Śrīcakra. Cāruhāsā: the moon is her smile, a state known only from a guru.

Cārucandrakalādharā: or the princess Candrakalā of the Devī Bhāgavata (Book 3), who saw the exiled prince Sudarśana in a dream and chose him at her svayaṃvara, under the Goddess’s protection.

📜 Looking deeper

She dwells in the middle of the moon’s disc: the moon is one of her homes, as the Tithi Nityā series shows. The three cāru- names, lovely form, lovely smile, lovely crescent, are a small song of beauty.

The Devī Bhāgavata story is of Prince Sudarśana, driven to the forest, whom the Goddess protects; Princess Candrakalā sees him in a dream and chooses him at her svayaṃvara.

🌱 For today

Many people have found her by many paths.

ŚLOKA 60 · DEEP DIVE

PĀRVATĪ, RULER OF ALL

🔎 In context

Part 3 ends with her as ruler of all things, at home in the Śrīcakra, and as Pārvatī, the daughter of the Himalaya, wife of Śiva. The grand and the familiar meet in one śloka.

📖 What the commentary adds

Cakrarājaniketanā: the nine cakras of the Śrīcakra are her only dwelling. Pārvatī: daughter of Himavat.

Padmarāgasamaprabhā: ruby-bright; he cites a description of kuṇḍalinī as saffron-coloured, linking the name to the nectar-kuṇḍalinī.

📜 Looking deeper

Name 245 says the Śrīcakra is her house. Our Śrīcakra and Khaḍgamālā series walk through it room by room. Part 4 continues with the five Brahmās of her throne and the states of consciousness.

Trailokyamohana, “enchanter of the three worlds”, is the outermost square of the Śrīcakra, where our Khaḍgamālā and Āvaraṇa guides begin.

The ruler of all is also someone’s daughter.
