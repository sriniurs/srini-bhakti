KĀMA BURNED, BHAṆḌA BORN

How a demon rose from the ash of love

LALITOPĀKHYĀNA · PART 1 OF 6

FOREWORD

Every story of Lalitā begins with a fire. The god of love shoots his arrows at Śiva, and Śiva’s third eye burns him to ash. That much is a famous tale. The Lalitopākhyāna adds what nobody else tells: what happened to the ashes.

This is Part 1 of a six-part retelling, following the text chapter by chapter. It tells how an artist among Śiva’s attendants shaped a man from the ash, how he won Śiva’s boons, and how he came to be called Bhaṇḍa.

ZAPPY: So the villain is made out of the god of love?

PROF. BOLT: Out of his ashes. That's the twist that drives the whole story.

The Lalitopākhyāna, the “story of Lalitā”, is the last part of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa and the narrative heart of Śrīvidyā. Its war, its city and its heroines stand behind every name in the Sahasranāma and every deity in the guides of this series. This six-part retelling follows the text in order, checked against G. V. Tagare’s published translation.

Part 1 is the prologue to Lalitā’s appearance. It begins with a familiar story, the burning of Kāma, and gives it a dark sequel that only this Purāṇa tells: the creation of the demon Bhaṇḍa from Kāma’s ash.

Like the rest of the series, the retelling leaves out the text’s chapters on mantra, initiation and ritual procedure. Everything else, the story, its characters and its meaning, is told in full.

ZAPPY: Why does it start with a question?

PROF. BOLT: Because Agastya asks it. The whole Lalitopākhyāna is an answer to a sage's worried question about suffering.

The Lalitopākhyāna is framed as a dialogue. Agastya, visiting Kāñcī, worships Śiva and Kāmākṣī and asks how beings can be freed from suffering. Hayagrīva, sent by Viṣṇu, teaches him that the worship of Tripurā is the way, and then tells her story.

Before reaching Bhaṇḍa, Hayagrīva covers a good deal: the Goddess’s manifestations, Indra’s curse over a sage’s garland, Bṛhaspati’s teaching on sin and atonement, the churning of the ocean and Viṣṇu’s form as Mohinī. These chapters are rich but are not part of Lalitā’s own story, so our retelling begins with chapter 11.

Agastya’s question at the start of chapter 11 sets the plan for everything that follows: how Bhaṇḍa was born, how the Goddess appeared, and how she destroyed him. Our six parts answer in the same order.

TĀRAKA AND THE GODS

Why Kāma was sent

Brahmā’s adviceSummon Kāma to awaken Śiva’s love
Kāma’s weaponsA sugarcane bow and flower arrows

ZAPPY: Wait, isn't this the Kumārasambhava story?

PROF. BOLT: The same story Kālidāsa told. The Lalitopākhyāna takes it and adds a dark sequel: what happens to the ashes.

The Tāraka story is one of the best known in Indian literature. The asura Tāraka has won a boon that only a son of Śiva can kill him, knowing that Śiva, after Satī’s death, has withdrawn from the world. The gods need Śiva to marry again, and Pārvatī, Satī reborn, is waiting.

Brahmā advises the gods to send Kāma. The Lalitopākhyāna adds a note on Kāma’s weapons: his flower arrows and sugarcane bow came to him through a divine boon, the same weapons that Lalitā will later hold in her own hands.

Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava tells this part of the story with great beauty. The Lalitopākhyāna tells it briefly, because its real interest lies in what happens next.

PārvatīRejected, she goes to the forest to do penance

ZAPPY: Poor Kāma. He was only doing what the gods asked.

PROF. BOLT: He was. And he'll get his life back, at the very end. But first, his ashes cause trouble.

Kāma goes to the Himālaya, where Śiva sits in deep meditation and Pārvatī serves him with flowers. He looses his arrows, and for a moment Śiva’s mind turns toward Gaurī. Then Śiva sees Kāma, understands what has been done, and opens his third eye. Kāma is burned to ash.

Pārvatī, feeling rejected, goes to the forest with her parents’ blessing to perform penance, and in the familiar story she eventually wins Śiva by austerity rather than by Kāma’s arrows. The Lalitopākhyāna mentions her penance and moves on.

Kāma’s widow Rati grieves; her grief will matter at the end of the story, when it moves Lalitā to bring Kāma back.

ZAPPY: A painter made the demon?

PROF. BOLT: An artist playing with ashes, and Śiva's glance brought the sculpture to life. Nobody meant harm. That's what makes it a tragedy.

Citrakarmā is a lord of Śiva’s gaṇas, the troops of strange and powerful beings who attend him. His name means “one whose work is pictures”, an artist. Seeing the heap of ash, he shapes it into a man of wonderful form, the way a sculptor shapes clay.

Then Rudra glances at the figure, and it comes alive, blazing like the midday sun. Citrakarmā embraces it and tells it to praise Mahādeva. No one in the scene intends any harm: a curious artist, an absent-minded glance, a newly living being told to worship.

This innocence is important. The Purāṇa does not make Bhaṇḍa the product of evil intent, but of carelessness around something powerful: the remains of desire, handled lightly.

THE ŚATARUDRĪYA AND THE BOONS

How Bhaṇḍa won his power
His worshipCitrakarmā teaches him the Śatarudrīya, the great Vedic hymn to Rudra
His devotionHe bows to Rudra a hundred times as he recites it
Boon 1Half of every opponent’s strength joins his own
Boon 2His arrows make enemy weapons useless
Boon 3Rule over a kingdom for sixty thousand years
AlsoŚiva gives him divine weapons, then vanishes

ZAPPY: Śiva gave a demon all that?

PROF. BOLT: He gave a devotee what he asked. The Purāṇas do this often: honest devotion gets real boons, even when the devotee later goes wrong.

Citrakarmā teaches the new being the Śatarudrīya, the great Vedic hymn to Rudra, still chanted in Śiva temples today. The ash-born man recites it, bowing to Rudra a hundred times, and Śiva, pleased by his devotion, offers him a boon.

He asks for two things: that half the strength of any opponent should pass to him, and that his arrows should make the enemy’s weapons useless. Śiva grants both, adds rule over a kingdom for sixty thousand years, gives him divine weapons, and vanishes.

These boons explain why the gods cannot defeat Bhaṇḍa: every fight weakens them and strengthens him. Only a power beyond the gods, the Goddess herself, will be able to face him.

The Śatarudrīya is not a minor detail. It is one of the most sacred hymns of the Yajurveda, the hymn of the hundred names of Rudra, still chanted at Śiva temples across India and on this site as the Rudram. That the demon learns it first is a pointed touch: Bhaṇḍa begins as a sincere worshipper of the same god whose eye made him possible.

THE NAME BHAṆḌA

“Bhaṇḍa, bhaṇḍa!”
The cryBrahmā, seeing all this, exclaims “bhaṇḍa, bhaṇḍa”
The name sticksThe being becomes known as Bhaṇḍa, or Bhaṇḍāsura
MeaningDisputed: the translator notes a root meaning “to be fortunate”; others read it as a cry of dismay
Popular readingMany devotional retellings take it as “shameless” or “wretched”
Our stanceWe give the cry and leave its meaning open

ZAPPY: So does ‘Bhaṇḍa’ mean lucky or awful?

PROF. BOLT: The translator wonders if it was a blessing; popular retellings hear dismay. The word sits on the line, like the being himself: blessed by Śiva, destined for disaster.

Brahmā, the creator, sees all this happen and cries out, “bhaṇḍa, bhaṇḍa”. The being is known from then on as Bhaṇḍa, and with the suffix for demons, Bhaṇḍāsura.

What the cry means is not clear. The translator notes that the root may mean “to be fortunate”, which would make it a blessing. Many devotional retellings read it instead as “shameful” or “wretched”, an exclamation of dismay at what had been created. The text itself does not explain.

We leave the meaning open. Either reading fits: a being blessed by Śiva, and a being whose existence is a disaster waiting to happen.

ŚŪNYAKA

The empty city
Next chapterBhaṇḍa needs a capital
ŚukraThe asuras’ teacher urges it
MayaThe asura architect builds the city of Śūnyaka
CoronationBhaṇḍa is crowned king of the asuras
The nameŚūnyaka: “the empty one”, “the void”
LaterLalitā will reduce it to ashes in Part 6

ZAPPY: A capital called ‘empty’?

PROF. BOLT: A city built on ash, named emptiness. The storyteller is telling you how it'll end before it begins.

In the next chapter, Śukra, the teacher of the asuras, urges that Bhaṇḍa should have a capital worthy of a king. Maya, the great asura architect, builds him the city of Śūnyaka, and Bhaṇḍa is crowned. He gathers the asuras around him and begins to oppress the gods.

Śūnyaka means “the empty one”. A city built for a king made of ash bears the name of emptiness. When the war ends in Part 6, Lalitā’s final weapon will burn Bhaṇḍa and Śūnyaka to nothing, and the name will be fulfilled.

The architect Maya is a familiar figure: he also builds the Pāṇḍavas’ hall in the Mahābhārata. Later in the Lalitopākhyāna, he and Viśvakarmā together will build Lalitā’s own city, Śrīpura.

The text places Śūnyaka on the shore of the great ocean near the mountain Mahendra, a city a hundred yojanas across. When Lalitā’s army later marches, the Purāṇa describes the city’s walls cracking and meteors falling over it, omens of the emptiness it was named for.

ZAPPY: Where did the brothers come from?

PROF. BOLT: Most retellings say from Bhaṇḍa himself. The chapter we're following doesn't say. So we flag it.

Bhaṇḍa rules with two brothers, Viśukra and Viṣaṅga. In widespread tradition they arise from Bhaṇḍa’s own body, from his right and left sides. The chapter we are following does not tell their birth, so we give the tradition with that caution.

They play large parts in the war. Viśukra sets the obstacle-yantra that paralyses Lalitā’s army; Viṣaṅga leads a treacherous night attack. Both are finally killed by Lalitā’s two officers, Vārāhī and Śyāmalā, on the third day.

Commentators who read the story inwardly sometimes take the three brothers as layers of ignorance, with Bhaṇḍa as the root ego and his brothers as its offshoots. That reading is theirs, not the Purāṇa’s, but it runs through much later writing.

Many devotional retellings add that Viśukra and Viṣaṅga were as devoted to Śiva as Bhaṇḍa was, and as gifted. Whatever their origin, the three act together as a single power with three heads, and the war is designed to take them down one layer at a time.

ZAPPY: So the lesson is don't burn your feelings?

PROF. BOLT: Don't just burn them. Something grows from the ashes. The rest of the story shows the better way: the Lady of Desire.

Part 1 sets up a puzzle that the rest of the story solves. Desire was a problem, so it was burned. But burning it did not end it; it produced something worse, a being of enormous power and no wisdom. The Lalitopākhyāna is, among other things, a meditation on what to do with desire.

Its answer is the Goddess. Lalitā will hold Kāma’s own bow and arrows, take Śiva as Kāmeśvara, the lord of desire, and at the end bring Kāma back to life. Desire is not destroyed but brought home, held by the right hands.

The story also warns about devotion without wisdom. Bhaṇḍa’s worship of Śiva is real and his boons are real. What he does with them is the problem. Power from the Divine still needs a good heart to carry it.

ZAPPY: Does the Sahasranāma tell this part?

PROF. BOLT: It remembers its ending: the revival of Kāma. The burning itself it leaves to the Purāṇa.

haranetrāgnisaṃdagdhakāmasaṃjīvanauṣadhiḥ

She who is the life-giving herb for Kāma, who had been burned by the fire of Hara’s eye. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

The Lalitā Sahasranāma assumes its listener knows the Lalitopākhyāna. Its war section, which our later parts will follow name by name, mentions Bhaṇḍa’s sons, his brothers, his city and his end.

The events of Part 1 are recalled mostly through their resolution. A name near the end of the war section praises the Goddess as the one who restored Kāma, who had been burned by the fire of Śiva’s eye. The burning and the revival frame the whole story.

Readers who chant the Sahasranāma will find that knowing this part changes how those names sound.

Other Sahasranāma names mention Bhaṇḍa directly only in the war section, where his sons, his brothers and his city are each given a line. Our Parts 4 to 6 will quote them in order.
