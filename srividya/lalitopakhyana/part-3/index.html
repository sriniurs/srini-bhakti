ŚRĪPURA, THE CITY OF THE GODDESS

A tour from the iron wall to the throne

LALITOPĀKHYĀNA · PART 3 OF 6

FOREWORD

On the central peak of Mount Meru stands a city of concentric walls: black iron, bronze, copper, silver, gold, then topaz, ruby, diamond, sapphire, pearl. Gardens of heavenly trees bloom between them. At the centre is a palace of wish-jewels.

This is Śrīpura, Lalitā’s city, described in chapters 31–37. Part 3 walks you through it, wall by wall, and shows why devotees read it as the Śrīcakra.

ZAPPY: A city built by the gods' architect AND the demons' architect?

PROF. BOLT: Both. Viśvakarmā and Maya. When the Goddess builds, everyone works for her.

The Lalitopākhyāna devotes seven chapters to describing Lalitā’s city, Śrīpura, in loving and almost architectural detail: walls, gates, distances, gardens, guardians and palaces. It is one of the most elaborate descriptions of a divine city in Indian literature.

This part gives a guided tour, from the outermost wall of black iron to the Cintāmaṇi palace at the centre, using the text’s own order. It also shows how nine names of the Lalitā Sahasranāma trace the same route, and how devotees read the city as the Śrīcakra.

Readers who want to follow the war without interruption can skip to Part 4 and come back; this part stands on its own.

ZAPPY: So we're skipping ahead?

PROF. BOLT: A little. Think of it as a tour of the capital before the campaign. Part 4 picks up the war exactly where Part 2 left it.

In the Lalitopākhyāna, the full description of Śrīpura comes after the victory over Bhaṇḍa. Once peace is restored, the gods decide to build a permanent palace for the divine couple, and Viśvakarmā and Maya set to work. Earlier, at the wedding, Viśvakarmā had built a first city, but it is this later one that the text describes at length.

This series follows the plan laid out at its start, which places Śrīpura as Part 3, between the wedding and the war. Seeing the city first makes the army, the chariots and the Śrīcakra deities easier to follow in the battle chapters.

Nothing in the story depends on the order. Śrīpura is, in the tradition’s view, eternal; the chapters describe rather than create it.

The order also matches how many devotees meet the story. They learn the Sahasranāma first, and its early names walk through Śrīpura long before its war names begin. Hearing about the city before the battle follows the hymn’s own sequence.

ON THE PEAK OF MERU

The city’s place
Mount MeruThe axis of the world in Indian cosmology
The peakŚrīpura stands on Meru’s great central peak
SizeThe text gives the peak four hundred yojanas in height and breadth
PurposeThe gods build a palace for the divine couple’s eternal joy and every purpose
Sahasranāma“Seated on the central peak of Sumeru”
Other tellingsSome place her city in an ocean of nectar: the jewel island, Maṇidvīpa

ZAPPY: Why on top of Meru?

PROF. BOLT: Because Meru is the centre of everything. Her city sits on the axis of the universe.

Mount Meru stands at the centre of the world in Indian cosmology, the axis around which the continents and heavens are arranged. Placing the Goddess’s city on its central peak puts her at the very centre of reality.

The text gives the peak a size of four hundred yojanas in height and breadth. A yojana is a large ancient measure of distance; the numbers are meant to overwhelm rather than to be measured.

Other descriptions place her home elsewhere. The Devī Bhāgavata describes Maṇidvīpa, an island in an ocean of nectar, and the Sahasranāma includes a name calling her the one in the midst of the ocean of nectar. A heavenly city can have more than one address.

VIŚVAKARMĀ AND MAYA

Two architects, one city
ViśvakarmāThe craftsman of the gods, builder of their cities and weapons
MayaThe craftsman of the asuras, who built Bhaṇḍa’s city and the Pāṇḍavas’ hall
TogetherAt the command of the gods, they build Śrīpura side by side
MeaningThe Goddess’s city gathers every skill, divine and demonic
EchoIn Part 1 Maya built Bhaṇḍa’s capital; now he builds hers

ZAPPY: Maya built the enemy's city too!

PROF. BOLT: And now he builds the Goddess's. Even the demons' architect ends up working for her. Nothing stays outside her kingdom.

Viśvakarmā is the gods’ master craftsman, maker of their weapons, chariots and cities. Maya is the asuras’ master craftsman, builder of the Tripura cities and of the great hall of the Pāṇḍavas in the Mahābhārata. They are usually on opposite sides.

In building Śrīpura they work together. The text presents this simply as the gods’ command, but readers have long noticed the meaning: the Goddess’s city gathers every kind of skill, from both sides of the cosmic divide.

Maya’s presence closes a circle from Part 1, where he rebuilt Bhaṇḍa’s capital. The architect of the empty city now builds the city of fullness.

There is a practical touch too. The text has the gods decide to build a palace “for perpetual enjoyment and every purpose”: a home, not just a fortress. Two architects working together suggest the scale of the work, and the text’s precise measurements suggest the pride of craftsmen describing their masterpiece.

THE WALLS OF METAL

From black iron to gold
1 · Black ironThe outermost wall, sixteen thousand yojanas around, four gates, four yojanas high
2 · BronzeOne yojana within
3 · CopperSeven yojanas within
4–6 · Lead, brass, five metalsThe next rings inward
7 · SilverSeven yojanas within
8 · GoldSeven yojanas within
GatesEach wall has gateways and towers (gopuras)

ZAPPY: Why start with iron and end with gold?

PROF. BOLT: Each wall is more precious than the last. Walking inward, you walk from the hardest, darkest metal toward the brightest.

The outermost wall is of kālāyasa, black iron, sixteen thousand yojanas around, with four gates and a height of four yojanas. Then, ring within ring, come walls of bronze, copper, lead, brass, an alloy of five metals, silver and gold, with intervals of a yojana or seven yojanas between them.

The sequence moves from the dullest and hardest metal to the brightest and most precious. A pilgrim walking inward, in imagination, passes through the whole range of the earth’s metals, each a little finer than the last.

Each wall has gates and gopuras, the tall gate-towers familiar from South Indian temples. The city is, in a sense, a vast temple, and southern temple architecture with its concentric prākāras echoes the same idea.

MandāraThe coral tree of heaven
PārijātaThe fragrant tree Kṛṣṇa brought to earth

SeasonsThe trees are “pervaded by many seasons”: all seasons at once

ZAPPY: Gardens between walls?

PROF. BOLT: The whole city is layered: wall, garden, wall, garden. You'd walk through every heavenly tree on your way in.

Between the walls lie gardens of the trees of heaven: the kalpaka or wish-fulfilling tree, the santāna, the haricandana or heavenly sandal, the mandāra, the pārijāta and the kadamba. The text says they are pervaded by many seasons, so that every season’s flowers bloom together.

These trees appear throughout Indian myth as treasures of Indra’s heaven. In Śrīpura they are only the gardens between walls, a sign of how far the Goddess’s city surpasses even heaven.

The kadamba grove has a special place: Śyāmalā’s palace stands there, and the Sahasranāma calls Lalitā herself a dweller in the kadamba forest.

ZAPPY: Walls made of rubies and diamonds?

PROF. BOLT: Seven rings of gems inside the eight of metal. Every precious thing in the world is a wall of her city.

Inside the metal walls come walls of gems. Chapter 33 begins with topaz and continues through ruby, gomeda (hessonite), diamond, lapis lazuli, sapphire and pearl. Other gems, including emerald and coral, appear in the innermost rings in the following chapters.

Each gem wall has its own interval, its own gates and its own residents, described in detail by the text. Many are devotees of the Goddess; some are deities who appear again in the Śrīcakra.

For devotees who visualise the city, the gem walls are a meditation in colour: yellow, red, golden-brown, white, blue, deep blue, pearl-white, ring after ring of light.

THE OFFICERS’ QUARTERS

Where the court lives
ŚyāmalāHer palace stands by the Cintāmaṇi palace, in the grove of kadamba trees
DaṇḍanāthāHer emerald chamber is close to Lalitā’s palace
The armyBarracks in the Mahāpadmāṭavī, the great forest of lotuses
Daṇḍanāthā’s homeAlso in the lotus forest, with her troops
PatternCounsel and command live closest to the queen

ZAPPY: Even the army has quarters in a lotus forest?

PROF. BOLT: Barracks among lotuses. It's that kind of city.

The officers of Lalitā’s court live close to her. Śyāmalā, the minister, has her palace beside the Cintāmaṇi palace in the kadamba grove. Daṇḍanāthā, the general, has an emerald chamber near Lalitā’s palace, and a permanent home in the great lotus forest where the army’s barracks stand.

The arrangement mirrors their roles. Counsel lives beside the throne; the army lives a little further out, guarding the approach. The minister and the general are both close, but the minister closest.

Readers of the Deities guides on Vārāhī and Śyāmalā will recognise these quarters.

Other attendants have their places too. The text assigns quarters and guardians throughout the walls, and many of the beings who live there reappear as deities of the Śrīcakra’s enclosures. Śrīpura is not only a palace but a whole society arranged around its queen.

THE CINTĀMAṆI PALACE

The house of wish-jewels
MaterialBuilt entirely of cintāmaṇi, the wish-fulfilling gem
HeightWalls four yojanas high; upper storeys rising much higher
RoofsTall coverings of cintāmaṇi slabs, rising in peaks
LightJewelled lamps with the splendour of ten million suns
Inner chambersRooms associated with Vaśinī and the other goddesses of speech (ch. 37)
Sahasranāma“Dwelling within the Cintāmaṇi palace”

ZAPPY: A palace made of wish-granting jewels?

PROF. BOLT: Every stone grants wishes. You'd hardly need to ask for anything inside. Which is rather the point.

At the heart of the city stands the Cintāmaṇi-gṛha, a palace made entirely of cintāmaṇi, the mythical gem that grants every wish. Its walls are four yojanas high, its upper storeys rise much higher, and its roofs of cintāmaṇi slabs rise in peaks. Jewelled lamps with the splendour of ten million suns light the interior.

Chapter 37 describes the inner apartments, associated with Vaśinī and the other goddesses of speech, the same goddesses who composed the Lalitā Sahasranāma at Lalitā’s command.

A palace of wish-jewels is a lovely paradox: in a house where every stone grants wishes, there is nothing left to wish for. The Goddess’s home is fullness itself.

Indian poets often describe the cintāmaṇi as the gem that Viṣṇu wears or that the ocean yields. To build an entire palace of it is the poet’s way of saying that here the rarest treasure of all is ordinary building material.

THE HEART OF THE CITY

The Śrīcakra and the couch
At the centreThe Śrīcakra, the Goddess’s yantra
The seatThe couch whose legs are Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra and Īśvara, and whose plank is Sadāśiva
The coupleLalitā seated on Kāmeśvara’s left thigh
The courtLakṣmī and Sarasvatī with fly-whisks; the goddesses of speech
ReadingThe city is a Śrīcakra laid out in space; the Śrīcakra is the city drawn small

ZAPPY: So the city IS the Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: That's how devotees read it. Walk inward through Śrīpura, or trace the Śrīcakra from its square to its dot: same journey.

Within the palace, the tradition places the Śrīcakra, and upon it the couch of five Brahmas, with Lalitā seated on Kāmeśvara’s left thigh. The scene is the one described in the Foundations guide on Lalitā and painted in countless Tanjore and Mysore pictures.

Devotees read the whole city as the Śrīcakra spread out in space: the outer walls as the square enclosure, the groves and gem walls as the circles and petals, the palace as the inner triangles, the couch as the central point. The Śrīcakra, in turn, is the city drawn small.

The Śrīcakra section of this series follows that reading enclosure by enclosure.

The scene at the centre is also where the story’s threads meet. Kāmeśvara, who burned Kāma in Part 1, is seated here as the lord of desire. The weapons given at the wedding in Part 2 are in the Goddess’s hands. The officers who will lead the war in Part 4 live just outside.

ZAPPY: The Sahasranāma gives directions to her house?

PROF. BOLT: Nine names in a row, and you've walked from the mountain peak to her throne.

sumerumadhyaśṛṅgasthā śrīmannagaranāyikā
cintāmaṇigṛhāntasthā pañcabrahmāsanasthitā
mahāpadmāṭavīsaṃsthā kadambavanavāsinī
sudhāsāgaramadhyasthā kāmākṣī kāmadāyinī

Seated on the central peak of Sumeru, lady of the glorious city, dwelling within the Cintāmaṇi palace, seated on the five-Brahma couch, abiding in the great lotus forest, dweller in the kadamba grove, in the midst of the ocean of nectar, Kāmākṣī, giver of all desires. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

Nine consecutive names of the Lalitā Sahasranāma trace the route of this chapter: the peak of Sumeru, the glorious city, the Cintāmaṇi palace, the five-Brahma couch, the great lotus forest, the kadamba grove, the ocean of nectar, and then Kāmākṣī, giver of desires.

Chanting them, devotees make the journey in a breath. Bhāskararāya’s commentary links each name to the Purāṇic description and adds inner readings, for example taking the lotus forest as the thousand-petalled lotus at the crown of the head.

The last two names, Kāmākṣī and Kāmadāyinī, bring the tour home to Kāñcī, where Agastya first heard the story.

ŚRĪPURA AND MAṆIDVĪPA

Two descriptions of the Goddess’s home
Maṇidvīpa“The jewel island”, the Goddess’s home in the Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa
SettingAn island in an ocean of nectar, not a mountain peak
SharedConcentric walls of metal and gem, gardens between, the Cintāmaṇi palace at the centre
DifferencesNumbers and order of walls differ; Maṇidvīpa’s goddess is Bhuvaneśvarī
Sahasranāma“In the midst of the ocean of nectar” suggests the island picture too
Our stanceTwo Purāṇic visions of one idea; neither is “correct”

ZAPPY: Is it on a mountain or an island?

PROF. BOLT: The Lalitopākhyāna says Meru; the Devī Bhāgavata says an island in nectar. The Sahasranāma names both. A heavenly city doesn't need one address.

The Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa describes the Goddess’s home as Maṇidvīpa, an island in the ocean of nectar, ringed by walls of metals and gems and gardens, with the Cintāmaṇi palace at its centre. Its goddess is Bhuvaneśvarī rather than Lalitā, and its details differ.

The two descriptions share so much that many devotees treat them as one vision told twice. Others keep them apart, as the homes of different forms of the Goddess. Both readings are traditional.

The Sahasranāma name “in the midst of the ocean of nectar” suggests that the island picture was known to its composers too.

Some modern temples build models or murals of Maṇidvīpa or Śrīpura, and Navarātri decorations in some South Indian homes recreate the concentric walls in miniature. The image of a jewelled city of rings has proved irresistible to artists.

WHAT IT MEANS

The city within
OrderHer kingdom is ordered, beautiful, layered: the opposite of Bhaṇḍa’s empty city
Inward journeyFrom iron to gold, from gold to gems, from gems to wish-jewels, to her
Every giftEvery metal, gem and tree of the world is part of her home
MapThe city as the Śrīcakra; the Śrīcakra as the body
BhāvanopaniṣadReads the yantra as the human body: the city is within
Inner readingThe journey to the Goddess is a journey inward, layer by layer

ZAPPY: So I'm supposed to imagine walking through all those walls?

PROF. BOLT: Many devotees do exactly that in meditation. And the old texts say the city is inside you anyway.

Śrīpura is the opposite of Śūnyaka, Bhaṇḍa’s “empty” city. Where his capital was built on ash and named for emptiness, hers is built of every precious thing and ordered with perfect care. The contrast is the story’s argument in architectural form.

For devotees, the city is above all an invitation inward. Each wall is more precious than the last; each step brings the traveller closer to the Goddess. Meditation on Śrīpura is a way of rehearsing that journey.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad completes the picture by reading the Śrīcakra, and so the city, as the human body and mind. In that reading, the walls are layers of our own being, and the Goddess on the couch is the deepest Self.

There is also a simple devotional reading. Most people cannot visualise sixteen thousand yojanas of black iron, but anyone can imagine walking through a series of doors toward someone they love. That is what Śrīpura asks of the reader: to keep walking inward.
