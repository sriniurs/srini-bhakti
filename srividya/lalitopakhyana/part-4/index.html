THE WAR: THE FIRST DAY

Chariots, serpents, darkness and the moon

LALITOPĀKHYĀNA · PART 4 OF 6

FOREWORD

The armies march: Lalitā on her nine-tiered chariot Cakrarāja, her minister and general on theirs. In Bhaṇḍa’s city the walls crack. Then, one after another, his generals ride out, and one after another the Goddess’s Śaktis answer.

Part 4 follows chapters 17 to 25: the first day of the war, ending with a treacherous night attack and the fifteen goddesses of the moon who drive it off.

ZAPPY: A whole war in one day?

PROF. BOLT: Day one of four. And it's packed.

The war against Bhaṇḍa lasts four days in the Lalitopākhyāna, and the first is the longest and busiest. Lalitā’s armies march, her three great chariots are described, Bhaṇḍa’s city panics, and then wave after wave of asura generals meet the Goddess’s Śaktis and fall. At night, the enemy breaks the rules of war and is driven off by the goddesses of the moon.

This part follows chapters 17 to 25, checked against the published translation. It connects with the Deities guides for Sampatkarī, Aśvārūḍhā and Tiraskariṇī, and introduces the fifteen Nityās, who have their own guides later in the series.

The war is told as war. Commentators’ inner readings are given separately, and labelled.

ZAPPY: Twenty-five names, sung on the march?

PROF. BOLT: A short hymn in the middle of a battle march. The Śaktis praise their queen as they ride. That's how this army goes to war.

After the wedding and the birth of the army, the march begins. Chapter 17 describes Mantriṇī Śyāmalā’s army setting out; Daṇḍanāthā’s forces march with war-drums and banners. In chapter 18, Lalitā advances on her chariot, and the Śaktis praise her with twenty-five sacred names, a hymn sung on the move.

The scale of the description is deliberately overwhelming: drums made of oceans, Śaktis beyond counting, banners filling the sky. The Goddess’s army is not a band of heroes but the whole of divine power on the march.

The Sahasranāma marks the moment with names describing Lalitā accompanied by the army of Śaktis intent on slaying Bhaṇḍa, and praised by gods and sages.

ZAPPY: A chariot with nine floors?

PROF. BOLT: Nine tiers, like the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra. Lalitā rides the whole yantra into battle.

cakrarājarathārūḍhasarvāyudhapariṣkṛtā
geyacakrarathārūḍhamantriṇīparisevitā
kiricakrarathārūḍhadaṇḍanāthāpuraskṛtā

She who rides the chariot Cakrarāja, armed with every weapon; attended by Mantriṇī on the chariot Geyacakra; honoured by Daṇḍanāthā on the chariot Kiricakra. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

Chapters 19 and 20 describe the three great chariots in detail. Lalitā’s Cakrarāja, the king of wheels, rises in nine tiers, each occupied by named goddesses. Śyāmalā’s Geyacakra and Daṇḍanāthā’s Kiricakra are described in the same way. Mantriṇī is introduced as the one through whom every petition reaches the queen.

Many commentators read the chariots as moving Śrīcakras, with their tiers matching the nine enclosures and their deities matching the yantra’s goddesses. The war, in this reading, is the Śrīcakra in action.

Three consecutive Sahasranāma names salute the chariots and their riders, quoted on this page.

PANIC IN ŚŪNYAKA

Chapter 21

The councilBhaṇḍa meets his brothers Viśukra and Viṣaṅga
Viṣaṅga’s adviceSend spies first; learn the enemy
Bhaṇḍa’s choiceIgnore caution; mobilise at once
KuṭilākṣaHis commander-in-chief, ordered to gather the army
Bhaṇḍa’s moodBoastful, sure of his boons

ZAPPY: Viṣaṅga gave good advice and Bhaṇḍa ignored it?

PROF. BOLT: Classic tyrant. He trusts his boons more than his brother's sense. Pride is already losing him the war.

As Lalitā’s army approaches, terrible omens strike Bhaṇḍa’s city, now called Śūnyaka: walls crack, meteors fall, smoke fills the streets. Bhaṇḍa calls a council with his brothers Viśukra and Viṣaṅga.

Viṣaṅga advises caution: send spies, learn the enemy’s strength. Bhaṇḍa, confident in Śiva’s boons, will not wait. He orders his commander Kuṭilākṣa to mobilise the army and boasts of easy victory over a “woman”.

The scene sets up the pattern of the war. Each time, Bhaṇḍa underestimates the Goddess and sends a commander; each time, a different Śakti answers.

Bhaṇḍa’s contempt for a female enemy is a recurring note in Goddess stories. Mahiṣa in the Devī Māhātmya makes the same mistake. In both stories, the demon’s inability to take the Goddess seriously is the first sign of his defeat.

FIRST BLOOD

SampatkarīOn her great elephant, she kills Durmada
AśvārūḍhāOn her horse, she kills Kuraṇḍa
Their originThe two goddesses born from Lalitā’s goad and noose
EffectThe asuras learn that this army is not like the gods
SahasranāmaThe elephant and horse names honour these two

ZAPPY: The first two kills go to the elephant goddess and the horse goddess?

PROF. BOLT: The weapons-turned-generals strike first. The queen's goad and noose, in person, open the war.

The first generals sent are Durmada and Kuraṇḍa. Against them ride the two goddesses born from Lalitā’s goad and noose: Sampatkarī on her mountainous elephant and the cavalry goddess on her horse. Sampatkarī kills Durmada; the cavalry goddess kills Kuraṇḍa.

It is fitting that these two open the war. They are Lalitā’s own weapons in living form, and their victories show that the queen’s power is already in the field before she lifts a hand.

The Sahasranāma honours them in two names quoted in the Deities guides: the elephant herds of Sampatkarī and the countless horses of Aśvārūḍhā.

The text describes the battles with the vividness of epic: dust clouds raised by the armies, the trumpeting of elephants, the clash of weapons. Readers who know the Mahābhārata’s battle books will recognise the style, adapted to an army of goddesses.

NakulīA goddess whose name means “mongoose”
The fightMongoose against serpents: she destroys Sarpiṇī’s māyā
The generalsShe kills the five
MercyShe spares the surviving asura soldiers

ZAPPY: A mongoose goddess against a snake sorceress?

PROF. BOLT: Perfect casting. Every Indian child knows who wins that fight.

Bhaṇḍa next sends five generals led by Karaṅka, with an enormous force. With them comes Sarpiṇī, a serpent-sorceress whose māyā fills the battlefield with snakes and drives the asuras into a frenzy.

Against her comes Nakulī, whose name means “mongoose”. As a mongoose destroys snakes, she destroys Sarpiṇī’s illusion, and then kills the five generals. The text adds that she spares the surviving asura soldiers.

Nakulī is one of many goddesses of the Lalitopākhyāna who appear only in a single episode. Her mercy to the defeated soldiers is a small detail with a large meaning: the Goddess’s army does not slaughter for its own sake.

There is a pleasing logic in how the Goddess answers each threat. A serpent-sorceress is met by a mongoose; a general who fights with numbers is met by the mightiest elephant; boastful brothers are met by blindness. The war is less a slugging match than a series of precise answers.

PROF. BOLT: Day one belongs to the Śaktis. Each one Bhaṇḍa sends, a different power answers.

Deities: Sampatkarī · Aśvārūḍhā · Tiraskariṇī

Third come the seven Balāhaka brothers, sons of Kīkasā, said to be always victorious. Tiraskariṇī rides out in her dark chariot Tamoliptā, looses the weapon of blindness and destroys them.

The Deities guide to Tiraskariṇī tells this episode in full, using chapter 24.

By the end of these three battles, the pattern is clear: Bhaṇḍa sends ever stronger forces, and each is met by a goddess whose power answers it exactly: elephant and horse against generals, mongoose against serpents, darkness against clouds.

THE NIGHT ATTACK

Chapter 25
The planBhaṇḍa orders Viṣaṅga to strike from behind while the army moves ahead
The approachBy a northern path, circling round, waiting in darkness without drums
The targetThe rear of Cakrarāja, where Lalitā rides under a pearl umbrella
The insultViṣaṅga’s arrow shatters Lalitā’s royal fan
LalitāFurious at the treachery; she permits the Nityās to fight
Rules brokenNight attacks and rear attacks break the code of war

ZAPPY: He attacked at night, from behind?

PROF. BOLT: Twice against the rules. Bhaṇḍa's side has stopped pretending to fight fair.

Chapter 25 brings a turn. Bhaṇḍa orders Viṣaṅga to strike from behind while the Goddess’s main forces press ahead. Viṣaṅga takes a northern path, circles round, and waits in darkness without drums. Then he attacks the rear of Cakrarāja, where Lalitā rides under a pearl-studded umbrella.

An arrow from Viṣaṅga shatters the Goddess’s royal fan. The insult enrages the Nityās who attend her, and Lalitā herself is furious at the treachery. She gives them leave to fight.

The night attack breaks two rules of righteous war at once: fighting after dark, and striking from behind. The asuras have stopped pretending to fight fairly.

ZAPPY: They let him go?

PROF. BOLT: ‘It is not proper to chase a warrior who flees.’ The Goddess's army keeps the rules even when the enemy breaks them. That's the difference between the sides.

nityāparākramāṭopanirīkṣaṇasamutsukā

She who is eager to watch the display of the Nityās’ valour. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

The Nityās are fifteen goddesses of the lunar days, attendants of Lalitā who rule the waxing and waning of the moon. Led by Kāmeśvarī, the first, they counter-attack. Vahnivāsinī and Jvālāmālinī, goddesses of fire, blaze up so that the dark field is lit and the enemy can be seen.

The battle lasts three watches of the night. Kāmeśvarī’s arrows split Viṣaṅga’s armour. As his generals fall and dawn approaches, he flees with his survivors. The Nityās refuse to chase him: it is not proper, they say, to pursue a warrior in flight. Lalitā, pleased, heals their wounds with a single merciful glance.

The contrast is sharp. The enemy attacked at night, from behind; the Goddess’s goddesses would not even chase a retreating foe.

ZAPPY: Fifteen moon goddesses fighting at night?

PROF. BOLT: Where else would the moon fight? Each phase of the moon, a warrior.

Chapter 25 gives the Nityās’ names, with some variant spellings. In the more familiar forms they are Kāmeśvarī, Bhagamālinī, Nityaklinnā, Bheruṇḍā, Vahnivāsinī, Mahāvajreśvarī, Śivadūtī, Tvaritā, Kulasundarī, Nityā, Nīlapatākā, Vijayā, Sarvamaṅgalā, Jvālāmālinī and Citrā. Lalitā herself is the sixteenth, Mahānityā.

Each Nityā rules one lunar day of the waxing moon, from the first after the new moon to the full moon. Their worship is one of the oldest layers of Śrīvidyā, set out in the Tantrarāja and the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava.

The Nityā guides in this series give each one her own article: her day, form, colour, weapons, place on the Śrīcakra and meaning.

It is worth noticing that the Nityās appear in the story before they appear in ritual. Many devotees first meet them as names in the Sahasranāma or in Śrīcakra worship; the Lalitopākhyāna shows them as warriors, with personalities and deeds.

ZAPPY: The generals felt guilty?

PROF. BOLT: Very. Their queen was attacked from behind while they chased glory in front. Part 5 opens with their apology.

The first day closes with two scenes. Kuṭilākṣa, Bhaṇḍa’s commander, leads ten armies against Daṇḍanāthā and is defeated by her sharp arrows; he flees, and his army is destroyed in the night. Meanwhile word reaches the officers of the treacherous attack on the rear.

Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā are dismayed. They had pushed ahead and left the Goddess’s chariot without proper defence. Chapter 26 opens with them descending from their own chariots, climbing the nine steps of Cakrarāja and prostrating before Lalitā.

Their remorse is a human touch in a cosmic war. Even the Goddess’s greatest officers make mistakes, and the story lets them own it.

WHAT IT MEANS

The first day, read inwardly
Many powersEach threat is met by a different Śakti: no one power does everything
SerpentsFears and poisons of the mind, met by the mongoose’s quickness
BlindnessPride blinded; the boastful lose sight of the field
NightAttacks come when we are unguarded; the moon’s phases stand watch
ChivalryVictory without cruelty: the Nityās let a fleeing enemy go
Inner readingCommentators map the generals to the mind’s faults, the Śaktis to its powers

ZAPPY: Is this war about real fighting or about the mind?

PROF. BOLT: Both, the commentators say. The Purāṇa tells it as war. Teachers read it as a map of the inner fight.

Read as a story, the first day is a gallery of goddesses, each with a moment of glory. Read inwardly, as many teachers read it, it shows the mind’s powers meeting its faults one by one: steadiness and swiftness against the first rush of trouble, quickness against fear, clarity against pride.

The night attack has a pointed inner meaning. Trouble often comes when we are unguarded, from behind, in the dark, and the remedy in the story is light: the fire goddesses illuminate the field so the enemy can be seen.

And the Nityās’ refusal to chase a fleeing enemy is a lesson in itself. Strength that keeps its own rules, even when its opponent does not, is the kind the Goddess honours.

Many teachers also stress the officers’ mistake at the end of the day. Even the best forces of the mind, pressing forward eagerly, can leave the heart unguarded. The cure in the story is humility: the officers return, admit their fault, and make the rear secure.
