THE WAR: BĀLĀ AND GAṆEŚA

A child, a laugh, and a wall of fire

LALITOPĀKHYĀNA · PART 5 OF 6

FOREWORD

After the treachery of the first night, the camp is ringed with a wall of fire. Then Bhaṇḍa sends his thirty sons, and Lalitā’s nine-year-old daughter Bālā asks to fight them.

Part 5 follows chapters 26 to 28: Bālā’s victory, Viśukra’s stone of gloom, Gaṇeśa born from Lalitā’s laughter, the thirst weapon, the ocean that revives the army, and the fall of Bhaṇḍa’s brothers.

ZAPPY: This is the part with the girl and the elephant god?

PROF. BOLT: And a wall of fire, a magic stone, and an ocean. Busy two days.

Part 4 ended with the Goddess’s officers dismayed: in their eagerness they had left her chariot unguarded, and the enemy had struck from behind. Part 5 covers the next two days of the war, chapters 26 to 28, in which the camp is secured, a child wins the day, a god is born from laughter, and Bhaṇḍa’s two brothers fall.

Several of the best-loved scenes of the whole Lalitopākhyāna are here: Bālā on her swan-drawn palanquin, Gaṇeśa grinding the stone of obstacles to powder, and the ocean that pours over the parched army.

This part also meets one of the text’s famous puzzles: the Purāṇa and the Sahasranāma disagree about which officer killed which brother. We give both versions.

THE OFFICERS’ APOLOGY

Morning of the second day
Their reportThe night attack on the queen’s chariot
Their confession“Shallow-minded that we were, we had gone very far ahead”
Their faultCakrarāja had no proper guard
Their gestureThey climb the nine steps of her chariot and prostrate
LalitāShe hears them and orders a defence for the camp
ToneNo anger: she fixes the problem

ZAPPY: The goddess's generals said sorry?

PROF. BOLT: Openly. The best officers own their mistakes. And the best queens fix the problem instead of punishing.

At dawn on the second day, Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā come before Lalitā. They climb down from their own chariots, ascend the nine steps of Cakrarāja and prostrate. They report the night attack and blame themselves: they had been shallow-minded, pressing far ahead and leaving the great chariot without a proper guard.

The scene is unusual in Purāṇic war stories. Divine generals rarely admit error. Here the two greatest officers of the Goddess’s army do so plainly, and she responds not with rebuke but with orders to make the camp secure.

The lesson for readers is quiet but clear: even the best forces of the mind can rush ahead and leave the heart exposed, and the remedy is honest correction, not shame.

THE WALL OF FIRE

Jvālāmālinī’s rampart
WhoJvālāmālinī, “wreathed in flames”, one of the fifteen Nityās
SizeA ring a hundred yojanas round, thirty yojanas high
GateA single opening one yojana wide
DirectionFacing south, toward Bhaṇḍa’s city Śūnyaka
GuardsAttendants who never sleep or idle, stopping all coming and going
Sahasranāma“She who stands within the rampart of fire thrown up by Jvālāmālinī”

ZAPPY: A wall made of fire?

PROF. BOLT: A hundred yojanas round. Nobody sneaks up from behind again.

jvālāmālinikākṣiptavahniprākāramadhyagā

She who stands in the midst of the rampart of fire raised by Jvālāmālinī. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

Lalitā orders Jvālāmālinī, one of the fifteen Nityās and the goddess “wreathed in flames”, to raise a rampart of fire around the camp. The text gives its measure: a hundred yojanas round, thirty yojanas high, with a single opening a yojana wide, facing south toward Śūnyaka.

Tireless attendants who never sleep are posted to guard the opening, stopping anyone from entering or leaving without leave. After the treachery of the night, the camp is sealed.

The Sahasranāma remembers the scene with a name describing Lalitā standing in the midst of Jvālāmālinī’s rampart of fire. Devotees chanting it picture the Goddess at the still centre of a ring of flame.

THE CAMP

How the army is arranged

RightŚyāmalā’s chariot
Behind · in frontSampatkarī behind; the horse-goddess in front

ZAPPY: A whole battle plan in the text?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, a proper camp layout. Queen in the middle, officers to each side, cavalry forward, elephants back, a gatekeeper at the door.

The text sets out the camp in detail. It lies south of Mount Mahendra. Lalitā’s chariot stands at the centre; Daṇḍinī’s chariot on the left; Śyāmalā’s on the right; Sampatkarī behind; the horse-goddess in front. The gate is guarded by Stambhinī, “the one who stops”, also called Vighnadevī, with twenty armies.

Commentators who read the war inwardly note how closely this resembles a yantra: a centre, four directions, an enclosing wall, a single guarded gate. The camp is a Śrīcakra in battle formation.

The arrangement also makes plain the officers’ new resolve: never again will the queen be left exposed.

BĀLĀ ASKS TO FIGHT

Chapter 26
WhoBālā, Lalitā’s daughter, nine years old
Her requestTo fight Bhaṇḍa’s thirty sons: “it is my play”
Lalitā refusesShe is too young, and too dear to her mother
Bālā persistsShe will not be refused
Lalitā relentsShe embraces her daughter and lets her go
The officersTry to dissuade her too, then go with her

ZAPPY: She called a war her ‘play’?

PROF. BOLT: In Sanskrit, krīḍā: game, sport. To a goddess, even this battle is play. Her mother still worries, like any mother.

Bālā, Lalitā’s nine-year-old daughter, hears that Bhaṇḍa’s thirty sons are coming to battle and asks her mother to let her fight them. It is, she says, her play.

Lalitā refuses. Bālā is too young, and too dear: the text says she is as precious to the Goddess as her own life. Bālā keeps asking. Finally Lalitā embraces her and relents. Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā also try to talk her out of it, and when they fail, they ride with her.

The scene is tender and very human. The supreme Goddess, who will soon destroy Bhaṇḍa with weapons that summon the avatāras, hesitates like any mother when her child asks to face danger.

ZAPPY: Swans pulling a war-palanquin?

PROF. BOLT: Swans: the bird of purity and grace. A nine-year-old rides them into battle and comes back with thirty victories.

bhaṇḍaputravadhodyuktabālāvikramananditā

She who is delighted by the valour of Bālā, intent on slaying the sons of Bhaṇḍa. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

Bālā rides to battle in a covered palanquin drawn by hundreds of swans. Bhaṇḍa’s sons, led by Caturbāhu, come with an army of two hundred akṣauhiṇīs. She destroys the army with the Nārāyaṇa missile, then looses thirty crescent-tipped arrows, one for each son, and all thirty fall together.

The Śaktis are amazed. Lalitā rejoices at the news of her daughter’s deed, and Mantriṇī and Daṇḍanāthā embrace the child. The Sahasranāma records the moment in a name describing Lalitā’s delight at Bālā’s valour.

The news breaks Bhaṇḍa. Grief at his sons’ deaths drives the rest of the war, and from here his side turns more and more to tricks.

THE YANTRA OF OBSTACLES

Chapter 27
ViśukraAt Bhaṇḍa’s order he prepares the Jayavighna yantra
The stoneInscribed on a huge rock slab, a gavyūti square
The throwFrom outside the fire-wall he hurls it into the camp
EffectsDejection, listlessness, weapons laid down, sleep, apathy
WorseŚaktis ignore their commanders and neglect the Goddess’s worship
NameJayavighna: “obstacle to victory”

ZAPPY: A rock that makes you lazy?

PROF. BOLT: That's exactly what it is. The army doesn't lose a fight; it just stops caring. The most dangerous weapon in the war.

The first trick is Viśukra’s. At Bhaṇḍa’s order he prepares the Jayavighna yantra, “obstacle to victory”, inscribing it on a huge slab of rock. Standing outside the wall of fire, he hurls it over into the camp.

Its effect is not destruction but demoralisation. The Śaktis grow dejected and listless; they lay down their weapons, fall asleep, reel at the gate, ignore their commanders and even neglect the worship of the Goddess. The army that won every battle on the first day stops caring.

Many readers find this the most modern-seeming episode of the war. Every practitioner, student or worker knows days when a fog of apathy settles and nothing gets done. The Lalitopākhyāna gives that fog a name and a cure.

GAṆEŚA FROM A SMILE

The birth of Mahāgaṇapati
Lalitā seesHer army sunk in gloom
Her responseShe glances at Kāmeśvara and laughs
From the smileAn elephant-faced god appears from its radiance
His deedHe strikes the stone with his tusks and grinds it to powder
ResultThe Śaktis shake off their lethargy and take up arms again
SahasranāmaTwo names: his birth from her glance, and her joy at the shattered yantra

ZAPPY: She laughed, and that made Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: The cure for gloom, in this story, is a laugh between lovers. Joy defeats despair before any weapon does.

Lalitā sees her army sunk in gloom. She glances at Kāmeśvara and laughs, and from the radiance of her smile an elephant-faced god appears. He finds the yantra-stone, strikes it with his tusks, grinds it to powder and scatters it to the winds. At once the Śaktis shake off their lethargy and take up their weapons.

The Sahasranāma gives two names to this scene: one for Gaṇeśa’s birth from Lalitā’s glance at Kāmeśvara’s face, one for her joy at the shattered obstacle-yantra.

Of all the births of Gaṇeśa in the Purāṇas, this is perhaps the happiest: the remover of obstacles is born from a shared laugh between the divine couple.

ZAPPY: So that's why every pūjā starts with Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: According to this story, yes: a mother's reward for her son clearing the way.

Deities: Bālā · Mahāgaṇapati

Viśukra, defeated in his trick, sends Gajāsura, an elephant-demon, with seven armies. Gaṇeśa overpowers him; Viśukra flees; and Gaṇapati, alone and riding his mouse, destroys Gajāsura and his whole army.

Pleased with her son, Lalitā grants him a boon: he shall be worshipped before all other deities. This, the Lalitopākhyāna says, is why every act of worship begins with Gaṇeśa.

The Deities guide on Mahāgaṇapati describes his ten-armed form and his consort. Here he appears in action, a young god proving himself on his first day.

THE THIRST WEAPON

Day three, chapter 28
Viśukra’s weaponThe Tṛṣāstra, the missile of thirst
Its effectBurning thirst; parched palates; weak bodies
ResultThe Śaktis drop their weapons
Mantriṇī’s adviceOnly one thing can quench this: the ocean of liquor
DaṇḍanāthāShe commands the ocean to come
PatternAgain an attack on the army’s will, not its strength

ZAPPY: Another sneaky weapon from Viśukra?

PROF. BOLT: He's the trickster. First a stone of gloom, now a missile of thirst. He never fights straight.

On the third day, Viśukra tries again. He looses the Tṛṣāstra, the missile of thirst, at the Śakti army. A burning fever of thirst seizes them; their palates dry, their bodies weaken, and they drop their weapons.

Mantriṇī sees that ordinary water will not help and tells Daṇḍanāthā that only the ocean of liquor can quench this thirst. Daṇḍanāthā, with a single command from her chariot, summons it.

Like the Jayavighna yantra, the thirst weapon attacks the army’s will rather than its bodies. Viśukra is the war’s great psychological fighter: gloom one day, craving the next.

The partnership of the two officers is on display here. Mantriṇī, the counsellor, diagnoses the problem and names the remedy; Daṇḍanāthā, the commander, acts on it at once. Wisdom and command working together solve what neither could alone.

THE OCEAN OF WINE

How the army is revived
The oceanCalled Madirāsindhu and Surasindhu: the ocean of liquor
It comesAt Daṇḍanāthā’s command, it showers the army
A rampartThe goddess of the kadamba grove makes sure it falls only on the Śaktis
They drinkFor about two praharas, six hours
They fightRevived and exultant
ReadingŚākta commentators read the “wine” as the bliss of the Goddess

ZAPPY: Wine? In a holy war?

PROF. BOLT: The text says liquor. Śākta tradition reads it as ānanda: divine bliss that cures the thirst of the soul. Both readings are old.

The ocean, called Madirāsindhu and Surasindhu, pours its liquor over the army. The goddess of the kadamba grove raises a rampart so that it falls only on the Śaktis and not on the enemy. The Śaktis drink for about two praharas, and then, exultant, return to battle.

The text plainly describes liquor. Śākta commentators, who use wine as a symbol in their own tradition, read the ocean as ānanda, the bliss of the Goddess, which alone can quench the thirst of craving. Both readings have long histories, and we give them side by side.

For most devotees the scene simply shows the Goddess’s officers solving every problem Viśukra creates, and the army returning stronger.

The goddess who raises the rampart is called, in the text, the dweller in the kadamba grove, a title that the Sahasranāma gives to Lalitā herself and that tradition links with Śyāmalā’s grove in Śrīpura. Whoever she is, her role is protective: the gift reaches only those it is meant for.

Viṣaṅga in the textDaṇḍanāthā fights him with an iron club till midnight, and he falls
In the SahasranāmaThe names give it the other way: Vārāhī kills Viśukra, Mantriṇī kills Viṣaṅga
CommentatorsHave noted the difference for centuries
A Śṛṅgeri answer“Does it matter?” Purāṇic tellings often differ
AgreedThe brothers fall to the two officers; only Bhaṇḍa is left

ZAPPY: So which one's right?

PROF. BOLT: Both books are honoured. The Purāṇa tells the story one way, the hymn remembers it the other. We show both and keep chanting.

As the third day ends, the two brothers fall. In the Lalitopākhyāna’s own telling, chapter 28, Mantriṇī cuts down Viśukra’s flagstaff, charioteer and bowstring and kills him with the Brahmaśiras missile, while Daṇḍanāthā fights Viṣaṅga with an iron club until midnight and he falls.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma gives the pairing the other way round: Lalitā delighted by Vārāhī’s valour in taking Viśukra’s life, and by Mantriṇī’s slaying of Viṣaṅga. Commentators noticed the difference long ago; some argue for the Purāṇa’s order, most devotees follow the hymn they chant.

A Śṛṅgeri pontiff, asked about it, is reported to have smiled and asked whether it matters, since Purāṇic tellings differ often. On the essential point both texts agree: Bhaṇḍa’s brothers fall to the Goddess’s two chief officers, and only Bhaṇḍa remains.

WHAT IT MEANS

Days two and three, read inwardly
HumilityThe officers own their fault; the queen fixes, not blames
ProtectionA wall of fire around what is precious
InnocenceA child’s courage wins where armies hesitate
DespairThe subtlest weapon; cured by joy, not force
ThirstCraving answered by bliss, in the Śākta reading
Inner readingBhaṇḍa’s sons as restless thoughts; Viśukra’s tricks as discouragement and craving

ZAPPY: So the real enemies are gloom and craving?

PROF. BOLT: That's how many teachers read it. And the cures in the story: a child's courage, a laugh, and the Goddess's bliss.

These two days are full of inner meaning for those who read the war as a map of the mind. Bhaṇḍa’s thirty sons are restless thoughts, cut down by a child’s simple, undivided devotion. Viśukra’s weapons are discouragement and craving, the two great saboteurs of any practice.

The cures are telling. Discouragement is not defeated by effort or discipline but by joy: a laugh between Lalitā and Kāmeśvara produces the god who breaks it. Craving is not defeated by suppression but by a greater sweetness: the ocean of the Goddess’s bliss.

And underneath it all is the wall of fire and the officers’ humility: the practitioner’s heart protected, and the mind honest about its mistakes.
