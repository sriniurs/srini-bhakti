BHAṆḌA FALLS, KĀMA RETURNS

The last battle, and a widow’s wish

LALITOPĀKHYĀNA · PART 6 OF 6

FOREWORD

On the fourth day, with every brother, son and general dead, Bhaṇḍa comes out himself, and Lalitā rides to meet him. Every weapon he throws she answers, and from her fingernails rise the avatāras of Viṣṇu.

Part 6 follows chapters 29 and 30: the final duel, the burning of Śūnyaka, and the ending that closes the circle, when Rati’s grief moves the Goddess to bring Kāma back to life.

ZAPPY: Finally, Lalitā fights?

PROF. BOLT: Herself. Weapon against weapon. And then, the most beautiful ending in the Purāṇas.

This is the last part of our retelling. On the fourth day of the war, with his brothers, sons and generals dead, Bhaṇḍa comes out himself, and Lalitā rides out to meet him. The duel that follows is the most spectacular passage of the Lalitopākhyāna: a contest of weapons in which every evil Bhaṇḍa summons is answered by its exact remedy.

Then comes the ending, told in chapter 30, that makes the whole story cohere. Rati, Kāma’s widow, grieves; the Goddess restores Kāma to life; Śiva and Pārvatī are united; and Skanda is born to kill Tāraka, the demon whose threat began the story in Part 1.

The text is followed closely, using G. V. Tagare’s translation for chapter 29 and a published summary for chapter 30.

THE QUEEN RIDES OUT

Chapter 29
Her chariotCakrarāja: its wheels the four Vedas, its horses the four aims of life, its flag Bliss
OrderGeyacakra leads; Kiricakra brings up the rear
The ŚaktisRiding man-lions, camels, tigers and more
The wallJvālāmālinī widens the gate in the fire
Her exitTo drums and falling flowers
OpponentBhaṇḍa, alone now: brothers, sons and generals gone

ZAPPY: Her horses are the four aims of life?

PROF. BOLT: Dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa: duty, wealth, love, freedom. All four pull her chariot. Nothing in life is left out of her victory.

Lalitā rides out on Cakrarāja, now described in symbolic terms: its wheels are the four Vedas, its horses the four aims of life, dharma, artha, kāma and mokṣa, and its flag is bliss. Geyacakra leads the column and Kiricakra guards the rear; the Śaktis ride man-lions, camels, tigers and other mounts.

Jvālāmālinī widens the opening in the wall of fire so the armies can pass, and the Goddess leaves the camp to the sound of drums and a rain of flowers.

The image of the four aims of life as her horses is worth pausing on. Śrīvidyā’s view that enjoyment and freedom go together is built into her chariot: all four aims of a human life pull together toward the victory.

THE DUEL OF WEAPONS

Bhaṇḍa’s astras and her answers
Great darknessAnswered by the Mahātaraṇi, the great sun-arrow
HeresyAnswered by Gāyatrī
BlindnessAnswered by Cakṣuṣmattā, the gift of sight
DeathAnswered by Mahāmṛtyuñjaya, the conqueror of death
Forgetting all weaponsAnswered by Dhāraṇā, steady memory
Fear · disease · short lifeAnswered by Indra’s missile · the three names of Viṣṇu · Kālasaṅkarṣaṇī

ZAPPY: Every weapon has its answer?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly matched. Darkness meets the sun; forgetting meets memory; death meets the death-conqueror. It's a catalogue of remedies.

Bhaṇḍa opens with weapons that attack not bodies but faculties. Andhatāmisraka, great darkness, is dispelled by the Mahātaraṇi, the great sun. Pākhaṇḍāstra, the weapon of heresy and false teaching, is answered by Gāyatrī, the Vedic mantra of light. The weapon of blindness is answered by Cakṣuṣmattā, clear sight.

Against Antakāstra, the weapon of death, she sends Mahāmṛtyuñjaya, the conqueror of death. Against the weapon that makes warriors forget all their weapons, she sends Dhāraṇā, steady memory. Fear is answered by Indra’s missile; great diseases by the three names of Viṣṇu, Acyuta, Ananta and Govinda; loss of life-span by Kālasaṅkarṣaṇī.

The list reads like a manual of the spiritual life: every inner affliction, from ignorance and doubt to fear and the dread of death, has a matching remedy in the Goddess’s armoury.

ZAPPY: Durgā turns up in Lalitā's war?

PROF. BOLT: As her answer to Bhaṇḍa's weapon that raises the old demons. Mahiṣa returns, and so does his slayer. The Devī Māhātmya inside the Lalitopākhyāna.

On this site: Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts

Bhaṇḍa’s Mahāsurāstra summons the great asuras of the past, Madhu, Mahiṣa and others, back into battle. Lalitā answers with Durgā, who arises from the radiance of the gods exactly as she does in the Devī Māhātmya, and destroys them again.

This is a striking moment for readers who know both Purāṇas. The whole Devī Māhātmya is folded into a single exchange of weapons: Lalitā contains Durgā, and Durgā’s victories are hers.

Bhaṇḍa’s weapon of dumbness, meant to silence the Śaktis, is answered by Mahāvāgvādinī, the great goddess of eloquent speech, and the weapon of fainting by the Śāmbhava missile. No affliction, of body, mind or speech, is left without a cure.

THE AVATĀRAS FROM HER FINGERNAILS

The heart of the battle
Ocean weaponThe Tortoise, from her right index nail
HiraṇyākṣaThe Boar, from her right middle nail
HiraṇyakaśipusNarasiṃha, from her right ring finger
Bali’s weaponVāmana, from her right little finger
The HaihayasParaśurāma, from her left thumb
RāvaṇaRāma with Lakṣmaṇa, from her left index nail
Dvivida the vānaraBalarāma, from her left middle nail
Demon kings · KaliThe four forms of Vāsudeva · Kalki

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu's avatāras came out of her fingernails?

PROF. BOLT: Every one Bhaṇḍa needed. Whatever demon he summoned from an old story, she produced the hero who'd beaten it.

Then comes the passage for which this chapter is most famous. Bhaṇḍa calls up, one by one, the great demons and threats that Viṣṇu’s avatāras once defeated. And from Lalitā’s fingernails, one after another, those very avatāras appear and defeat them again.

Against the ocean-weapon, the Tortoise rises from her right index nail; against Hiraṇyākṣa, the Boar; against many Hiraṇyakaśipus springing from Bhaṇḍa’s eyebrows, Narasiṃha, who tears them apart; against Bali’s weapon, Vāmana; against the Haihaya kings, Paraśurāma; against Rāvaṇa, Rāma with Lakṣmaṇa; against Dvivida the rogue vānara, Balarāma; against demon kings, the four forms of Vāsudeva; against Kali, Kalki.

The text says the leaders of ten avatāras did the work, though it names nine explicitly. The point is plain: every saving act of Viṣṇu in history is here shown as flowing from the Goddess’s own hand.

IN THE SAHASRANĀMA

The final battle in four names

PāśupataShe who burned the asura army in the fire of the great Pāśupata missile

ZAPPY: The whole fight is in the Sahasranāma?

PROF. BOLT: Four names, one breath each. Chant them and you've watched the war's last hour.

bhaṇḍāsurendranirmuktaśastrapratyastravarṣiṇī
karāṅgulinakhotpannanārāyaṇadaśākṛtiḥ
mahāpāśupatāstrāgninirdagdhāsurasainikā
kāmeśvarāstranirdagdhasabhaṇḍāsuraśūnyakā

She who showers counter-weapons on the weapons of Bhaṇḍa; from whose fingernails the ten forms of Nārāyaṇa arose; who burned the asura army in the fire of the great Pāśupata missile; who burned Bhaṇḍa and his city Śūnyaka with the Kāmeśvara missile. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

Four consecutive names of the Lalitā Sahasranāma compress this entire chapter. The first describes the Goddess raining counter-weapons on Bhaṇḍa’s weapons; the second, the ten forms of Nārāyaṇa arising from her fingernails; the third, the Pāśupata fire consuming the asura army; the fourth, Bhaṇḍa and Śūnyaka burned by the Kāmeśvara missile.

Bhāskararāya’s commentary on these names explains each with reference to the Purāṇa, and adds inner readings in which the avatāras are the Goddess’s powers within the practitioner.

Many devotees who could not recount the story in detail know it through these four names, chanted daily.

THE END OF BHAṆḌA

Three great missiles
Nārāyaṇa missileTurns his remaining armies to ash
Pāśupata missileDestroys his forty generals
Mahākāmeśvara missileKills Bhaṇḍa
ŚūnyakaBurns with all its people, cattle, riches and granaries
What remainsBare ground: “the empty city”, emptied
EchoBorn from ash, he returns to ash

ZAPPY: The three missiles are Viṣṇu's, Śiva's and…?

PROF. BOLT: Kāmeśvara's: Śiva as lord of desire, through her. The demon born of burned desire is finished by desire made divine.

The end comes in three blows. The Nārāyaṇa missile reduces the remaining armies to ash. The Pāśupata missile, Śiva’s supreme weapon, destroys Bhaṇḍa’s forty generals. And the Mahākāmeśvara missile, the weapon of the lord of desire, kills Bhaṇḍa himself.

Śūnyaka burns with everything in it, its people, cattle, riches and granaries, until only bare ground remains. The “empty city” is finally empty. Bhaṇḍa, born from ash in Part 1, returns to ash.

The choice of the final weapon carries the story’s meaning. Bhaṇḍa was born from desire destroyed carelessly. He is ended by the power of desire mastered: Kāmeśvara’s weapon, wielded by Kāmeśvarī.

THE WORLDS DANCE

After the victory
The worlds“The three worlds danced with delight”
KāmeśvarīShines in victory
The armyBrought back to camp at sunset, weary and triumphant
MeritThe chapter promises freedom from sin to those who hear it
The godsPraise her glory: Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Indra and all
ButOne grief remains unanswered

ZAPPY: Everyone's happy, then?

PROF. BOLT: Almost everyone. One person in the whole universe is still weeping.

The text says simply that the three worlds danced with delight. Kāmeśvarī shines in her victory, and the weary army is brought back to camp at sunset. The gods, led by Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Indra, praise her glory; the Sahasranāma has a name for this praise too.

The chapter ends, as Purāṇic chapters often do, with a promise: those who read or hear this account will be freed from sin and gain spiritual powers.

But the story is not quite over. The war began, long before Bhaṇḍa, with a grief that has not yet been answered.

There is a quiet detail in the return to camp: the Goddess who has just destroyed an empire leads her tired soldiers home at sunset. The epic war ends with an image any soldier would recognise, the evening after a long day, the fighting done.

RATI’S GRIEF

Chapter 30
RatiKāma’s wife, widowed since Part 1
Her griefShe comes before the Goddess mourning her husband
The godsJoin her plea
LalitāIs moved by Rati’s sorrow
The questionCan the one burned by Śiva’s eye live again?
Her answerYes

ZAPPY: The whole war, and Rati was waiting all along?

PROF. BOLT: From the first page. The burning of Kāma began everything. His wife's tears end it.

In chapter 30, Rati comes before the Goddess. She has been a widow since Kāma was burned on the Himālaya, at the very beginning of our Part 1. Now, with the demon born of her husband’s ash destroyed, she asks for her husband back. The gods join her plea.

Lalitā is moved. The supreme Goddess, just victorious over the most powerful asura of the age, turns to the grief of a single widow.

This is one of the loveliest turns in Purāṇic literature: the epic war does not end with a triumph but with an act of compassion.

ZAPPY: So the god of love comes back and does the job he was sent to do!

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The arrows that started this story finish it. And this time no one burns.

haranetrāgnisaṃdagdhakāmasaṃjīvanauṣadhiḥ

She who is the life-giving herb for Kāma, burned by the fire of Hara’s eye. (Lalitā Sahasranāma)

Lalitā restores Kāma to life. The Sahasranāma remembers this in a name calling her the life-giving herb for Kāma, burned by the fire of Hara’s eye. The god of love lives again.

Restored, Kāma does what he was sent to do in the first place. His arrows unite Śiva and Pārvatī; their son Skanda is born and, in the familiar story, kills Tāraka. The problem that set the whole tale in motion is finally solved.

Kāma, now called Madana, returns to Śrīpura to serve the Goddess. Desire, once the cause of disaster, has found its true home: in her service.

THE WHOLE STORY

Part 1Kāma burned; from his ash Bhaṇḍa
Part 2The gods give themselves; she rises; she weds
Part 3Her city: a map of the inward road
Part 4The first day: each threat met by a Śakti
Part 5A child, a laugh, a wall of fire, an ocean
Part 6Bhaṇḍa to ash; Kāma to life

ZAPPY: It starts and ends with Kāma!

PROF. BOLT: That's the shape of it. Desire destroyed badly makes a demon. Desire restored by the Goddess makes the world whole.

Seen whole, the Lalitopākhyāna is a perfectly shaped story. It opens with Kāma burned and a demon born from his ash. It closes with the demon burned and Kāma born again. Between, the Goddess rises from the gods’ total offering, marries the lord of desire, builds her city and wins her war.

Every element has its place. Her weapons are Kāma’s own bow and arrows, given by the gods. Her husband is Śiva transformed into the lord of desire. Her final weapon is named for him. Her last act is to restore desire to life.

The deity guides of this series pick up the figures of the story one by one; the Śrīcakra guides map her city and army; the hymn guides give the Sahasranāma, which retells it all in a thousand names.

ZAPPY: You're skipping the last chapters?

PROF. BOLT: The story chapters are done. The ones left teach practice, and practice comes from a teacher. Kāmākṣī's chapters we'll meet in the Temples section.

After the story, the Lalitopākhyāna continues for many chapters. Chapters 31 to 37 describe Śrīpura, retold in our Part 3. Chapters 39 and 40 tell stories of Kāmākṣī at Kāñcī, which will appear in the Temples section of this series.

Chapter 38 and chapters 41 to 44 turn to practice: the Goddess’s mantra, japa, nyāsa, mudrās, the worship of the Śrīcakra, the kinds of initiation and the rites of daily worship. Following the rule set out at the start of this series, these are not summarised here. They are the domain of teachers.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma itself comes at the very end, taught by Hayagrīva to Agastya as the crown of the whole teaching.

Readers who want to know the shape of practice without its details will find it in the Foundations guides: what Śrīvidyā is, why its mantra is given by a teacher, and how its worship centres on the Śrīcakra. Those who want the practice itself should seek a teacher in a recognised line.

WHAT IT MEANS

The war, read whole
RemediesEvery evil has a matching cure in the Goddess
All traditionsViṣṇu’s avatāras, Durgā, Gāyatrī, Śiva’s missiles: all serve her
Desire redeemedThe weapon that kills the demon is Kāmeśvara’s
CompassionThe victor’s last act is to heal a widow’s grief
RestorationNot just victory: the world’s order put back, and love returned
Inner readingThe ego born of suppressed desire dissolves; desire returns, purified, to serve the Goddess

ZAPPY: So the lesson is…?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess doesn't destroy desire. She destroys what grows from crushing it, and gives desire back, made holy.

Read as a whole, the final duel says that every affliction has its remedy in the Goddess. Darkness, doubt, blindness, death, forgetting, fear, disease, speechlessness: each is answered. And the remedies come from every tradition: Viṣṇu’s avatāras, Durgā, Gāyatrī, Śiva’s missiles. In the Śrīvidyā view, all of them are hers.

The ending adds a deeper point. The Goddess does not destroy desire; she destroys what grows from its careless destruction, and then gives desire back, purified and in her service. That is the Lalitopākhyāna’s answer to the puzzle set in Part 1.

And the last act of the victorious queen is to heal a widow’s grief. Power, in this story, ends in compassion.

For a devotee chanting the Sahasranāma, the story’s two ends meet in two names a few lines apart: the Kāmeśvara missile that burns Bhaṇḍa, and the life-giving herb that restores Kāma. Fire that destroys, and medicine that heals, from the same Goddess, in the same breath.
