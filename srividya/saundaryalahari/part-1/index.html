ŚRĪ SAUNDARYALAHARĪ · PART 1 OF 5 · VERSES 1–20

SAUNDARYALAHARĪ

The wave of beauty · Ānandalaharī begins

HYMNS OF THE GODDESS

HOW THIS WORKS: All twenty verses in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive into the ideas and stories behind it.

WHERE WE ARE

The Saundaryalaharī, “wave of beauty”, is a hymn of a hundred verses to the Goddess as Tripurasundarī, traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara; some scholars place it later, and we note the attribution as traditional. Its first 41 verses, the Ānandalaharī, “wave of bliss”, carry much of Śrīvidyā’s teaching; the rest praise her form from crown to feet. This is Part 1 of 5.

Our text opens, as the Vaidika Vignanam edition does, with a short prayer verse, bhumauskhalita pādānāṃ bhūmirevā valambanam, tvayī jātā parādhānāṃ tvameva śaraṇaṃ śive: “for those whose feet slip on the ground, the ground itself is the support; for those who have offended you, you alone are the refuge, O Śivā.”

Tradition attaches a yantra and a particular use to every verse of the Saundaryalaharī. Those belong to teachers, and we leave them out. Some verses (9, 10, 11, 19) also point to inner teachings of Śrīvidyā; we explain the words and do not teach the practice.

VERSE 1

ŚIVA NEEDS ŚAKTI
śivaḥ śaktyā yukto yadi bhavati śaktaḥ prabhavituṃna cedevaṃ devo na khalu kuśalaḥ spanditumapiatastvāmārādhyāṃ hariharaviriñcādibhirapipraṇantuṃ stotuṃ vā kathamakṛtapuṇyaḥ prabhavati ‖1‖
Only when Śiva is joined with Śakti is he able to create; otherwise the god cannot even stir. So how could anyone who has made no merit bow to you or praise you, whom even Viṣṇu, Śiva, Brahmā and the rest worship?
WordMeans
śivaḥ śaktyā yuktaḥŚiva, joined with Śakti
prabhavitumto bring forth, to create
spanditum apieven to stir
hari-hara-viriñciViṣṇu, Śiva, Brahmā
akṛta-puṇyaḥone who has made no merit
Without Śakti, Śiva cannot even stir.

VERSE 1 · DEEP DIVE

ŚIVA NEEDS ŚAKTI

🔎 In context

The Saundaryalaharī, “wave of beauty”, is a hundred-verse hymn to the Goddess as Tripurasundarī, traditionally ascribed to Ādi Śaṅkara. Many editions divide it into the Ānandalaharī, “wave of bliss”, verses 1–41, which is dense with Śrīvidyā teaching, and the Saundaryalaharī proper, 42–100, which praises her form from crown to feet. This first verse states the whole theology in four lines.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators love a small play on letters here: take the vowel i, standing for Śakti, out of śiva and you are left with śava, a corpse. Without his power even the supreme god is inert. The second half turns from doctrine to devotion: if the three great gods worship her, bowing to her is itself a sign of past merit. The verse quietly asks the reader to count themselves fortunate for having come this far.

🌱 For today

Power and presence go together. Stillness alone does nothing; energy alone has no ground.

VERSE 2

THE DUST OF HER FEET
tanīyāṃsaṃ pāṃsuṃ tava caraṇapaṅkeruhabhavaṃviriñcissañcinvan viracayati lokānavikalamvahatyenaṃ śauriḥ kathamapi sahasreṇa śirasāṃharassaṅkṣudyainaṃ bhajati bhasitoddhūlanavidhim ‖2‖
Gathering a tiny speck of dust born from your lotus feet, Brahmā creates the worlds without flaw; Śauri bears it, somehow, on his thousand heads; and Hara crushes it to powder and smears it on himself as sacred ash.
WordMeans
tanīyāṃsaṃ pāṃsuma very fine grain of dust
caraṇa-paṅkeruhalotus feet
viriñciḥBrahmā, the creator
śauriḥ … sahasreṇa śirasāmViṣṇu, as the thousand-headed serpent who bears the worlds
bhasitoddhūlanasmearing with sacred ash
Three gods, one grain of her dust.

VERSE 2 · DEEP DIVE

THE DUST OF HER FEET

🔎 In context

This verse turns the three great gods into three uses of one speck of dust. Brahmā creates from it, Viṣṇu (as Śeṣa, the serpent of a thousand hoods on whom the worlds rest) supports it, and Śiva, the destroyer, grinds it into the ash he wears. Creation, preservation and dissolution become three ways of handling the dust of her feet.

📜 Looking deeper

The scale is the point. The whole universe, made, held up and dissolved, is a grain of dust from her feet, and “somehow” (kathamapi) Viṣṇu manages to carry even that. Devotional poetry often praises a deity by belittling the others; here the gods are not belittled but placed: each is great because of what he does with her dust.

🌱 For today

If the greatest gods prize the dust of her feet, humility is no loss of dignity.

↑ VERSE 2 · Three gods, one grain of her dust.

VERSE 3

WHAT THE DUST DOES
avidyānāmanta-stimira-mihiradvīpanagarījaḍānāṃ caitanya-stabaka-makaranda-srutijharīdaridrāṇāṃ cintāmaṇiguṇanikā janmajaladhaunimagnānāṃ daṃṣṭrā muraripu-varāhasya bhavati ‖3‖
For the ignorant it is an island city of the sun that ends inner darkness; for the dull, a cascade of honey from the flower-clusters of consciousness; for the poor, a string of wish-gems; and for those sunk in the ocean of birth, it is the tusk of the Boar, Viṣṇu, that lifts them out.
WordMeans
avidyānāmfor those without knowledge
antas-timira-mihira-dvīpa-nagarīisland city of the sun that ends inner darkness
caitanya-stabaka-makarandahoney from clusters of consciousness
cintāmaṇi-guṇanikāa string of wish-fulfilling gems
murāripu-varāhasya daṃṣṭrāthe tusk of Varāha, Viṣṇu
Light, honey, wealth and rescue.

VERSE 3 · DEEP DIVE

WHAT THE DUST DOES

🔎 In context

The subject is still the dust of her feet from verse 2. Four kinds of people, four gifts: knowledge for the ignorant, awakening for the dull, wealth for the poor, rescue for the drowning. The last image recalls Viṣṇu’s Boar avatāra, who lifted the earth from the cosmic ocean on his tusk.

📜 Looking deeper

Each image is exact. The sun ends darkness; honey wakes the senses; the wish-gem (cintāmaṇi) grants every wish; the tusk lifts. The verse moves from the inner life to the outer and back, and ends with the deepest need: rescue from the ocean of repeated birth. The Goddess’s grace, the poet says, meets each person where they stand.

🌱 For today

Grace does not come in one shape. It comes as what the person in front of you most needs.

↑ VERSE 3 · Light, honey, wealth and rescue.

VERSE 4

HER FEET ALONE
tvadanyaḥ pāṇibhyāmabhayavarado daivatagaṇaḥtvamekā naivāsi prakaṭitavarābhītyabhinayābhayāt trātuṃ dātuṃ phalamapi ca vāñchāsamadhikaṃśaraṇye lokānāṃ tava hi caraṇāveva nipuṇau ‖4‖
All the gods other than you show the gestures of fearlessness and boon-giving with their hands. You alone make no such display. For your feet alone are skilled enough to protect the worlds from fear and to give more than they desire, O refuge of the worlds.
WordMeans
abhaya-varadathe gestures of fearlessness and boon-giving
prakaṭita … abhinayādisplaying, acting out
vāñchā-samadhikammore than what is wished for
śaraṇyeO refuge
nipuṇauthe two expert ones (your feet)
Her feet protect and give.

VERSE 4 · DEEP DIVE

HER FEET ALONE

🔎 In context

In temple images, most deities hold one hand raised in abhaya (“fear not”) and one lowered in varada (“I give”). Lalitā is shown holding her four weapons and does not need these gestures. The poet turns that into praise: what others signal with their hands, her feet simply do.

📜 Looking deeper

Taking refuge at the feet is the oldest gesture of Indian devotion. The verse makes the feet themselves the source of protection and of gifts, which is why so many hymns, including this one, linger over the feet of the Goddess. “More than desired” matters: she does not merely meet the wish; she exceeds it.

🌱 For today

Real help often makes no announcement. It simply arrives.

↑ VERSE 4 · Her feet protect and give.

VERSE 5

MOHINĪ AND KĀMA
haristvāmārādhya praṇatajanasaubhāgyajananīṃpurā nārī bhūtvā puraripumapi kṣobhamanayatsmaro'pi tvāṃ natvā ratinayanalehyena vapuṣāmunīnāmapyantaḥ prabhavati hi mohāya mahatām ‖5‖
Hari, by worshipping you, the mother of good fortune for all who bow, once became a woman and stirred even Śiva, the destroyer of the cities. And Smara too, bowing to you, with a body that Rati’s eyes drink in, troubles the hearts of even the great sages.
WordMeans
hariḥ … nārī bhūtvāViṣṇu, becoming a woman (Mohinī)
puraripumŚiva, enemy of the three cities
saubhāgya-jananīmmother of good fortune
smaraḥKāma, the god of love
rati-nayana-lehyena vapuṣāwith a body Rati’s eyes feed on
Enchantment, too, comes from her.

VERSE 5 · DEEP DIVE

MOHINĪ AND KĀMA

🔎 In context

Two famous stories of enchantment are traced back to her. Viṣṇu took the form of Mohinī, the enchantress, when the ocean was churned, and later captivated Śiva himself. Kāma, the god of love, has the power to unsettle even sages. Both powers, the verse says, were gained by worshipping her.

📜 Looking deeper

Śrīvidyā teaches that Kāma was the first worshipper of the Goddess’s vidyā; one of its traditional forms is named after him. This verse is the hymn’s nod to that teaching. It also widens what is meant by beauty: the power to attract, to move hearts, is itself a gift of the Goddess, to be used rightly.

🌱 For today

Charm is a power, and like every power it is borrowed. Use it with care.

↑ VERSE 5 · Enchantment, too, comes from her.

VERSE 6

THE BODILESS ARCHER
dhanuḥ pauṣpaṃ maurvī madhukaramayī pañca viśikhāḥvasantaḥ sāmanto malayamarudāyodhanarathaḥtathāpyekaḥ sarvaṃ himagirisute kāmapi kṛpāmapāṅgātte labdhvā jagadida-manaṅgo vijayate ‖6‖
His bow is of flowers, its string a line of bees, his arrows just five; Spring is his general and the Malaya breeze his war chariot. Even so, the bodiless one, alone, having gained some grace from the corner of your eye, O daughter of the snow mountain, conquers this whole world.
WordMeans
dhanuḥ pauṣpama bow of flowers
maurvī madhukaramayīa bowstring made of bees
pañca viśikhāḥfive arrows
vasantaḥ sāmantaḥSpring, his feudal general
anaṅgaḥthe bodiless one: Kāma after Śiva burned him
One glance makes the weakest army win.

VERSE 6 · DEEP DIVE

THE BODILESS ARCHER

🔎 In context

Kāma’s weapons are famously weak: flowers, bees, five arrows, a season and a breeze. He has not even a body, since Śiva’s third eye burned it. The Lalitopākhyāna tells how the Goddess restored him; this verse says that with one glance of hers he conquers everything anyway.

📜 Looking deeper

The joke is gentle and serious at once. An army of flowers wins every war because the Goddess’s favour is behind it. The verse is also a reminder of the Lalitopākhyāna’s order of events: Kāma burned, Bhaṇḍa born from his ash, the Goddess arising and Kāma revived by her glance.

🌱 For today

Small means are enough when grace is behind them.

↑ VERSE 6 · One glance makes the weakest army win.

VERSE 7

MAY SHE APPEAR BEFORE US
kvaṇatkāñcīdāmā karikalabhakumbhastananatāparikṣīṇā madhye pariṇataśaraccandravadanādhanurbāṇān pāśaṃ sṛṇimapi dadhānā karatalaiḥpurastādāstāṃ naḥ puramathiturāhopuruṣikā ‖7‖
With a jingling girdle, bending a little under breasts like the temples of a young elephant, slender at the waist, her face like the full autumn moon, holding the bow, the arrows, the noose and the goad in her hands: may she, the very pride of Śiva, destroyer of the cities, appear before us.
WordMeans
kvaṇat-kāñcī-dāmāwith a tinkling girdle
pariṇata-śarac-candra-vadanāface like the full autumn moon
dhanur-bāṇān pāśaṃ sṛṇimbow, arrows, noose and goad
purastāt āstāmmay she stand before (us)

VERSE 7 · DEEP DIVE

MAY SHE APPEAR BEFORE US

🔎 In context

This is the hymn’s first full picture of Lalitā, and it matches the Sahasranāma: four arms with the sugarcane bow, the flower arrows, the noose and the goad. Her face is the full moon of autumn, the brightest of the year. The prayer is simple: may she be present in front of us.

📜 Looking deeper

The last word is unusual. Āhopuruṣikā means pride or self-assertion, “I am the one!” The poet calls the Goddess Śiva’s own sense of “I”: what makes the supreme Self aware of itself. It is a compressed statement of the Śākta view that Śakti is Śiva’s own self-awareness.

🌱 For today

Before asking for anything, ask for presence.

↑ VERSE 7 · May she stand before us.

VERSE 8

THE ISLAND OF GEMS
sudhāsindhormadhye suraviṭapivāṭīparivṛtemaṇidvīpe nīpopavanavati cintāmaṇigṛheśivākāre mañce paramaśivaparyaṅkanilayāṃbhajanti tvāṃ dhanyāḥ katicana cidānandalaharīm ‖8‖
In the middle of the ocean of nectar, ringed by gardens of wish-granting trees, on the island of gems, in a palace of wish-gems in a grove of kadamba trees, on a couch shaped of the Śivas, resting in the lap of Paramaśiva: only a blessed few worship you, wave of consciousness and bliss.
WordMeans
sudhā-sindhuocean of nectar
maṇi-dvīpathe island of gems
nīpa-upavanakadamba grove
cintāmaṇi-gṛhathe palace of wish-gems
cid-ānanda-laharīwave of consciousness and bliss
Ocean, island, palace, her.

VERSE 8 · DEEP DIVE

THE ISLAND OF GEMS

🔎 In context

Here is Lalitā’s home as the tradition pictures it: Maṇidvīpa, an island of gems in an ocean of nectar, with the Cintāmaṇi palace at its heart. The Lalitopākhyāna builds the same city, Śrīpura, ring within ring. Her couch is formed of gods (in later descriptions, Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra and Īśvara as its legs and Sadāśiva as its plank).

📜 Looking deeper

The verse travels inward, from ocean to island to garden to grove to palace to couch, and ends in a name: cidānandalaharī, wave of consciousness and bliss. It mirrors the movement through the Śrīcakra from the outer square to the bindu. “Only a blessed few” is a gentle note that such worship is rare and precious.

🌱 For today

A long journey inward ends in a very simple place.

VERSE 9

THROUGH THE SIX CENTRES
mahīṃ mūlādhāre kamapi maṇipūre hutavahaṃsthitaṃ svādhiṣṭhāne hṛdi marutamākāśamuparimano'pi bhrūmadhye sakalamapi bhitvā kulapathaṃsahasrāre padme saha rahasi patyā viharase ‖9‖
Piercing earth in the mūlādhāra, water in the maṇipūra, fire in the svādhiṣṭhāna, air in the heart, space above it, and the mind between the eyebrows, the whole path of the kula, you sport in secret with your Lord in the thousand-petalled lotus.
WordMeans
mūlādhārathe root centre
maṇipūra / svādhiṣṭhānathe navel centre / the centre above the root
kula-pathamthe path of the kula, through the centres
sahasrāre padmein the thousand-petalled lotus

Six centres pierced, union at the crown.

VERSE 9 · DEEP DIVE

THROUGH THE SIX CENTRES

🔎 In context

This verse and the next describe the Goddess rising through the six centres of the subtle body to unite with Śiva at the crown, and returning. They are among the most quoted verses on kuṇḍalinī. We describe the imagery; any practice connected with it belongs to a teacher.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators note that the verse places water in the maṇipūra and fire in the svādhiṣṭhāna, the reverse of the order in many yoga texts, and explain the difference by the school the hymn follows. The six elements, earth to mind, are left behind one by one, and what remains is union. Śrīvidyā reads this as the inner meaning of Lalitā and Kāmeśvara in the bindu.

🌱 For today

What is outside, the elements, is also inside. The journey is inward and upward.

↑ VERSE 9 · Six centres pierced, union at the crown.

VERSE 10

THE RETURN
sudhādhārāsāraiścaraṇayugalāntarvigalitaiḥprapañcaṃ siñcantī punarapi rasāmnāyamahasaḥavāpya svāṃ bhūmiṃ bhujaganibhamadhyuṣṭavalayaṃsvamātmānaṃ kṛtvā svapiṣi kulakuṇḍe kuhariṇi ‖10‖
Showering the whole universe again with streams of nectar that flow from between your feet, you come back from the radiance of the moon-realm, regain your own place, make yourself coiled like a serpent and sleep in the hollow of the kula-kuṇḍa.
WordMeans
sudhā-dhārā-āsāraiḥwith showers of nectar
prapañcaṃ siñcantīsprinkling the universe
rasāmnāya-mahasaḥfrom the moon’s radiance
bhujaga-nibhamlike a serpent
kula-kuṇḍe svapiṣiyou sleep in the hollow of the kula
Up to union, down as nectar.

VERSE 10 · DEEP DIVE

THE RETURN

🔎 In context

The ascent of verse 9 is followed by the return. Having united with Śiva above, the Goddess descends, bathing every centre and the whole universe in nectar, and coils again at the base, asleep. The coiled serpent image is the source of the word kuṇḍalinī, “she who is coiled”.

📜 Looking deeper

The two verses together make a full cycle, up and down, union and return. Commentators read it as the rhythm of the whole cosmos as well as of the meditator: creation flows out as nectar, rests, and rises again. Again, we leave practice to teachers and stay with the poem.

🌱 For today

After every peak comes a return that nourishes everything on the way down.

↑ VERSE 10 · Up to union, down as nectar.

VERSE 11

THE ŚRĪCAKRA
caturbhiḥ śrīkaṇṭhaiḥ śivayuvatibhiḥ pañcabhirapiprabhinnābhiḥ śambhornavabhirapi mūlaprakṛtibhiḥcatuścatvāriṃśadvasudalakalāśratrivalaya-trirekhābhiḥ sārdhaṃ tava śaraṇakoṇāḥ pariṇatāḥ ‖11‖
With four triangles of Śiva and five of Śakti, nine root natures distinct from Śambhu, and with the eight-petalled and sixteen-petalled lotuses, the three circles and the three lines, the corners of your dwelling come to forty-four.
WordMeans
catvāraḥ śrīkaṇṭhāḥfour Śiva triangles
pañca śiva-yuvatayaḥfive Śakti triangles
vasu-dala / kalā-aśraeight petals / sixteen petals
tri-valaya, tri-rekhāthree circles, three lines
catuś-catvāriṃśatforty-four
The Śrīcakra, in four lines.

VERSE 11 · DEEP DIVE

THE ŚRĪCAKRA

🔎 In context

This is the most precise description of the Śrīcakra in any hymn. Nine interlocking triangles, four pointing up (Śiva) and five pointing down (Śakti), two lotus rings of eight and sixteen petals, three circles and a square of three lines. The guides to the Śrīcakra in this series take each part in turn.

📜 Looking deeper

The number forty-four is famous among commentators. The triangles make forty-three; counting the bindu, the central point, gives forty-four, or, as some say, the count includes other corners. Lakṣmīdhara and others discuss it at length. The verse shows that the poet knew the yantra exactly, and that the hymn is not only devotional but technical.

🌱 For today

A pattern that looks complicated is built from a few simple parts, carefully joined.

↑ VERSE 11 · The Śrīcakra, in four lines.

VERSE 12

BEAUTY BEYOND COMPARE
tvadīyaṃ saundaryaṃ tuhinagirikanye tulayituṃkavīndrāḥ kalpante kathamapi viriñciprabhṛtayaḥyadālokautsukyādamaralalanā yānti manasātapobhirduṣprāpāmapi giriśasāyujyapadavīm ‖12‖
To find a likeness for your beauty, O daughter of the snow mountain, the best poets, Brahmā and the rest, try somehow and fail. Celestial women, longing to see it, reach in their minds that union with Śiva which is hard to gain even by austerity.
WordMeans
saundaryambeauty
tulayitumto compare, to weigh
kavīndrāḥthe best of poets
amara-lalanāḥcelestial women
giriśa-sāyujya-padavīmthe state of union with Śiva
No poet can compare it.

VERSE 12 · DEEP DIVE

BEAUTY BEYOND COMPARE

🔎 In context

The theme of the whole second half of the hymn, her beauty, is announced here. Even Brahmā, the creator and first poet, cannot find anything to compare it with. The apsarases, the most beautiful women of heaven, long to see her and in that longing reach a state that sages win only by long penance.

📜 Looking deeper

There is a clever turn in the second half. To see Lalitā’s beauty fully, one would have to be Śiva, who alone sees her as she is. The celestial women, wanting that sight, become in their minds one with Śiva. Longing for beauty, rightly directed, leads straight to union.

🌱 For today

Some things can only be pointed at, never matched.

↑ VERSE 12 · No poet can compare it.

VERSE 13

ONE GLANCE
naraṃ varṣīyāṃsaṃ nayanavirasaṃ narmasu jaḍaṃtavāpāṅgāloke patitamanudhāvanti śataśaḥgaladveṇībandhāḥ kucakalaśavisrastasicayāhaṭhāt truṭyatkāñcyo vigalitadukūlā yuvatayaḥ ‖13‖
An old man, unpleasing to the eye and slow in the arts of love: once your side-glance falls on him, young women run after him in hundreds, their braids coming loose, their garments slipping, their girdles breaking in haste, their silks falling away.
WordMeans
naraṃ varṣīyāṃsaman old man
nayana-virasamunpleasing to the eye
tava apāṅga-ālokein the light of your side-glance
śataśaḥin hundreds
yuvatayaḥyoung women
Her glance makes anyone radiant.

VERSE 13 · DEEP DIVE

ONE GLANCE

🔎 In context

This verse belongs to a well-known kind of Sanskrit praise poetry: the god’s or goddess’s grace is shown by its most extravagant possible effect. Here the effect is charm. Even the least attractive of men, touched by her glance, becomes irresistible. The hymn’s many “fruit” verses work this way.

📜 Looking deeper

Commentators read it at two levels. On the surface it is an image of overwhelming attraction. Inwardly, the “old man” is anyone worn out and without charm in the world’s eyes; the Goddess’s glance makes such a person radiant. We give the plain sense and leave the image as the poet drew it.

🌱 For today

Grace changes how a person is seen, and how they see themselves.

↑ VERSE 13 · Her glance makes anyone radiant.

VERSE 14

ABOVE THE RAYS
kṣitau ṣaṭpañcāśad dvisamadhikapañcāśadudakehutāśe dvāṣaṣṭiścaturadhikapañcāśadaniledivi dviṣṣaṭtriṃśanmanasi ca catuṣṣaṣṭiriti yemayūkhāsteṣāmapyupari tava pādāmbujayugam ‖14‖
Fifty-six rays in earth, fifty-two in water, sixty-two in fire, fifty-four in air, seventy-two in space and sixty-four in mind: such are the rays, and above even these are your two lotus feet.
WordMeans
kṣitau ṣaṭpañcāśatfifty-six in earth
udake dvi-sam-adhika-pañcāśatfifty-two in water
hutāśe dvāṣaṣṭiḥsixty-two in fire
divi dvi-ṣaṭ-triṃśatseventy-two in space
mayūkhāḥrays
360 rays, and her feet above them.

VERSE 14 · DEEP DIVE

ABOVE THE RAYS

🔎 In context

Each of the six centres of verse 9 is given a number of rays. The six numbers add up to 360, the days of the ideal year. The verse thus joins the body, the elements and time in one scheme, and places the Goddess’s feet above all of them, in the thousand-petalled lotus.

📜 Looking deeper

The arithmetic is deliberate: 56 + 52 + 62 + 54 + 72 + 64 = 360. Lakṣmīdhara and others build long explanations on it, linking the rays to the days of the year and to the Goddess as the source of time. It is the same idea as the Tithi Nityās: time is a garland around the Goddess, and she is beyond it.

🌱 For today

Time and the body run on the same rhythms; something in us stands above both.

↑ VERSE 14 · 360 rays, and her feet above them.

VERSE 15

THE WHITE FORM
śarajjyotsnāśuddhāṃ śaśiyutajaṭājūṭamakuṭāṃvaratrāsatrāṇasphaṭikaghaṭikāpustakakarāmsakṛnna tvā natvā kathamiva satāṃ sannidadhatemadhukṣīradrākṣāmadhurimadhurīṇāḥ bhaṇitayaḥ ‖15‖
Pure as autumn moonlight, wearing the crescent moon in her crown of matted hair, her hands giving boons and fearlessness and holding a crystal rosary and a book: how could words sweet as honey, milk and grapes not come to the good who bow to you even once?
WordMeans
śaraj-jyotsnā-śuddhāmpure as autumn moonlight
vara-trāsa-trāṇaboon and protection from fear
sphaṭika-ghaṭikāa crystal rosary
pustakaa book
madhu-kṣīra-drākṣāhoney, milk and grapes
Her white form gives sweet speech.

VERSE 15 · DEEP DIVE

THE WHITE FORM

🔎 In context

Verses 15 to 17 describe forms of the Goddess to be meditated on for the gift of speech and poetry. This first is white, like Sarasvatī, with rosary and book. The promise is eloquence: words sweet as honey, milk and grapes, the three classic sweet tastes of Indian poetics.

📜 Looking deeper

The three sweets are sometimes matched to three styles of poetry: grape-like, easy to enjoy at once; milk-like, needing a little attention; and honey-like, deep. Whatever the reading, the verse links the Goddess with Sarasvatī, goddess of learning, and shows the Śrīvidyā view that all goddesses are forms of the one.

🌱 For today

Clear speech starts with a clear mind.

↑ VERSE 15 · Her white form gives sweet speech.

VERSE 16

THE DAWN-RED FORM
kavīndrāṇāṃ cetaḥkamalavanabālātaparuciṃbhajante ye santaḥ katicidaruṇāmeva bhavatīmviriñcipreyasyāstaruṇataraśṛṅgāralaharī-gabhīrābhirvāgbhirvidadhati satāṃ rañjanamamī ‖16‖
Those good people who worship you as red, the morning sun for the lotus-minds of great poets, delight the wise with words deep with waves of the young love of Sarasvatī, beloved of Brahmā.
WordMeans
aruṇāmred, dawn-coloured
cetaḥ-kamala-vana-bālātapathe morning sun for the lotus-forest of minds
viriñci-preyasīSarasvatī, Brahmā’s beloved

Her red form gives words of love.

VERSE 16 · DEEP DIVE

THE DAWN-RED FORM

🔎 In context

The second form is red, Lalitā’s own colour, the colour of dawn. As the rising sun opens lotuses, she opens the minds of poets. The fruit is again eloquence, this time with the flavour of śṛṅgāra, the mood of love, the first of the nine rasas.

📜 Looking deeper

Aruṇā, the red one, is one of Lalitā’s best-known names; the Sahasranāma opens its description of her with redness. Linking her red form to Sarasvatī’s love suggests that the deepest poetry is not cold learning but learning warmed by love.

🌱 For today

Warmth makes knowledge persuasive.

↑ VERSE 16 · Her red form gives words of love.

VERSE 17

WITH THE GODDESSES OF SPEECH
savitrībhirvācāṃ śaśimaṇiśilābhaṅgarucibhiḥvaśinyādyābhistvāṃ saha janani sañcintayati yaḥsa kartā kāvyānāṃ bhavati mahatāṃ bhaṅgirucibhiḥvacobhirvāgdevīvadanakamalāmodamadhuraiḥ ‖17‖
Whoever meditates on you, O Mother, together with Vaśinī and the others, the mothers of speech, bright as fragments of moonstone, becomes the author of great poems, with words full of graceful turns and fragrant as the lotus face of the Goddess of Speech.
WordMeans
vacāṃ savitrībhiḥwith the mothers of speech
śaśi-maṇi-śilāmoonstone
vaśinī-ādyābhiḥwith Vaśinī and the rest

VERSE 17 · DEEP DIVE

WITH THE GODDESSES OF SPEECH

🔎 In context

Vaśinī and the others are the eight Vāgdevīs, goddesses of speech, who in the Lalitopākhyāna composed the Lalitā Sahasranāma at the Goddess’s command. On the Śrīcakra they occupy the seventh enclosure, the eight triangles around the centre. The guide to that enclosure in this series names all eight.

📜 Looking deeper

This verse ties the hymn to the rest of Śrīvidyā: the same eight goddesses who gave the Sahasranāma are here invoked for the gift of poetry. To meditate on the Goddess with her poets around her is, the verse says, to become a poet oneself.

🌱 For today

Keep good company, even in the imagination; it shapes what you become.

↑ VERSE 17 · With the Vāgdevīs, poetry flows.

VERSE 18

BATHED IN RED
tanucchāyābhiste taruṇataraṇiśrīsaraṇibhiḥdivaṃ sarvāmurvīmaruṇimani magnāṃ smarati yaḥbhavantyasya trasyadvanahariṇaśālīnanayanāḥsahorvaśyā vaśyāḥ kati kati na gīrvāṇagaṇikāḥ ‖18‖
Whoever remembers sky and the whole earth plunged in red by the radiance of your body, the splendour of the young sun: how many celestial courtesans, with Urvaśī herself, with eyes shy as a frightened forest deer, do not come under his sway?
WordMeans
tanu-chāyābhiḥby the radiance of your body
taruṇa-taraṇithe young (rising) sun
aruṇimani magnāmplunged in redness
urvaśīUrvaśī, the most famous apsaras

VERSE 18 · DEEP DIVE

BATHED IN RED

🔎 In context

The meditation here is vast: the whole universe, sky and earth, flooded with the red light of the Goddess’s body. The fruit is described, as often in this hymn, as the power to charm; Urvaśī, the most celebrated nymph of heaven, is named as the example.

📜 Looking deeper

Inwardly the image is of a world seen entirely in the Goddess’s colour, nothing left outside her. The charm promised is a sign of that: one who sees everything bathed in her light becomes, in turn, magnetic to others. The verse is among the many that tradition attaches to particular aims; we give only its sense.

🌱 For today

What you see everywhere, you start to carry with you.

↑ VERSE 18 · The whole world in her colour.

VERSE 19

MANMATHA’S DIGIT
mukhaṃ binduṃ kṛtvā kucayugamadhastasya tadadhoharārdhaṃ dhyāyedyo haramahiṣi te manmathakalāmsa sadyaḥ saṅkṣobhaṃ nayati vanitā ityatilaghutrilokīmapyāśu bhramayati ravīndustanayugām ‖19‖
Whoever meditates on your form as the digit of Manmatha, O queen of Hara, making the face the bindu, the two breasts below it, and below them the half of Hara: he at once unsettles women, and quickly sets swirling even the three worlds, whose breasts are the sun and the moon.
WordMeans
mukhaṃ binduṃ kṛtvāmaking the face the bindu

manmatha-kalāmthe digit (form) of Manmatha

A symbol of the Goddess, set in a verse.

VERSE 19 · DEEP DIVE

MANMATHA’S DIGIT

🔎 In context

Commentators read this verse as describing kāmakalā, a symbolic diagram of the Goddess built from a point, two points and a triangle, which is central to Śrīvidyā’s inner teaching. The details of that meditation are given at initiation. We give only the plain sense of the words.

📜 Looking deeper

The fruit, as in verses 13 and 18, is an overwhelming power to stir and attract, extended here to the whole universe, pictured as a woman whose breasts are the sun and the moon. The verse is a good example of why the Saundaryalaharī has always been read at more than one level, by poets for its images and by practitioners for its teaching.

🌱 For today

Some verses are meant to be understood slowly, with a teacher; it is enough to know that.

↑ VERSE 19 · A symbol of the Goddess, set in a verse.

VERSE 20

THE MOONSTONE IMAGE
kirantīmaṅgebhyaḥ kiraṇanikurambāmṛtarasaṃhṛdi tvāmādhatte himakaraśilāmūrtimiva yaḥsa sarpāṇāṃ darpaṃ śamayati śakuntādhipa ivajvarapluṣṭān dṛṣṭyā sukhayati sudhādhārasirayā ‖20‖
Whoever holds you in the heart like an image of moonstone, raining from your limbs the nectar of masses of rays, he, like the king of birds, quells the pride of serpents, and with a glance that flows with nectar soothes those burning with fever.
WordMeans

VERSE 20 · DEEP DIVE

THE MOONSTONE IMAGE

🔎 In context

The last verse of this part pictures the Goddess as cool and healing: a moonstone image raining nectar. The fruit is protection and healing: power over snake poison, like Garuḍa, enemy of serpents, and the power to cool fever with a glance. Tradition attaches this verse especially to healing.

📜 Looking deeper

After the red and fiery forms of the previous verses, this one is moonlike and cool. The Goddess is both: the dawn that sets the world aglow and the moon that soothes. The Śrīvidyā tradition pictures her as containing sun, moon and fire, and the hymn moves among them freely.

🌱 For today

Calm, held in the heart, cools the people around you too.

↑ VERSE 20 · A cool image that heals.

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

Part 1 in five lines
1–4. Śiva cannot stir without Śakti; the dust of her feet makes, holds and ends the worlds; her feet alone protect and give.
5–8. Mohinī and Kāma draw their power from her; she stands with bow, arrows, noose and goad, on her island of gems.
9–11. She rises through the six centres to union at the crown and returns as nectar; the Śrīcakra is her house.
12–14. No poet can match her beauty; her glance makes anyone radiant; her feet stand above the 360 rays.
15–20. White, red and moonstone forms, with the goddesses of speech: meditations that give eloquence, charm and healing.
