ŚRĪ SAUNDARYALAHARĪ · PART 3 OF 5 · VERSES 42–60

SAUNDARYALAHARĪ

The wave of beauty · from her crown to her ears

HYMNS OF THE GODDESS

HOW THIS WORKS: All nineteen verses in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive into the ideas and stories behind it.

WHERE WE ARE

With verse 42 the Saundaryalaharī proper begins: the second half of the hymn, which describes the Goddess’s beauty from her crown down to her feet. Part 3 covers her crown, hair, the parting of her hair, her face, forehead, brows, eyes and ears.

The poetry here is at its most playful. Almost every verse is an utprekṣā, “I imagine that…”: her brows are Kāma’s bow, her eyes are eight cities or three rivers, her face is a chariot. Under the play run the hymn’s serious themes: her eyes create and protect the world, and her glance falls on everyone like moonlight.

Verse 44 gives the hymn its name, saundaryalaharī, the wave of beauty of her face. As before, the yantras and uses traditionally attached to each verse are left out.

VERSE 42

THE CROWN OF GEMS
gatairmāṇikyatvaṃ gaganamaṇibhiḥ sāndraghaṭitaṃkirīṭaṃ te haimaṃ himagirisute kīrtayati yaḥsa nīḍeyacchāyācchuraṇaśabalaṃ candraśakalaṃdhanuḥ śaunāsīraṃ kimiti na nibadhnāti dhiṣaṇām ‖42‖
O daughter of the snow mountain, whoever describes your golden crown, thickly set with the sky’s jewels, the suns, turned into rubies, will he not imagine the crescent moon on it, mottled by the play of their light, to be Indra’s rainbow?
WordMeans
gagana-maṇibhiḥwith the jewels of the sky: the suns
māṇikyatvaṃ gataiḥthat have become rubies
kirīṭaṃ haimamgolden crown
candra-śakalamthe crescent moon
dhanuḥ śaunāsīramIndra’s bow: the rainbow
From the crown downwards.

VERSE 42 · DEEP DIVE

THE CROWN OF GEMS

🔎 In context

With verse 42 the hymn turns from teaching to description. The second part, the Saundaryalaharī proper, praises the Goddess from crown to feet, verse by verse. It begins, naturally, at the top: her golden crown, its gems the twelve suns of Indian cosmology, turned into rubies.

📜 Looking deeper

The image is layered. The crescent moon on her crown, catching the many colours of the ruby-suns, looks like a rainbow. Poets of this kind of hymn delight in such fanciful explanations (utprekṣā, “I imagine that…”). The whole second half is full of them.

🌱 For today

Look closely at something you love; there is always more to see.

↑ VERSE 42 · From the crown downwards.

VERSE 43

HER HAIR
dhunotu dhvāntaṃ nastulitadalitendīvaravanaṃghanasnigdhaślakṣṇaṃ cikuranikurambaṃ tava śiveyadīyaṃ saurabhyaṃ sahajamupalabdhuṃ sumanasovasantyasmin manye valamathanavāṭīviṭapinām ‖43‖
May your mass of hair, dense, glossy and soft, like a forest of opening blue water-lilies, drive away our darkness, O Śivā. To share its natural fragrance, I think, the flowers of the trees in Indra’s garden come to live in it.
WordMeans
dhunotu dhvāntammay it shake off darkness
dalita-indīvara-vanama forest of opening blue lilies
cikura-nikurambammass of hair
sahajaṃ saurabhyamnatural fragrance
valamathana-vāṭī-viṭapināmof the trees in Indra’s garden
Her dark hair drives away darkness.

VERSE 43 · DEEP DIVE

HER HAIR

🔎 In context

The flowers in her hair, the poet says, are not there to perfume it. They come from the heavenly trees of Indra’s garden to borrow her hair’s own natural fragrance. It is a classic reversal: what usually adorns her is honoured by her.

📜 Looking deeper

The prayer is that her dark hair should drive away our darkness: a paradox the poet enjoys. Commentators note a debate in Indian poetics about whether women’s hair has a natural fragrance; this verse sides firmly with “yes”, at least for the Goddess.

🌱 For today

What is truly fine needs no perfume; it lends its own.

↑ VERSE 43 · Her dark hair drives away darkness.

VERSE 44

THE PARTING IN HER HAIR
tanotu kṣemaṃ nastava vadanasaundaryalaharī-parīvāhasrotaḥsaraṇiriva sīmantasaraṇiḥvahantī sindūraṃ prabalakabarībhāratimira-dviṣāṃ bṛndairbandīkṛtamiva navīnārkakiraṇam ‖44‖
May the parting of your hair bring us well-being: like a channel for the overflowing stream of the wave of beauty of your face, it carries sindūra, like the rays of the new sun held captive by the hosts of darkness of your thick braid.
WordMeans
sīmanta-saraṇiḥthe line of the parting
vadana-saundarya-laharīthe wave of beauty of your face
sindūramred powder in the parting
kabarī-bhāra-timirathe darkness of your heavy braid
navīna-arka-kiraṇamthe rays of the new sun
Here is the wave of beauty.

VERSE 44 · DEEP DIVE

THE PARTING IN HER HAIR

🔎 In context

This verse gives the hymn its name. The phrase “wave of beauty” (saundaryalaharī) appears here: the beauty of her face is a flood, and the parting of her hair is its overflow channel. In it lies the red sindūra worn by married women.

📜 Looking deeper

The second image is a small war: her dark hair is an army of darkness that has taken the red rays of the rising sun prisoner. Sindūra in the parting is a sign of a married woman whose husband lives, so the verse quietly repeats the theme of verse 28.

🌱 For today

Small signs can carry a great deal of meaning.

↑ VERSE 44 · Here is the wave of beauty.

VERSE 45

FACE AND CURLS
arālaiḥ svābhāvyādalikalabhasaśrībhiralakaiḥparītaṃ te vaktraṃ parihasati paṅkeruharucimdarasmere yasmin daśanarucikiñjalkaruciresugandhau mādyanti smaradahanacakṣurmadhulihaḥ ‖45‖
Your face, ringed by curls naturally curved and as lovely as young bees, mocks the beauty of the lotus. In it, gently smiling, bright with the filaments of your shining teeth, fragrant, the bees that are the eyes of Śiva, burner of Kāma, grow drunk.
WordMeans
alakacurls
ali-kalabhayoung bees
parihasati paṅkeruha-rucimmocks the beauty of the lotus
daśana-ruci-kiñjalkateeth shining like lotus filaments
smara-dahana-cakṣuḥthe eyes of Śiva, burner of Kāma
Her face outshines the lotus.

VERSE 45 · DEEP DIVE

FACE AND CURLS

🔎 In context

The comparison of face and lotus is the commonest in Indian poetry, and the poet plays with every part of it: her curls are bees, her teeth the lotus filaments, her smile its opening. Then the twist: the bees drinking at this lotus are Śiva’s eyes.

📜 Looking deeper

The irony is gentle. Śiva burned Kāma, the god of desire, yet his own eyes are intoxicated by her face. The tradition sees no contradiction: the love between Śiva and Śakti is not desire in the ordinary sense, but the source of all attraction.

🌱 For today

Even the most austere can be moved by true beauty.

↑ VERSE 45 · Her face outshines the lotus.

VERSE 46

HER FOREHEAD
lalāṭaṃ lāvaṇyadyutivimalamābhāti tava ya-ddvitīyaṃ tanmanye makuṭaghaṭitaṃ candraśakalamviparyāsanyāsādubhayamapi sambhūya ca mithaḥsudhālepasyūtiḥ pariṇamati rākāhimakaraḥ ‖46‖
Your forehead shines, pure with the radiance of beauty. I think it is a second crescent moon, besides the one set in your crown; the two, placed upside down and joined together, would become the full moon, sealed with nectar.
WordMeans
lalāṭamforehead
lāvaṇya-dyuti-vimalampure with the light of beauty
makuṭa-ghaṭitaṃ candra-śakalamthe crescent set in the crown
viparyāsa-nyāsātby placing one inverted
rākā-himakaraḥthe full moon
Two crescents make a full moon.

VERSE 46 · DEEP DIVE

HER FOREHEAD

🔎 In context

A charming bit of geometry. The crescent on her crown and the curve of her forehead are two halves of a moon. Put one upside down against the other and you have the full moon, its joint sealed with nectar.

📜 Looking deeper

The full moon recurs throughout Śrīvidyā: Lalitā is the sixteenth digit that never wanes, and Citrā, the full-moon Nityā, is her last companion. The poet’s fancy fits the tradition’s deepest image of her.

🌱 For today

Two halves, rightly placed, make a whole.

↑ VERSE 46 · Two crescents make a full moon.

VERSE 47

HER BROWS
bhruvau bhugne kiñcidbhuvanabhayabhaṅgavyasaninitvadīye netrābhyāṃ madhukararucibhyāṃ dhṛtaguṇamdhanurmanye savyetarakaragṛhītaṃ ratipateḥprakoṣṭhe muṣṭau ca sthagayati nigūḍhāntaramume ‖47‖
Your brows, slightly curved, intent on breaking the fears of the world, with your eyes like a string of bees: I think they are the bow of Kāma, held in his left hand, its middle hidden by his wrist and fist, O Umā.
WordMeans
bhruvau bhugnethe curved brows
bhuvana-bhaya-bhaṅga-vyasaninieager to break the world’s fear
madhukara-rucibhyām(eyes) dark as bees
ratipateḥ dhanuḥthe bow of Kāma, Rati’s lord
nigūḍha-antaramwith its middle hidden
Her brows: Kāma’s bow.

VERSE 47 · DEEP DIVE

HER BROWS

🔎 In context

Kāma’s bow, the poet recalls from verse 6, has a string made of bees. Her two brows are that bow; her dark eyes the bees on its string; and the gap between the brows is where Kāma’s fist and wrist hide the middle of the bow as he holds it.

📜 Looking deeper

The phrase “eager to break the world’s fear” turns the image from charm to protection. Her brows are a weapon, but a merciful one, aimed at fear itself. The Goddess as protector and the Goddess as beauty are never separate in this hymn.

🌱 For today

Even beauty can be a weapon against fear.

↑ VERSE 47 · Her brows: Kāma’s bow.

VERSE 48

SUN, MOON AND FIRE
ahaḥ sūte savyaṃ tava nayanamarkātmakatayātriyāmāṃ vāmaṃ te sṛjati rajanīnāyakatayātṛtīyā te dṛṣṭirdaradalitahemāmbujaruciḥsamādhatte sandhyāṃ divasaniśayorantaracarīm ‖48‖
Your right eye, being the sun, gives birth to the day; your left, being the moon, creates the night; and your third eye, like a slightly opened golden lotus, makes the twilight that moves between day and night.
WordMeans
savyaṃ nayanam arkātmakatayāthe right eye, being the sun
triyāmāmthe night
rajanī-nāyakatayābeing the lord of night, the moon
tṛtīyā dṛṣṭiḥthe third eye
sandhyāmtwilight
Her eyes make day, night and twilight.

VERSE 48 · DEEP DIVE

SUN, MOON AND FIRE

🔎 In context

The Goddess has three eyes, like Śiva, and they are the three lights of Śrīvidyā: sun, moon and fire. Her eyes make time itself: day, night and the two twilights between them.

📜 Looking deeper

The twilights, dawn and dusk, are the sandhyā, the junctions at which daily Vedic worship is performed. The third eye, the eye of fire, governs them. This verse is often quoted to show that in Śrīvidyā the Goddess does not live in time; she produces it with her glance.

🌱 For today

Day, night and the hours between are all given; use them well.

↑ VERSE 48 · Her eyes make day, night and twilight.

VERSE 49

EIGHT CITIES IN A GLANCE
viśālā kalyāṇī sphuṭarucirayodhyā kuvalayaiḥkṛpādhārādhārā kimapi madhurābhogavatikāavantī dṛṣṭiste bahunagaravistāravijayādhruvaṃ tattannāmavyavaharaṇayogyā vijayate ‖49‖
Your glance is wide (Viśālā), auspicious (Kalyāṇī), unconquerable by blue lilies in its radiance (Ayodhyā), a stream of mercy (Dhārā), sweet (Madhurā), of vast reach (Bhogavatī), protecting (Avantī), victorious (Vijayā): it surely deserves to be called by the names of all these great cities.
WordMeans
viśālā / kalyāṇīwide / auspicious: Viśālā, Kalyāṇī
ayodhyāunconquerable: Ayodhyā
dhārā / madhurāstream / sweet: Dhārā, Madhurā (Mathurā)
bhogavatī / avantīexpansive / protecting: Bhogavatī, Avantī
vijayāvictorious: Vijayā
Eight great cities in one glance.

VERSE 49 · DEEP DIVE

EIGHT CITIES IN A GLANCE

🔎 In context

A famous verse of word-play. Each adjective describing her glance is also the name of an ancient city: Viśālā (Ujjain’s old name), Kalyāṇī, Ayodhyā, Dhārā, Madhurā (Mathurā), Bhogavatī, Avantī and Vijayā. Her glance deserves to be called by the names of all of them.

📜 Looking deeper

The game works only in Sanskrit, but the idea carries across. Every quality of a great city, wealth, safety, beauty, victory, is in her glance. Commentators sometimes read the eight cities as eight states of the eye in different moods.

🌱 For today

A single glance can hold a whole world of qualities.

VERSE 50

EYES AND EARS
kavīnāṃ sandarbhastabakamakarandaikarasikaṃkaṭākṣavyākṣepabhramarakalabhau karṇayugalamamuñcantau dṛṣṭvā tava navarasāsvādataralā-vasūyāsaṃsargādalikanayanaṃ kiñcidaruṇam ‖50‖
Your two ears, delighting only in the nectar of the flower-clusters of poets’ compositions, are not left by your two eyes, which are like young bees darting in side-glances. Seeing them, your third eye, eager to taste the nine rasas, has grown a little red, as if from jealousy.
WordMeans
kavīnāṃ sandarbhathe compositions of poets
kaṭākṣa-vyākṣepathe darting of side-glances
karṇa-yugalamthe pair of ears
nava-rasa-āsvādatasting the nine rasas
alika-nayanamthe eye on the forehead
Even her eyes love poetry.

VERSE 50 · DEEP DIVE

EYES AND EARS

🔎 In context

Her eyes are so long they reach her ears, a mark of beauty in Indian poetry. The poet explains why: the eyes, like bees, are drawn to the ears, which are always full of the honey of poetry. The third eye, left out, has gone a little red with envy.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse is a compliment to poets as much as to the Goddess. The Goddess listens to poetry with delight, and the poet’s own hymn is part of that honey. Commentators note that the red tinge of the third eye was a known feature in descriptions of her.

🌱 For today

Listen with your whole attention, and the eyes will follow.

↑ VERSE 50 · Even her eyes love poetry.

VERSE 51

NINE MOODS IN ONE GLANCE
śive śṛṅgārārdrā taditarajane kutsanaparāsaroṣā gaṅgāyāṃ giriśacarite vismayavatīharāhibhyo bhītā sarasiruhasaubhāgyajananīsakhīṣu smerā te mayi jananī dṛṣṭiḥ sakaruṇā ‖51‖
Your glance, Mother, is soft with love towards Śiva, scornful towards other men, angry towards Gaṅgā, full of wonder at Śiva’s deeds, frightened of Hara’s serpents, triumphant over the beauty of the lotus, smiling at your friends, and full of compassion towards me.
WordMeans
śṛṅgāra-ārdrāmoist with love (śṛṅgāra)
kutsana-parāfull of disdain (bībhatsa)
saroṣā gaṅgāyāmangry at Gaṅgā (raudra)
vismayavatīfull of wonder (adbhuta)
mayi sakaruṇācompassionate to me (karuṇa)
Nine moods, ending in compassion for me.

VERSE 51 · DEEP DIVE

NINE MOODS IN ONE GLANCE

🔎 In context

Indian aesthetics names the rasas, the basic moods of art: love, disgust, anger, wonder, fear, heroism, laughter, compassion and, in some lists, peace. This verse shows her glance passing through them in turn, each with its object.

📜 Looking deeper

The anger at Gaṅgā is a playful touch: Gaṅgā sits in Śiva’s hair, a rival. The fear is of the snakes Śiva wears. The heroism (some texts read jayinī, “victorious”) is over the lotus. And the poet keeps the best for last: towards me, compassion. It is the turn every devotee hopes for.

🌱 For today

Of all the moods, the one we most need is the last: compassion.

ARROWS DRAWN TO THE EAR

🔎 In context

In Indian archery an arrow is drawn back to the ear before release. Her long eyes, reaching to her ears, with lashes like the feathers at an arrow’s end, look like Kāma’s arrows at full draw. Their target is Śiva’s meditation.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse carries the story of Kāma forward. When Kāma himself shot at Śiva, he was burned. Her eyes succeed where Kāma failed, because they are hers. The Goddess, not the god of love, is the real power of attraction in the world.

🌱 For today

What force cannot do, a gentle look sometimes can.

↑ VERSE 52 · Her eyes: Kāma’s arrows, drawn.

VERSE 53

THREE COLOURS, THREE GUṆAS
vibhaktatraivarṇyaṃ vyatikaritalīlāñjanatayāvibhāti tvannetratritayamidamīśānadayitepunaḥ sraṣṭuṃ devān druhiṇaharirudrānuparatānrajaḥ sattvaṃ bibhrattama iti guṇānāṃ trayamiva ‖53‖
Your three eyes, O beloved of Īśāna, shine with three distinct colours from the collyrium playfully applied, as if holding the three guṇas, rajas, sattva and tamas, in order to create again the gods Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Rudra, who had come to rest.

VERSE 53 · DEEP DIVE

THREE COLOURS, THREE GUṆAS

🔎 In context

Her eyes are red, white and black, from the kohl at their edges. The poet sees in them the three guṇas: rajas (red, activity), sattva (white, clarity), tamas (black, inertia). From these she will create again the three gods who dissolved in verse 26.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse connects her glance with the whole cycle of creation. The gods end at the great dissolution; her eyes, holding the three qualities of nature, bring them back. Creation is the Goddess opening her eyes, a theme verse 55 develops.

🌱 For today

Every new beginning holds the old qualities, renewed.

↑ VERSE 53 · Three colours, three guṇas.

VERSE 54

THREE RIVERS
pavitrīkartuṃ naḥ paśupatiparādhīnahṛdayedayāmitrairnetrairaruṇadhavalaśyāmarucibhiḥnadaḥ śoṇo gaṅgā tapanatanayeti dhruvamamuṃtrayāṇāṃ tīrthānāmupanayasi sambhedamanagham ‖54‖
O you whose heart is given to Paśupati, to purify us, with your eyes that are friends of mercy, red, white and dark in colour, you surely bring together the flawless confluence of three sacred rivers: the Śoṇa, the Gaṅgā and the Yamunā, daughter of the sun.
WordMeans

Her glance: a sacred confluence.

VERSE 54 · DEEP DIVE

THREE RIVERS

🔎 In context

The three colours of verse 53 become three rivers: the red Śoṇa, the white Gaṅgā and the dark Yamunā. A confluence of rivers, a saṅgama such as Prayāga, is among the holiest places for bathing. Her eyes, the poet says, are such a confluence.

📜 Looking deeper

To be looked at by her is to bathe at the holiest place on earth. The verse turns a pilgrimage into a glance. Commentators enjoy the precision: three colours, three rivers, three guṇas, three eyes, all one compassionate look.

🌱 For today

A kind look can cleanse more than a long journey.

↑ VERSE 54 · Her glance: a sacred confluence.

VERSE 55

EYES THAT NEVER CLOSE
nimeṣonmeṣābhyāṃ pralayamudayaṃ yāti jagatītavetyāhuḥ santo dharaṇidhararājanyatanayetvadunmeṣājjātaṃ jagadidamaśeṣaṃ pralayataḥparitrātuṃ śaṅke parihṛtanimeṣāstava dṛśaḥ ‖55‖
The wise say that the world dissolves and arises with the closing and opening of your eyes, O daughter of the mountain king. Since this whole world was born from your eyes opening, I suspect your eyes never close, to save it from dissolution.
WordMeans
nimeṣa-unmeṣābhyāmby closing and opening (the eyes)
pralayam udayamdissolution and arising
dharaṇidhara-rājanya-tanayeO daughter of the mountain king
parihṛta-nimeṣāḥwho have given up blinking
paritrātumto protect
She never closes her eyes.

VERSE 55 · DEEP DIVE

EYES THAT NEVER CLOSE

🔎 In context

Creation as the opening of the Goddess’s eyes and dissolution as their closing is an old idea, picked up from verse 53. The poet draws a tender conclusion: she must never blink, so that the world she loves will not end.

📜 Looking deeper

Indian lore says the gods do not blink; animeṣa, “unblinking”, is a name for them. The poet gives the Goddess’s unblinking gaze a reason: care. She keeps her eyes open because if she closed them, even for a moment, we would be gone.

🌱 For today

Care sometimes means not looking away.

↑ VERSE 55 · She never closes her eyes.

VERSE 56

THE FISH AND THE LILY
tavāparṇe karṇejapanayanapaiśunyacakitānilīyante toye niyatamanimeṣāḥ śapharikāḥiyaṃ ca śrīrbaddhacchadapuṭakavāṭaṃ kuvalayamjahāti pratyūṣe niśi ca vighaṭayya praviśati ‖56‖
O Aparṇā, afraid that your eyes, reaching to your ears, will whisper tales about them, the little fish hide in the water, never blinking. And Lakṣmī, the beauty of your eyes, leaves the blue lily at dawn, its petals shut like doors, and enters it again at night, opening them.
WordMeans
aparṇeO Aparṇā, “leafless” (Pārvatī)
karṇejapawhispering in the ear, telling tales
śapharikāḥlittle fish
śrīḥbeauty, Lakṣmī
kuvalayamthe blue water-lily
Fish and lilies, outshone.

VERSE 56 · DEEP DIVE

THE FISH AND THE LILY

🔎 In context

Aparṇā, “she who did without even leaves”, is Pārvatī’s name from her austerities to win Śiva. Fish-shaped eyes are a classic mark of beauty, and the fish, outdone, hide underwater in fear that her eyes, which reach her ears, will gossip about them. That, the poet says, is why fish never blink.

📜 Looking deeper

The second half explains why the blue lily closes in the day and opens at night: its beauty leaves it at dawn to dwell in her eyes, and returns at night when she sleeps. Two little nature-myths in one verse, both in honour of her eyes.

🌱 For today

Delight in small things is part of devotion too.

↑ VERSE 56 · Fish and lilies, outshone.

VERSE 57

BATHE ME IN YOUR GLANCE
dṛśā drāghīyasyā daradalitanīlotpalarucādavīyāṃsaṃ dīnaṃ snapaya kṛpayā māmapi śiveanenāyaṃ dhanyo bhavati na ca te hāniriyatāvane vā harmye vā samakaranipāto himakaraḥ ‖57‖
With your long glance, bright as a blue lily just opening, bathe me too in mercy, O Śivā, poor and far away as I am. By this I will be blessed, and it costs you nothing: the moon sheds its rays equally on the forest and on the palace.
WordMeans

VERSE 57 · DEEP DIVE

BATHE ME IN YOUR GLANCE

🔎 In context

Here the poet speaks for himself, and for every reader. Her glance is long enough to reach even someone far away and poor. He asks only to be bathed in it, adding a gentle argument: it will cost her nothing.

📜 Looking deeper

The last line is one of the most loved in the hymn. The moon shines equally on forest and palace; it does not choose. So too her grace. Whatever our station, we are within reach of her glance. It is a verse many devotees recite as a personal prayer.

🌱 For today

Grace is like moonlight; it does not ask who you are.

↑ VERSE 57 · The moon shines on forest and palace alike.

VERSE 58

HER TEMPLES
arālaṃ te pālīyugalamagarājanyatanayena keṣāmādhatte kusumaśarakodaṇḍakutukamtiraścīno yatra śravaṇapathamullaṅghya vilasa-nnapāṅgavyāsaṅgo diśati śarasandhānadhiṣaṇām ‖58‖
O daughter of the mountain king, whose mind does the curved pair of your temples not fill with the idea of Kāma’s flowery bow? There, your side-glance, crossing the path of the ear and shining, makes one think of an arrow fitted to the string.
WordMeans

VERSE 58 · DEEP DIVE

HER TEMPLES

🔎 In context

The archery images continue. The curve of her temples, between eye and ear, is Kāma’s bow; her side-glance, passing towards the ear, is the arrow being fitted to it. A whole archer’s stance is drawn on her face.

📜 Looking deeper

This group of verses, 47 to 58, shows how far the poet will go in pursuing one comparison. Brows, eyes, lashes and temples become bow, bees, arrows and string. The effect is of a face that is itself the weapon of love, wielded by the Goddess, not by Kāma.

🌱 For today

Pursue one good idea fully, and it opens up.

↑ VERSE 58 · Her temples: a bow ready to shoot.

VERSE 59

A CHARIOT FOR KĀMA
sphuradgaṇḍābhogapratiphalitatāṭaṅkayugalaṃcatuścakraṃ manye tava mukhamidaṃ manmatharathamyamāruhya druhyatyavanirathamarkenducaraṇaṃmahāvīro māraḥ pramathapataye sajjitavate ‖59‖
Your face, with the pair of earrings reflected on the gleaming expanse of your cheeks, I take to be Kāma’s four-wheeled chariot. Mounted on it, the great hero Māra challenges Śiva, lord of the pramathas, who had readied the earth as his chariot, with the sun and moon as its wheels.
WordMeans

VERSE 59 · DEEP DIVE

A CHARIOT FOR KĀMA

🔎 In context

When Śiva went to destroy the three cities, the earth became his chariot and the sun and moon its two wheels. The poet counts: her two earrings and their two reflections in her cheeks make four wheels. Kāma, riding her face, has a chariot with twice as many wheels as Śiva’s.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse is pure play, but it repeats the hymn’s central claim: love’s power, through her, is greater than any god’s. It also mentions the tāṭaṅka, the earrings, again, as in verse 28.

🌱 For today

Playfulness and reverence can live in the same breath.

↑ VERSE 59 · Her face: Kāma’s chariot.

VERSE 60

EARRINGS THAT APPLAUD
sarasvatyāḥ sūktīramṛtalaharīkauśalaharīḥpibantyāḥ śarvāṇi śravaṇaculukābhyāmaviralamcamatkāraślāghācalitaśirasaḥ kuṇḍalagaṇojhaṇatkāraistāraiḥ prativacanamācaṣṭa iva te ‖60‖
As you drink, O Śarvāṇī, through the cups of your ears, the waves of skill of the nectar-sweet verses of Sarasvatī, without pause, your earrings, set swinging by your head nodding in delighted praise, seem to answer her with their high tinkling.

VERSE 60 · DEEP DIVE

EARRINGS THAT APPLAUD

🔎 In context

Sarasvatī, goddess of learning, recites poetry to the Goddess. Lalitā drinks it in through her ears, nodding in appreciation, and her earrings tinkle as she nods, as if they too were praising. It is a scene of a perfect audience.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse closes this part with a picture that suits the whole hymn: the Goddess listening to poetry with delight. The poet hopes, of course, that his own verses are among those she nods at. Part 4 continues the description downwards from her face.

🌱 For today

Appreciation is a gift too. Let those who do good work know it.

↑ VERSE 60 · Her earrings applaud the poetry.

THE HYMN ON ONE PAGE

Part 3 in five lines
42–44. A crown of ruby suns, hair like a forest of lilies, and the parting that carries the wave of beauty.
45–48. A face that outshines the lotus, a forehead that completes the moon, brows like Kāma’s bow, eyes that make day, night and twilight.
49–53. Eight cities, nine moods and three guṇas in her eyes; lashes like arrows drawn to the ear.
