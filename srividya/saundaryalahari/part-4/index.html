HOW THIS WORKS: All twenty verses in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive into the ideas and stories behind it.

WHERE WE ARE

Part 4 continues the head-to-foot description of the Goddess that began at verse 42. It moves from her nose and lips, smile, tongue and voice, to her chin, neck and arms, and then to the Goddess as Mother, nursing Skanda and Gaṇeśa.

Verse 75 is among the most discussed in the hymn: the Draviḍa child who drank her milk and became a great poet. Some say the poet means himself, Śaṅkara; others, the Tamil saint Tirujñānasambandar. We give both views.

Some of these verses describe the Goddess’s body with the frankness of classical Sanskrit poetry. We render them plainly, in the devotional spirit in which they are recited. As before, the yantras and uses traditionally attached to each verse are left out.

ZAPPY: Who was the Draviḍa child, really?

PROF. BOLT: Nobody can prove it either way. Tradition says Śaṅkara; many scholars say Sambandar. The verse’s point stands either way: poetry is her gift.

VERSE 61

HER NOSE AND ITS PEARL
asau nāsāvaṃśastuhinagirivaṃśadhvajapaṭitvadīyo nedīyaḥ phalatu phalamasmākamucitamvahannantarmuktāḥ śiśirataraniśvāsagalitaṃsamṛddhyā yattāsāṃ bahirapi ca muktāmaṇidharaḥ ‖61‖
O banner of the Himalayan line, may your nose, like a slender bamboo, grant us, who are close to you, the fruit we deserve. It holds pearls within, and, from their abundance, wears outside too a pearl, carried out by your cool breath.
WordMeans
nāsā-vaṃśaḥthe bridge of the nose, like a bamboo
tuhina-giri-vaṃśa-dhvaja-paṭiO banner of the Himalayan lineage
antar muktāḥpearls within
śiśiratara-niśvāsavery cool breath
muktā-maṇi-dharaḥwearing a pearl
A pearl carried on her breath.

VERSE 61 · DEEP DIVE

HER NOSE AND ITS PEARL

🔎 In context

Indian lore holds that some bamboos contain pearls. The poet uses this: her nose, straight as a bamboo, has pearls inside, and the pearl of her nose-ring is one of them, carried out on her breath. The verse also puns on vaṃśa, which means both bamboo and family line.

📜 Looking deeper

A small request sits in the middle of the description: “may it grant us the fruit we deserve”. Bamboo bears fruit rarely, so the request is pointed. Even in the most decorative verses, the devotee keeps asking.

🌱 For today

Ask simply for what is right for you; it is enough.

↑ VERSE 61 · A pearl carried on her breath.

VERSE 62

HER LIPS
prakṛtyā raktāyāstava sudati dantacchadaruceḥpravakṣye sādṛśyaṃ janayatu phalaṃ vidrumalatāna bimbaṃ tadbimbapratiphalanarāgādaruṇitaṃtulāmadhyāroḍhuṃ kathamiva vilajjeta kalayā ‖62‖
O you of lovely teeth, I shall describe a likeness for the glow of your naturally red lips. Let the coral creeper bear a fruit; that might do. But the bimba fruit, reddened only by reflecting your lips, how could it not be ashamed to be weighed against them even in part?
WordMeans
sudatiO you of lovely teeth
danta-cchada“covering of the teeth”: the lips
vidruma-latācoral creeper
bimbamthe red bimba fruit
pratiphalana-rāgātred from reflecting (your lips)
Her lips: beyond comparison.

VERSE 62 · DEEP DIVE

HER LIPS

🔎 In context

The bimba fruit is the standard comparison for red lips in Sanskrit poetry. The poet rejects it: the bimba is red only because it reflects her lips. Coral might do, if coral bore fruit, which it doesn’t. Her lips have no true likeness.

📜 Looking deeper

This reversal, the thing compared borrowing its beauty from the Goddess, runs through the whole second half of the hymn. The world’s beautiful things are reflections of her, not models for her.

🌱 For today

Beauty in the world is borrowed light.

↑ VERSE 62 · Her lips: beyond comparison.

VERSE 63

HER SMILE LIKE MOONLIGHT
smitajyotsnājālaṃ tava vadanacandrasya pibatāṃcakorāṇāmāsīdatirasatayā cañcujaḍimāataste śītāṃśoramṛtalaharīmamlarucayaḥpibanti svacchandaṃ niśi niśi bhṛśaṃ kāñjikadhiyā ‖63‖
The cakora birds, drinking the moonlight of the smile from your moon-face, found their beaks numbed by its excessive sweetness. So, for a sour taste, they drink freely the nectar-waves of the cool-rayed moon, night after night, thinking it rice-gruel.
WordMeans
smita-jyotsnā-jālamthe net of moonlight that is your smile
cakorāṇāmof the cakora birds
atirasatayāfrom excessive sweetness
amla-rucayaḥcraving sourness
kāñjika-dhiyāthinking it sour rice-gruel
Next to her smile, the moon tastes sour.

VERSE 63 · DEEP DIVE

HER SMILE LIKE MOONLIGHT

🔎 In context

The cakora, as in verse 37, lives on moonlight. Her smile is a moonlight so sweet that the birds could take no more and turned to the real moon for something sour by comparison: kāñjika, a sour rice-water drink.

📜 Looking deeper

It is one of the funniest images in the hymn: the moon, the model of sweetness in Indian poetry, reduced to sour gruel beside her smile. Under the joke, the poet makes the same point again: all sweetness is a lesser version of hers.

🌱 For today

A smile can be the sweetest thing someone receives all day.

↑ VERSE 63 · Next to her smile, the moon tastes sour.

VERSE 64

HER TONGUE
aviśrāntaṃ patyurguṇagaṇakathāmreḍanajapājapāpuṣpacchāyā tava janani jihvā jayati sāyadagrāsīnāyāḥ sphaṭikadṛṣadacchacchavimayīsarasvatyā mūrtiḥ pariṇamati māṇikyavapuṣā ‖64‖
Your tongue, Mother, red as the hibiscus, ceaselessly repeating like japa the story of your Lord’s virtues, triumphs; seated on its tip, the crystal-white form of Sarasvatī turns into a figure of ruby.
WordMeans
patyuḥ guṇa-gaṇa-kathāthe story of your Lord’s virtues
āmreḍana-japārepeating like japa
japā-puṣpahibiscus flower
sphaṭika-dṛṣadcrystal
māṇikya-vapuṣāwith a ruby form
Sarasvatī turns ruby-red on her tongue.

VERSE 64 · DEEP DIVE

HER TONGUE

🔎 In context

Her tongue constantly recites Śiva’s virtues, like a japa. There is a pun: japā is the hibiscus, and her tongue is hibiscus-red, while japa is repetition. Sarasvatī, goddess of speech, sits on her tongue, and white as she is, she takes on its ruby colour.

📜 Looking deeper

The image of the Goddess endlessly praising her husband balances the earlier verses in which the gods praise her. It is a picture of mutual devotion. And speech, Sarasvatī, takes its colour from the one who speaks.

🌱 For today

Words take the colour of the heart that speaks them.

↑ VERSE 64 · Sarasvatī turns ruby-red on her tongue.

VERSE 65

THE BETEL SHE OFFERS
raṇe jitvā daityānapahṛtaśirastraiḥ kavacibhi-rnivṛttaiścaṇḍāṃśatripuraharanirmālyavimukhaiḥviśākhendropendraiḥ śaśiviśadakarpūraśakalāvilīyante mātastava vadanatāmbūlakabalāḥ ‖65‖
Returning from battle after defeating the demons, still in armour, helmets off, Skanda, Indra and Viṣṇu, turning away from the leftovers of Śiva, which belong to Caṇḍa, take the betel morsels from your mouth, white with bits of camphor bright as the moon, and they melt away.
WordMeans
raṇe jitvā daityānhaving defeated the demons in battle
apahṛta-śirastraiḥwith helmets removed
caṇḍāṃśa … nirmālyaŚiva’s offering-remains, Caṇḍa’s share
viśākha-indra-upendraiḥby Skanda, Indra and Viṣṇu
tāmbūla-kabalāḥbetel morsels
The gods prefer her prasāda.

VERSE 65 · DEEP DIVE

THE BETEL SHE OFFERS

🔎 In context

A scene after victory. The gods come home from battle and are given prasāda. They decline Śiva’s offering-remains, which by custom go to his attendant Caṇḍa (Caṇḍeśvara), and gladly take the betel from her mouth instead, which melts away almost as soon as it is given.

📜 Looking deeper

Prasāda, food sanctified by having been offered to the deity, is central to Hindu worship. The verse ranks hers above Śiva’s. The detail that the betel “melts” suggests either its sweetness or how eagerly it is taken.

🌱 For today

Rest after effort, and receive what is given gratefully.

↑ VERSE 65 · The gods prefer her prasāda.

VERSE 66

HER VOICE AND SARASVATĪ’S VĪṆĀ
vipañcyā gāyantī vividhamapadānaṃ paśupate-stvayārabdhe vaktuṃ calitaśirasā sādhuvacanetadīyairmādhuryairapalapitatantrīkalaravāṃnijāṃ vīṇāṃ vāṇī niculayati colena nibhṛtam ‖66‖
As Vāṇī sings to her vīṇā the various deeds of Paśupati, and you begin to say “well done”, nodding your head, she quietly covers her vīṇā with its case: its sweet notes have been outdone by the sweetness of your words.
WordMeans
vipañcyā gāyantīsinging with the vīṇā
apadānam paśupateḥthe deeds of Śiva
sādhu-vacanewords of “well done”
apalapita-tantrī-kala-ravāmwhose string-notes are outdone
nibhṛtam nicolayatiquietly covers it
Her voice silences Sarasvatī’s vīṇā.

VERSE 66 · DEEP DIVE

HER VOICE AND SARASVATĪ’S VĪṆĀ

🔎 In context

Sarasvatī plays and sings Śiva’s deeds; the Goddess begins to praise her. At the very sound of her voice, Sarasvatī falls silent and wraps her vīṇā in its cover. No instrument can compete. The scene answers verse 60, where the Goddess was the listener.

📜 Looking deeper

The vīṇā of Sarasvatī is the classic image of musical perfection. The Lalitā Sahasranāma also has a name for this: Lalitā’s speech outdoes the vīṇā of Sarasvatī. A hymn and a name-list agree on the same small scene.

🌱 For today

Know when to let a better voice speak.

↑ VERSE 66 · Her voice silences Sarasvatī’s vīṇā.

VERSE 67

HER CHIN
karāgreṇa spṛṣṭaṃ tuhinagiriṇā vatsalatayāgirīśenodastaṃ muhuradharapānākulatayākaragrāhyaṃ śambhormukhamukuravṛntaṃ girisutekathaṅkāraṃ brūmastava cibukamaupamyarahitam ‖67‖
Your chin, touched with the fingertips of the Snow Mountain out of fatherly love, repeatedly lifted by Śiva in his eagerness to drink from your lips, fit to be held in the hand, the handle of the mirror that is your face for Śambhu: how shall we describe it, O daughter of the mountain? It has no likeness.
WordMeans
tuhinagiriṇā vatsalatayāby the Himalaya, out of fatherly love
adhara-pāna-ākulatayāin eagerness to drink from the lips
mukha-mukura-vṛntamthe handle of the mirror of the face
kathaṅkāramhow?
aupamya-rahitamwithout comparison
Her chin: the mirror’s handle.

VERSE 67 · DEEP DIVE

HER CHIN

🔎 In context

Two kinds of love touch her chin: her father Himavān’s affection, and Śiva’s longing. Her face is the mirror in which Śiva sees himself, and her chin is its handle. The poet gives up: it cannot be compared to anything.

📜 Looking deeper

The mirror image fits the tradition. Śiva knows himself through Śakti; she is the mirror of his own awareness, as verse 7 suggested. A tender family scene carries a philosophical idea.

🌱 For today

Love has many forms: a parent’s touch, a partner’s longing.

↑ VERSE 67 · Her chin: the mirror’s handle.

VERSE 68

HER NECK
bhujāśleṣān nityaṃ puradamayituḥ kaṇṭakavatītava grīvā dhatte mukhakamalanālaśriyamiyamsvataḥ śvetā kālāgurubahulajambālamalināmṛṇālīlālityaṃ vahati yadadho hāralatikā ‖68‖
Your neck, its hairs standing on end at the constant embraces of Śiva, destroyer of the cities, has the beauty of the stalk of the lotus that is your face; and the necklace below it, naturally white but darkened by the paste of black aloe, looks like a lotus fibre covered in mud.
WordMeans
bhuja-āśleṣānembraces
kaṇṭakavatībristling, thrilled
mukha-kamala-nālathe stalk of the lotus of the face
kāla-agurublack aloe paste
mṛṇālīlotus fibre
Face and neck: a lotus and its stalk.

VERSE 68 · DEEP DIVE

HER NECK

🔎 In context

The lotus image is carried down from the face: her neck is its stalk. A lotus stalk has tiny bristles; her neck has hairs standing up from Śiva’s embraces. Her pearl necklace, dark with fragrant paste, is the lotus fibre, muddy from the pond.

📜 Looking deeper

Even mud becomes beautiful in this description. The poet’s eye finds a lotus in every detail and a sign of love in every touch.

🌱 For today

Even the muddy roots belong to the lotus.

↑ VERSE 68 · Face and neck: a lotus and its stalk.

VERSE 69

THREE LINES ON HER THROAT
gale rekhāstisro gatigamakagītaikanipuṇevivāhavyānaddhapraguṇaguṇasaṅkhyāpratibhuvaḥvirājante nānāvidhamadhurarāgākarabhuvāṃtrayāṇāṃ grāmāṇāṃ sthitiniyamasīmāna iva te ‖69‖
The three lines on your throat, O you who are expert in songs with movement, grace and ornament, shine like reminders of the number of strands of the auspicious thread tied at your wedding, and like the boundaries that fix the three scales, the mines of many sweet rāgas.
WordMeans
gati-gamaka-gītasong with movement and grace-notes
vivāha-vyānaddhatied at the wedding
guṇa-saṅkhyā-pratibhuvaḥwitnesses of the number of strands
trayāṇāṃ grāmāṇāmof the three scales
rāga-ākarasource of rāgas
Wedding thread and three scales of music.

VERSE 69 · DEEP DIVE

THREE LINES ON HER THROAT

🔎 In context

Three faint lines on the throat are a mark of beauty. The poet gives them two meanings: the three strands of the thread tied at her wedding, and the three grāmas, the basic scales of ancient Indian music from which rāgas arise.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse ties marriage and music together on her throat: the auspicious thread of a married woman and the source of all melody. In Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, readers will think of the many kṛtis to the Goddess, including Dīkṣitar’s, which this series takes up later.

🌱 For today

Commitment and beauty can share the same small space.

↑ VERSE 69 · Wedding thread and three scales of music.

VERSE 70

FOUR ARMS
mṛṇālīmṛdvīnāṃ tava bhujalatānāṃ catasṛṇāṃcaturbhiḥ saundaryaṃ sarasijabhavaḥ stauti vadanaiḥnakhebhyaḥ santrasyan prathamamathanādandhakaripo-ścaturṇāṃ śīrṣāṇāṃ samamabhayahastārpaṇadhiyā ‖70‖
Brahmā, born of the lotus, praises the beauty of your four creeper-like arms, soft as lotus fibres, with his four faces: fearing the nails of Śiva, slayer of Andhaka, who once cut off his first head, he hopes to win a gesture of protection for all four heads at once.
WordMeans
mṛṇālī-mṛdvīnāmsoft as lotus fibres
bhuja-latānām catasṛṇāmof your four creeper-arms
sarasija-bhavaḥBrahmā, lotus-born
andhaka-ripoḥ nakhebhyaḥfrom the nails of Śiva
abhaya-hasta-arpaṇathe giving of a protecting hand
Four arms, four protecting hands.

VERSE 70 · DEEP DIVE

FOUR ARMS

🔎 In context

Brahmā once had five heads; Śiva cut off the fifth with his nail. Since then, Brahmā has been careful. The poet imagines him praising her four arms with his four mouths, hoping each arm will give a protecting gesture to one of his remaining heads.

📜 Looking deeper

The humour is affectionate and well placed: the creator himself needs the Goddess’s protection. Her four arms, which hold the noose, goad, bow and arrows in her images, here become four hands of reassurance.

🌱 For today

Even the powerful look for protection; there is no shame in it.

↑ VERSE 70 · Four arms, four protecting hands.

VERSE 71

HER HANDS
nakhānāmuddyotairnavanalinarāgaṃ vihasatāṃkarāṇāṃ te kāntiṃ kathaya kathayāmaḥ kathamumekayācidvā sāmyaṃ bhajatu kalayā hanta kamalaṃyadi krīḍallakṣmīcaraṇatalalākṣārasachaṇam ‖71‖
Tell us, Umā, how we can describe the radiance of your hands, which with the brilliance of their nails mock the colour of fresh lotuses? Perhaps a lotus could share a little of their likeness, if it were mixed with the red lac from the soles of Lakṣmī’s feet as she plays in it.

kathaya kathayāmaḥ kathamtell us, how shall we describe
lakṣmī-caraṇa-talathe soles of Lakṣmī’s feet
lākṣā-rasared lac dye
Her hands outshine even Lakṣmī’s lotus.

VERSE 71 · DEEP DIVE

HER HANDS

🔎 In context

The lotus is again found wanting. A lotus might come close to her hands only if Lakṣmī, who lives in the lotus, had been playing in it and left behind the red lac with which her soles are dyed. Even then, only “a little”.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse plays on the repeated sound of katha (“tell”, “how”) in its second line. It also places Lakṣmī, goddess of fortune, below the Goddess: Lakṣmī’s footprints are what might make a lotus barely fit for comparison.

🌱 For today

Sometimes the honest answer to “how can I describe it?” is: I can’t.

↑ VERSE 71 · Her hands outshine even Lakṣmī’s lotus.

VERSE 72

THE MOTHER WHO NURSES
samaṃ devi skandadvipavadanapītaṃ stanayugaṃtavedaṃ naḥ khedaṃ haratu satataṃ prasnutamukhamyadālokyāśaṅkākulitahṛdayo hāsajanakaḥsvakumbhau herambaḥ parimṛśati hastena jhaḍiti ‖72‖
O Devī, may your two breasts, nursed from by Skanda and the elephant-faced Gaṇeśa together, always flowing, take away our sorrow. Seeing them, Heramba, his heart troubled with doubt, quickly feels his own temples with his trunk, to everyone’s amusement.
WordMeans
skanda-dvipa-vadana-pītamdrunk from by Skanda and Gaṇeśa
prasnuta-mukhamflowing
khedaṃ haratumay it remove sorrow
herambaḥGaṇeśa
svakumbhau parimṛśatitouches his own temples (to check they are still there)
The mother of Skanda and Gaṇeśa.

VERSE 72 · DEEP DIVE

THE MOTHER WHO NURSES

🔎 In context

The poet turns to the Goddess as mother, nursing her two sons. Little Gaṇeśa, seeing how round they are, worries that someone has taken the round domes of his elephant head, and quickly feels for them with his trunk. Everyone laughs.

📜 Looking deeper

This is family comedy in a sacred hymn, and it is affectionate. The prayer at its heart is simple: may the mother who nurses her children take away our sorrow too. The rest of these verses (72–75) keep this mother’s view.

🌱 For today

A mother’s care, and a child’s funny confusion, are holy too.

↑ VERSE 72 · The mother of Skanda and Gaṇeśa.

VERSE 73

VESSELS OF NECTAR
amū te vakṣojāvamṛtarasamāṇikyakutupauna sandehaspando nagapatipatāke manasi naḥpibantau tau yasmādaviditavadhūsaṅgarasikaukumārāvadyāpi dviradavadanakrauñcadalanau ‖73‖
O banner of the mountain king, we have no doubt that your breasts are jewelled vessels of nectar. For the two boys who drink from them, the elephant-faced one and the splitter of Mount Krauñca, are still, to this day, boys who know nothing of married life.
WordMeans
amṛta-rasa-māṇikya-kutupaujewelled vessels of nectar
na sandeha-spandaḥnot a flicker of doubt
avidita-vadhū-saṅganot knowing the company of a wife
kumārau adyāpistill boys today
krauñca-dalanaḥSkanda, who split the Krauñca mountain
Her milk is nectar.

VERSE 73 · DEEP DIVE

VESSELS OF NECTAR

🔎 In context

The argument is playful: nectar makes one immortal and ever young, and her sons remain boys forever, so her milk must be nectar. In many traditions, especially in the south, both Gaṇeśa and Skanda are pictured as eternal brahmacārins.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse continues the gentle family scene of verse 72. Mother and children are at the centre of the hymn’s praise here, a reminder that the queen of the Śrīcakra is first of all Mother.

🌱 For today

Those cared for well stay young at heart.

↑ VERSE 73 · Her milk is nectar.

VERSE 74

THE PEARL NECKLACE
vahatyamba stamberamadanujakumbhaprakṛtibhiḥsamārabdhāṃ muktāmaṇibhiramalāṃ hāralatikāmkucābhogo bimbādhararucibhirantaḥ śabalitāṃpratāpavyāmiśrāṃ puradamayituḥ kīrtimiva te ‖74‖
O Mother, the expanse of your breast wears a spotless necklace made of pearls born from the temples of the elephant-demon, tinted within by the glow of your bimba-red lips: like the fame of Śiva, destroyer of the cities, mixed with his valour.
WordMeans
stamberama-danujathe elephant-demon
kumbha-prakṛtibhiḥborn from the temples
muktā-maṇibhiḥwith pearls
bimba-adhara-rucibhiḥwith the glow of red lips
pratāpa-vyāmiśrām kīrtimfame mixed with valour
Pearls of Śiva’s victory.

VERSE 74 · DEEP DIVE

THE PEARL NECKLACE

🔎 In context

Indian lore says pearls form in the temples of elephants. Her necklace is made from those of Gajāsura, the elephant demon Śiva killed. Fame is traditionally white and valour red, so the white pearls tinted red by the glow of her lips look like Śiva’s fame and valour mixed.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse puts a whole victory into a necklace. It also continues the theme of the Goddess as Śiva’s glory: what he wins, she wears.

🌱 For today

Victories are best worn lightly.

↑ VERSE 74 · Pearls of Śiva’s victory.

VERSE 75

THE DRAVIḌA CHILD
tava stanyaṃ manye dharaṇidharakanye hṛdayataḥpayaḥpārāvāraḥ parivahati sārasvatamivadayāvatyā dattaṃ draviḍaśiśurāsvādya tava yatkavīnāṃ prauḍhānāmajani kamanīyaḥ kavayitā ‖75‖
I think your milk, O daughter of the mountain, flowing from your heart, is an ocean of milk bearing the essence of Sarasvatī. For when the Draviḍa child tasted it, given by your mercy, he became a delightful poet among the greatest poets.
WordMeans
stanyam(your) milk
payaḥ-pārāvāraḥan ocean of milk
sārasvatamthe essence of Sarasvatī, poetic power
draviḍa-śiśuḥthe Draviḍa (Tamil) child
prauḍhānāṃ kavīnāmamong mature poets
Her milk makes poets.

VERSE 75 · DEEP DIVE

THE DRAVIḌA CHILD

🔎 In context

One of the most discussed verses of the hymn. Who is the Draviḍa child who drank the Goddess’s milk and became a great poet? Many traditional readers say it is Śaṅkara himself, speaking humbly of himself. Others say it is the Tamil Śaiva saint Tirujñānasambandar, of whom exactly this story is told.

📜 Looking deeper

The second reading has led some scholars to argue that the hymn was composed later than Śaṅkara, or that the verse refers back to Sambandar. We give both views without choosing. Either way, the verse’s claim is clear: poetry itself is the Goddess’s milk.

🌱 For today

Talent is a gift; receive it gratefully and use it well.

↑ VERSE 75 · Her milk makes poets.

VERSE 76

THE SMOKE OF KĀMA
harakrodhajvālāvalibhiravalīḍhena vapuṣāgabhīre te nābhīsarasi kṛtasaṅgo manasijaḥsamuttasthau tasmādacalatanaye dhūmalatikājanastāṃ jānīte tava janani romāvaliriti ‖76‖
Kāma, his body scorched by the flames of Hara’s anger, plunged into the deep lake of your navel; from it rose a thin line of smoke, O daughter of the mountain, which people know as the line of fine hair on your body, Mother.
WordMeans
hara-krodha-jvālāthe flames of Śiva’s anger
nābhī-sarasiin the lake of the navel
manasijaḥKāma, “mind-born”
dhūma-latikāa thin line of smoke
romāvaliḥthe line of fine hair
Kāma takes refuge in her.

VERSE 76 · DEEP DIVE

THE SMOKE OF KĀMA

🔎 In context

Back to the story of Kāma. Burned by Śiva’s third eye, Kāma dived into the cool lake of her navel to put out the fire. The smoke that rose from it is the fine line of hair above her navel, a classic mark of beauty in Sanskrit poetry.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse links the descriptive half of the hymn to the Lalitopākhyāna: Kāma burned, and Kāma restored by the Goddess. Here he finds refuge in her very body. Desire burned by austerity finds its healing in her.

🌱 For today

Even what is burned can find a cool place to recover.

↑ VERSE 76 · Kāma takes refuge in her.

VERSE 77

A RIPPLE OF THE YAMUNĀ
yadetat kālindītanutarataraṅgākṛti śivekṛśe madhye kiñcijjanani tava yadbhāti sudhiyāmvimardādanyo'nyaṃ kucakalaśayorantaragataṃtanūbhūtaṃ vyoma praviśadiva nābhiṃ kuhariṇīm ‖77‖
O Śivā, that which shines on your slender waist, Mother, like a fine wave of the Yamunā, appears to the wise as space itself, made thin by the pressing together of your breasts, entering the hollow of your navel.
WordMeans
kālindī-tanutara-taraṅgaa very slender wave of the Yamunā
kṛśe madhyeon the slender waist

VERSE 77 · DEEP DIVE

A RIPPLE OF THE YAMUNĀ

🔎 In context

The same line of hair is now seen differently: a dark ripple of the Yamunā, or, to the wise, a thread of space squeezed between her breasts and slipping into her navel. The poet keeps finding new images for one small feature.

📜 Looking deeper

Space, ākāśa, is the subtlest of the elements, and in verse 37 it was born in the throat centre. Here it threads through her body. The imagery is delicate and carefully framed: her body is a landscape in which rivers and the sky flow.

🌱 For today

Look again at a familiar thing, and it can become new.

↑ VERSE 77 · A ripple of the Yamunā.

VERSE 78

HER NAVEL
sthiro gaṅgāvartaḥ stanamukularomāvalilatā-kalāvālaṃ kuṇḍaṃ kusumaśaratejohutabhujaḥraterlīlāgāraṃ kimapi tava nābhirgirisutebiladvāraṃ siddhergiriśanayanānāṃ vijayate ‖78‖
Your navel, O daughter of the mountain, triumphs: a steady whirlpool of the Gaṅgā; a basin for the creeper of hair that bears the buds of your breasts; the fire-pit of Kāma’s blazing energy; the pleasure-house of Rati; the cave-door to success for Śiva’s eyes.
WordMeans

ālavālama basin at the foot of a plant
kusumaśara-tejo-hutabhujaḥ kuṇḍamfire-pit of Kāma’s energy
rateḥ līlā-āgāramRati’s pleasure-house
bila-dvāram siddheḥthe cave-door to attainment
Her navel: whirlpool, altar, cave.

VERSE 78 · DEEP DIVE

HER NAVEL

🔎 In context

Five images in four lines, each turning her navel into a sacred place: a whirlpool in the holy river, a gardener’s basin, a ritual fire-pit, a house of love, and the mouth of a cave where yogis go for siddhi. The last is aimed at Śiva the yogi, whose eyes find their “attainment” there.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse shows how freely this poetry mixes the sacred and the sensual. In the Śākta view, both belong to the Goddess. A fire-pit (kuṇḍa) is where offerings are made; to call her navel one is to make her body the altar.

🌱 For today

Every part of creation can be an altar, seen rightly.

↑ VERSE 78 · Her navel: whirlpool, altar, cave.

VERSE 79

MAY HER WAIST BE WELL
nisargakṣīṇasya stanataṭabhareṇa klamajuṣonamanmūrternārītilaka śanakaistruṭyata ivaciraṃ te madhyasya truṭitataṭinītīrataruṇāsamāvasthāsthemno bhavatu kuśalaṃ śailatanaye ‖79‖
Naturally slender, wearied by the weight of your breasts and bent forward, it seems about to break little by little. O jewel among women, daughter of the mountain, may your waist, which has the steadiness of a tree on a riverbank that is giving way, be well for a long time.
WordMeans

klama-juṣaḥwearied
nārī-tilakaornament among women

VERSE 79 · DEEP DIVE

MAY HER WAIST BE WELL

🔎 In context

The poet worries, lovingly, for her slender waist, as if it might snap under its burden. He compares it to a tree on a river bank that is being washed away: it holds, but for how long? He ends with a blessing: may it be well.

📜 Looking deeper

It is unusual and touching for a devotee to bless the Goddess. The verse is a sign of the intimacy this hymn allows: the worshipper cares for her as one cares for someone loved, not only bows to her.

🌱 For today

Love includes wishing well even to the strongest.

↑ VERSE 79 · A devotee blesses the Goddess.

VERSE 80

THE THREE FOLDS
kucau sadyaḥsvidyattaṭaghaṭitakūrpāsabhiduraukaṣantau dormūle kanakakalaśābhau kalayatātava trātuṃ bhaṅgādalamiti valagnaṃ tanubhuvātridhā naddhaṃ devi trivali lavalīvallibhiriva ‖80‖
Your breasts, their bodice bursting with sudden perspiration, pressing against the roots of your arms, gleaming like golden pots: Kāma, as if to protect your waist from breaking, saying “enough!”, has bound it three times round with lavalī creepers, O Devī: these are your three folds.

VERSE 80 · DEEP DIVE

THE THREE FOLDS

🔎 In context

The three soft folds above the waist (trivalī) are another classical mark of beauty. The poet gives them a story: Kāma, worried that her slender waist would break, tied it three times round with creepers for safety.

📜 Looking deeper

This part of the hymn ends, as Part 3 did, with a playful picture. The detailed description continues in Part 5 down to her hips, thighs and feet, and then turns to the hymn’s closing prayers.

🌱 For today

Care sometimes looks like three gentle knots.

↑ VERSE 80 · Kāma ties three folds to keep her safe.
