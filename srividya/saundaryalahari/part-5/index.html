ŚRĪ SAUNDARYALAHARĪ · PART 5 OF 5 · VERSES 81–100

SAUNDARYALAHARĪ

The wave of beauty · her feet, and the offering

HYMNS OF THE GODDESS

HOW THIS WORKS: All twenty verses in one go for chanting, then every verse on two pages: the verse, a picture, its meaning and key words; then a deep dive into the ideas and stories behind it.

WHERE WE ARE

Part 5 completes the Saundaryalaharī. The description reaches the Goddess’s feet, the part most worshipped of all, and the poet prays: “place your feet on my head too” (84). Then the hymn sums up her whole form as Śiva’s compassion (93), names her the fourth beyond Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī and Pārvatī (97), and ends by offering her own words back to her (100).

The Vaidika Vignanam text closes with a phalaśruti, saundaryalahari mukhyastotraṃ saṃvārtadāyakam, bhagavadpāda sankluptaṃ paṭhen muktau bhavennaraḥ: “the Saundaryalaharī, chief of hymns, composed by the Bhagavatpāda; whoever recites it will be liberated”, and a colophon naming Śaṅkara, pupil of Govinda Bhagavatpāda, as its author. We give these as the edition prints them; the attribution is traditional.

As in the other parts, the yantras and uses traditionally attached to each verse are left out.

VERSE 81

HER HIPS
gurutvaṃ vistāraṃ kṣitidharapatiḥ pārvati nijā-nnitambādācchidya tvayi haraṇarūpeṇa nidadheataste vistīrṇo gururayamaśeṣāṃ vasumatīṃnitambaprāgbhāraḥ sthagayati laghutvaṃ nayati ca ‖81‖
O Pārvatī, the king of mountains took weight and breadth from his own slopes and gave them to you as a wedding gift. So the broad and heavy expanse of your hips covers the whole earth, and makes it seem light.
WordMeans
gurutvaṃ vistāramweight and breadth
kṣitidhara-patiḥthe king of mountains, Himavān
haraṇa-rūpeṇaas a wedding gift (dowry)
aśeṣāṃ vasumatīmthe whole earth
laghutvaṃ nayatimakes light
A wedding gift from the Himalaya.

VERSE 81 · DEEP DIVE

HER HIPS

🔎 In context

The descent through her body reaches her hips. The poet explains their fullness with a family story: her father, the Himalaya, gave her his own weight and breadth as part of her wedding gifts. Her hips now outweigh the earth itself.

📜 Looking deeper

There is a pun: nitamba means both hips and the slopes of a mountain. A daughter of the mountain inherits its slopes. The verse is a reminder that Pārvatī is, quite literally, the mountain’s child, and that the Goddess and the earth are bound together.

🌱 For today

What we inherit from those who love us can be carried with grace.

↑ VERSE 81 · A wedding gift from the Himalaya.

VERSE 82

HER THIGHS AND KNEES
karīndrāṇāṃ śuṇḍān kanakakadalīkāṇḍapaṭalī-mubhābhyāmūrubhyāmubhayamapi nirjitya bhavatīsuvṛttābhyāṃ patyuḥ praṇatikaṭhinābhyāṃ girisutevidhijñe jānubhyāṃ vibudhakarikumbhadvayamasi ‖82‖
With your two thighs you have outdone both the trunks of the lordly elephants and the rows of golden plantain stems, O daughter of the mountain; and with your knees, rounded and hardened from bowing to your husband, you outdo the twin temples of the celestial elephant, O knower of right conduct.
WordMeans
karīndrāṇāṃ śuṇḍānthe trunks of great elephants
kanaka-kadalī-kāṇḍagolden plantain stems
patyuḥ praṇati-kaṭhinābhyāmhardened by bowing to your husband
vidhijñeO knower of right conduct
vibudha-kari-kumbhathe temples of the celestial elephant
Knees made firm by bowing in love.

VERSE 82 · DEEP DIVE

HER THIGHS AND KNEES

🔎 In context

Elephant trunks and plantain stems are the standard comparisons for thighs in Sanskrit poetry; the poet says she surpasses both. Then a touching detail: her knees are firm from bowing so often to her husband.

📜 Looking deeper

The Goddess who is worshipped by all the gods is herself shown bowing, in love and in right conduct (vidhi). The tradition is fond of this mutual reverence between Śiva and Śakti; verse 64 showed her tongue always praising him.

🌱 For today

Humility is not weakness; it shapes strength.

↑ VERSE 82 · Knees made firm by bowing in love.

VERSE 83

QUIVERS AND ARROWS
parājetuṃ rudraṃ dviguṇaśaragarbhau girisuteniṣaṅgau jaṅghe te viṣamaviśikho bāḍhamakṛtayadagre dṛśyante daśaśaraphalāḥ pādayugalī-nakhāgracchadmānaḥ suramakuṭaśāṇaikaniśitāḥ ‖83‖
To defeat Rudra, the five-arrowed god has surely made your shanks, O daughter of the mountain, into two quivers holding double the arrows; at their ends appear the ten arrowheads, disguised as the tips of the toenails of your two feet, sharpened on the whetstones of the gods’ crowns.
WordMeans
parājetuṃ rudramto defeat Rudra
dviguṇa-śara-garbhau niṣaṅgautwo quivers holding double the arrows
viṣama-viśikhaḥthe god with an odd number (five) of arrows: Kāma
daśa-śara-phalāḥten arrowheads
sura-makuṭa-śāṇathe whetstones of the gods’ crowns
Ten arrowheads: her toenails.

VERSE 83 · DEEP DIVE

QUIVERS AND ARROWS

🔎 In context

Kāma has five arrows. To win against Śiva, he has doubled them to ten and stored them in two quivers: her two shanks. The ten arrowheads are her ten toenails, sharpened by rubbing against the crowns of the gods who bow at her feet.

📜 Looking deeper

The archery theme of Part 3 returns, now at her feet. Every bow of the gods sharpens her toenails a little more. The poet is building to the feet, the part of the Goddess most worshipped of all.

🌱 For today

Even what seems small, a toenail, can be part of something great.

↑ VERSE 83 · Ten arrowheads: her toenails.

VERSE 84

PLACE YOUR FEET ON MY HEAD
śrutīnāṃ mūrdhāno dadhati tava yau śekharatayāmamāpyetau mātaḥ śirasi dayayā dhehi caraṇauyayoḥ pādyaṃ pāthaḥ paśupatijaṭājūṭataṭinīyayorlākṣālakṣmīraruṇaharicūḍāmaṇiruciḥ ‖84‖
Your feet, which the crowns of the Vedas, the Upaniṣads, wear as their head-ornament: these, Mother, place on my head too, in mercy. The water that washes them is the river in Paśupati’s matted hair, and the beauty of their red lac is the glow of the crest-jewel of Hari.
WordMeans
śrutīnāṃ mūrdhānaḥthe heads of the Vedas: the Upaniṣads
śekharatayāas a crest ornament
mamāpi … śirasion my head too
paśupati-jaṭā-jūṭa-taṭinīthe river in Śiva’s hair: Gaṅgā
hari-cūḍāmaṇiViṣṇu’s crest-jewel
Place your feet on my head.

VERSE 84 · DEEP DIVE

PLACE YOUR FEET ON MY HEAD

🔎 In context

This is the heart of the final part. The Upaniṣads, the “heads” of the Vedas, carry her feet on their heads: that is, their highest teaching points to her. The poet asks for the same blessing: put your feet on my head too.

📜 Looking deeper

The second half explains their sacredness. The Gaṅgā, flowing from Śiva’s hair, is the water that washes them; Viṣṇu’s crest-jewel, as he bows, colours them red. Placing the guru’s or deity’s feet on the head is the oldest gesture of grace in Indian religion, and the poet asks for it directly.

🌱 For today

Ask for blessing simply and directly; it is the oldest prayer.

↑ VERSE 84 · Place your feet on my head.

VERSE 85

THE AŚOKA TREE’S ENVY
namovākaṃ brūmo nayanaramaṇīyāya padayo-stavāsmai dvandvāya sphuṭarucirasālaktakavateasūyatyatyantaṃ yadabhihananāya spṛhayatepaśūnāmīśānaḥ pramadavanakaṅkelitarave ‖85‖
We say our salutations to this pair of your feet, lovely to the eyes, bright with the glow of red lac; at which Paśupati, lord of creatures, feels great envy of the aśoka tree in the pleasure garden, which longs to be struck by them.
WordMeans
namovākaṃ brūmaḥwe speak our salutations
alaktakavatebright with red lac
asūyati atyantamis very envious
abhihananāya spṛhayatelonging to be struck
kaṅkeli-taraveof the aśoka tree
Śiva envies the aśoka tree.

VERSE 85 · DEEP DIVE

THE AŚOKA TREE’S ENVY

🔎 In context

Sanskrit poets tell of trees that bloom only at a woman’s touch; the aśoka blossoms when kicked by a beautiful woman’s foot. The aśoka in her garden longs for that touch, and Śiva himself is jealous of the tree.

📜 Looking deeper

The joke deepens in the next verse, where Śiva actually receives that touch. It is a reminder that this part of the hymn is not just ornamental: devotion to the feet of the Goddess is the summit of the whole poem.

🌱 For today

Even a lord may envy a tree, if the tree is near what he loves.

VERSE 86

A LOVERS’ QUARREL
mṛṣā kṛtvā gotraskhalanamatha vailakṣyanamitaṃlalāṭe bhartāraṃ caraṇakamale tāḍayati tecirādantaḥśalyaṃ dahanakṛtamunmūlitavatātulākoṭikvāṇaiḥ kilikilitamīśānaripuṇā ‖86‖
When your husband, having pretended to slip and say another woman’s name, bows in embarrassment, your lotus foot strikes him on the forehead; and the enemy of Īśāna, Kāma, who has long had a thorn in his heart from being burned, now pulled out at last, cheers with the jingling of your anklets.
WordMeans
mṛṣā kṛtvā gotra-skhalanampretending to slip and say another name
vailakṣya-namitambowed in embarrassment
lalāṭe … tāḍayatistrikes on the forehead
antaḥ-śalyama thorn in the heart
tulā-koṭi-kvāṇaiḥwith the tinkle of anklets
Kāma cheers: Śiva bows.

VERSE 86 · DEEP DIVE

A LOVERS’ QUARREL

🔎 In context

A scene from love poetry. Śiva teasingly calls her by another name; she, in mock anger, touches his bowed forehead with her foot. Kāma, whom Śiva burned long ago, finally has his revenge: the stern god has been made to bow, and the anklets jingle like cheering.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse is light, and also deep. Śiva’s forehead bears the third eye that burned Kāma. Her foot touches that very eye. Commentators read it as the moment desire, burned by austerity, is restored by the Goddess, the story of the Lalitopākhyāna, told as a lovers’ quarrel.

🌱 For today

Even the deepest wounds can be healed by love, sometimes with a laugh.

↑ VERSE 86 · Kāma cheers: Śiva bows.

VERSE 87

FEET THAT OUTSHINE THE LOTUS
himānīhantavyaṃ himagirinivāsaikacaturauniśāyāṃ nidrāṇaṃ niśi caramabhāge ca viśadauvaraṃ lakṣmīpātraṃ śriyamatisṛjantau samayināṃsarojaṃ tvatpādau janani jayataścitramiha kim ‖87‖
The lotus is destroyed by snow; your feet are expert at living in the snow mountain. The lotus sleeps at night; your feet are awake night and day. The lotus is Lakṣmī’s home; your feet give Lakṣmī to their devotees. Your feet surpass the lotus, Mother: what wonder is there in that?
WordMeans
himānī-hantavyamdestroyed by snow
himagiri-nivāsadwelling in the snow mountain
niśāyāṃ nidrāṇamsleeping at night
lakṣmī-pātramvessel of Lakṣmī
śriyam atisṛjantaugiving away Lakṣmī, fortune
Her feet beat the lotus on every count.

VERSE 87 · DEEP DIVE

FEET THAT OUTSHINE THE LOTUS

🔎 In context

A neat three-point comparison. Lotuses die in snow; her feet live happily in the Himalaya. Lotuses close at night; her feet are always awake to help. The lotus holds Lakṣmī; her feet give Lakṣmī away to devotees. So of course her feet win.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse mentions samayins, followers of the Samaya way, the inner school of Śrīvidyā to which the commentator Lakṣmīdhara belonged. The feet that grant fortune are the same feet worshipped in the heart.

🌱 For today

What gives is greater than what only holds.

↑ VERSE 87 · Her feet beat the lotus on every count.

VERSE 88

SOFTER THAN A FLOWER
padaṃ te kīrtīnāṃ prapadamapadaṃ devi vipadāṃkathaṃ nītaṃ sadbhiḥ kaṭhinakamaṭhīkarparatulāmkathaṃ vā bāhubhyāmupayamanakāle purabhidāyadādāya nyastaṃ dṛṣadi dayamānena manasā ‖88‖
O Devī, how could the good poets compare the front of your foot, the home of fame and no home for misfortune, to the hard shell of a tortoise? And how could Śiva, destroyer of the cities, at your wedding, take it in his hands and place it on the stone, with a compassionate heart?
WordMeans
kīrtīnāṃ padamthe home of fame
apadaṃ vipadāmno place for misfortunes
kaṭhina-kamaṭhī-karparathe hard shell of a tortoise
upayamana-kāleat the time of the wedding
dṛṣadi nyastamplaced on the stone
Too soft for the wedding stone.

VERSE 88 · DEEP DIVE

SOFTER THAN A FLOWER

🔎 In context

Poets compare the arched front of a foot to a tortoise’s shell for its shape. The poet protests: it is far too soft. And at the Hindu wedding, the groom places the bride’s foot on a grinding stone (aśmārohaṇa) as a sign of steadfastness. How could Śiva bear to put so soft a foot on a stone?

📜 Looking deeper

The verse draws on a rite every Hindu reader knows from weddings. It imagines Śiva hesitating with tenderness. The Goddess, the queen of the universe, is shown as a bride whose husband is gentle with her.

🌱 For today

Tenderness makes even a ritual more meaningful.

↑ VERSE 88 · Too soft for the wedding stone.

VERSE 89

FEET THAT GIVE TO THE POOR
nakhairnākastrīṇāṃ karakamalasaṅkocaśaśibhi-starūṇāṃ divyānāṃ hasata iva te caṇḍi caraṇauphalāni svaḥsthebhyaḥ kisalayakarāgreṇa dadatāṃdaridrebhyo bhadrāṃ śriyamaniśamahnāya dadatau ‖89‖
Your feet, Caṇḍī, with toenails that are moons closing the lotus-hands of the celestial women, seem to laugh at the heavenly trees: those give fruit only to those who live in heaven, with the tips of their leaf-hands; your feet give auspicious fortune to the poor, always and at once.
WordMeans
nāka-strīṇāmof the celestial women
kara-kamala-saṅkoca-śaśibhiḥmoons that close the lotus-hands
divyānāṃ tarūṇāmof the heavenly trees
svaḥ-sthebhyaḥto those in heaven
daridrebhyaḥ … aniśam ahnāyato the poor, constantly and quickly
Her feet give to the poor.

VERSE 89 · DEEP DIVE

FEET THAT GIVE TO THE POOR

🔎 In context

The wish-granting trees of heaven, the kalpavṛkṣas, give fruit only to the gods who already live there. Her feet give to the poor on earth, constantly and without delay. Her toenails are like moons, and the celestial women, bowing with folded hands, find their lotus-hands closing at the sight, as lotuses close in moonlight.

📜 Looking deeper

The contrast is the hymn’s social conscience: heaven’s trees serve those who already have everything; the Goddess serves those who have nothing. Commentators often quote this verse to show that her grace has a special direction: towards the needy.

🌱 For today

Give first to those who have least.

↑ VERSE 89 · Her feet give to the poor.

VERSE 90

LET MY SOUL BE A BEE
dadāne dīnebhyaḥ śriyamaniśamāśānusadṛśī-mamandaṃ saundaryaprakaramakarandaṃ vikiratitavāsmin mandārastabakasubhage yātu caraṇenimajjanmajjīvaḥ karaṇacaraṇaḥ ṣaṭcaraṇatām ‖90‖
Your foot, which gives the poor fortune always, as they wish, scatters abundant honey in a flood of beauty and is lovely as a cluster of mandāra flowers. May my soul, with its six organs as its legs, dive into it and become a six-footed bee.
WordMeans
āśā-anusadṛśīmas they wish
saundarya-prakara-makarandamthe honey of the flood of beauty
mandāra-stabakaa cluster of mandāra flowers
karaṇa-caraṇaḥwith the (six) senses as legs
ṣaṭcaraṇatāmthe state of a six-footed bee
Let my soul become a bee.

VERSE 90 · DEEP DIVE

LET MY SOUL BE A BEE

🔎 In context

Bees have six legs. The poet notices that he has six “legs” too: the five senses and the mind. He prays that his soul, with these six, may become a bee diving into the flower of her foot and drinking its honey forever.

📜 Looking deeper

It is one of the loveliest prayers in the hymn. Instead of withdrawing the senses from the world, the poet asks that all six be turned to her, absorbed in her beauty. The senses are not enemies; they are legs to carry him to her.

🌱 For today

Turn every sense towards what is beautiful and good.

↑ VERSE 90 · Let my soul become a bee.

VERSE 91

THE SWANS WHO LEARN TO WALK
padanyāsakrīḍāparicayamivārabdhumanasaḥskhalantaste khelaṃ bhavanakalahaṃsā na jahatiatasteṣāṃ śikṣāṃ subhagamaṇimañjīraraṇita-cchalādācakṣāṇaṃ caraṇakamalaṃ cārucarite ‖91‖
Wanting, it seems, to learn your graceful way of walking, the swans of your palace keep following you as you play, stumbling. So your lotus foot, O you of lovely ways, with the ringing of your jewelled anklets as a pretext, seems to be teaching them.

pada-nyāsa-krīḍāthe grace of placing the feet, walking
bhavana-kalahaṃsāḥthe swans of the palace
skhalantaḥstumbling
maṇi-mañjīra-raṇitathe ringing of jewelled anklets
chalāt ācakṣāṇamteaching, under a pretext
Even swans learn to walk from her.

VERSE 91 · DEEP DIVE

THE SWANS WHO LEARN TO WALK

🔎 In context

A swan’s gait is the classic image of graceful walking in Indian poetry. Here the swans themselves try to learn from her, and stumble. Her anklets ring as she walks; the poet imagines they are giving a lesson.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse reverses the usual comparison again: the standard of grace learns from her. It also shows the hymn’s tenderness at its close: even the household swans are her pupils.

🌱 For today

Teach by example; your steps are lessons for others.

↑ VERSE 91 · Even swans learn to walk from her.

VERSE 92

ŚIVA AS HER COVER
gatāste mañcatvaṃ druhiṇaharirudreśvarabhṛtaḥśivaḥ svacchacchāyāghaṭitakapaṭapracchadapaṭaḥtvadīyānāṃ bhāsāṃ pratiphalanarāgāruṇatayāśarīrī śṛṅgāro rasa iva dṛśāṃ dogdhi kutukam ‖92‖
Brahmā, Hari, Rudra and Īśvara, the bearers, have become your couch; Śiva, of clear and bright lustre, has become its cover-cloth; reddened by the reflection of your radiance, he appears as śṛṅgāra, the mood of love, embodied, and fills your eyes with delight.
WordMeans
mañcatvaṃ gatāḥhave become the couch
druhiṇa-hari-rudra-īśvaraBrahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra, Īśvara
svaccha-cchāyāof clear lustre
pracchada-paṭaḥcovering cloth
śarīrī śṛṅgāraḥthe mood of love, with a body
Four gods her couch, Śiva her cover.

VERSE 92 · DEEP DIVE

ŚIVA AS HER COVER

🔎 In context

This is the full picture of Lalitā’s seat, which verse 8 hinted at: four gods are its legs, and Sadāśiva, white and clear, is its cover. Reflecting her red glow, he turns pink: the very form of śṛṅgāra, the rasa of love, whose colour in Indian aesthetics is that tint.

📜 Looking deeper

The Lalitā Sahasranāma calls her pañcabrahmāsanasthitā, seated on the five Brahmas, and pañcapretāsanāsīnā. The same image is central to the Śrīcakra’s bindu. Śiva as her couch-cover is a striking way of saying that the Goddess rests on pure consciousness.

🌱 For today

The highest rests on stillness.

↑ VERSE 92 · Four gods her couch, Śiva her cover.

VERSE 93

ŚIVA’S COMPASSION, RED
arālā keśeṣu prakṛtisaralā mandahasiteśirīṣābhā citte dṛṣadupalaśobhā kucataṭebhṛśaṃ tanvī madhye pṛthururasijārohaviṣayejagattrātuṃ śambhorjayati karuṇā kācidaruṇā ‖93‖
Curving in her hair, naturally straight in her gentle smile, soft as the śirīṣa flower in her heart, firm as stone in her breast, very slender at the waist, full in the hips and breasts: a certain red compassion of Śambhu, to save the world, triumphs.
WordMeans
arālā keśeṣucurving in her hair
prakṛti-saralā manda-hasitenaturally simple in her gentle smile
śirīṣa-ābhā cittesoft as śirīṣa in heart

VERSE 93 · DEEP DIVE

ŚIVA’S COMPASSION, RED

🔎 In context

Having described her limb by limb, the poet sums her up in one verse of contrasts: curved and straight, soft and firm, slender and full. Then the final word: she is Śiva’s compassion itself, coloured red, come to save the world.

📜 Looking deeper

This is perhaps the deepest line of the second half. All the beauty described in sixty verses turns out to be one thing: compassion. The Goddess is how Śiva’s mercy appears. Lalitā’s redness, praised throughout, is the colour of that compassion.

🌱 For today

Beauty, rightly seen, is a form of kindness.

↑ VERSE 93 · She is Śiva’s compassion, red.

VERSE 94

THE MOON AS HER CASKET
kalaṅkaḥ kastūrī rajanikarabimbaṃ jalamayaṃkalābhiḥ karpūrairmarakatakaraṇḍaṃ nibiḍitamatastvadbhogena pratidinamidaṃ riktakuharaṃvidhirbhūyo bhūyo nibiḍayati nūnaṃ tava kṛte ‖94‖
The moon’s dark mark is musk; the moon’s disc, made of water, is an emerald casket; its digits are pieces of camphor packed inside. Because you use them every day, it is emptied, and so Brahmā, for your sake, fills it up again and again.
WordMeans
kalaṅkaḥ kastūrīthe moon’s mark is musk

kalābhiḥ karpūraiḥwith digits as camphor

The moon is her camphor box.

VERSE 94 · DEEP DIVE

THE MOON AS HER CASKET

🔎 In context

A delightful explanation of the moon’s phases. The moon is her toiletry box: the dark spot is the musk, the bright digits are pieces of camphor. As she uses them, the moon wanes; Brahmā, her servant, refills it, and it waxes again.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse links back to the Tithi Nityās: the waxing and waning of the moon are tied to the Goddess. Here the cycle of fifteen days becomes a simple domestic routine: she uses, Brahmā refills. Time is part of her household.

🌱 For today

Even the moon’s cycle can be pictured as service.

↑ VERSE 94 · The moon is her camphor box.

VERSE 95

HARD TO WORSHIP
purārāterantaḥpuramasi tatastvaccaraṇayoḥsaparyāmaryādā taralakaraṇānāmasulabhātathā hyete nītāḥ śatamakhamukhāḥ siddhimatulāṃtava dvāropāntasthitibhiraṇimādyābhiramarāḥ ‖95‖
You are the inner palace of Śiva, enemy of the cities; so the full worship of your feet is not easy for those whose senses are restless. That is why Indra and the other gods attain only the incomparable siddhis, as they wait by your door with Aṇimā and the other attendant goddesses.
WordMeans
purārāteḥ antaḥpuramthe inner apartments of Śiva
saparyā-maryādāthe full rule of worship
tarala-karaṇānāmfor those with restless senses
atulāṃ siddhimincomparable powers
dvāra-upāntanear the door
The gods get only as far as the door.

VERSE 95 · DEEP DIVE

HARD TO WORSHIP

🔎 In context

The Goddess is Śiva’s inner chamber, where only the closest may enter. The gods, whose senses are restless, never get inside; they wait at the door, where the eight siddhi goddesses, Aṇimā and the rest, attend, and they receive siddhis, powers, instead of her.

📜 Looking deeper

This is a pointed verse. The siddhis, which many seekers prize, are only what is found at her gate. The true worship of her feet, inside, needs a steady mind. On the Śrīcakra, the siddhi goddesses are in the outermost enclosure: the gate, not the centre.

🌱 For today

Do not mistake the gate for the house.

↑ VERSE 95 · The gods get only as far as the door.

VERSE 96

THE FAITHFUL ONE
kalatraṃ vaidhātraṃ katikati bhajante na kavayaḥśriyo devyāḥ ko vā na bhavati patiḥ kairapi dhanaiḥmahādevaṃ hitvā tava sati satīnāmacaramekucābhyāmāsaṅgaḥ kuravakatarorapyasulabhaḥ ‖96‖
How many poets do not approach Brahmā’s consort, Sarasvatī? Who does not become a lord of Lakṣmī with a little wealth? But, O chaste one, first of faithful wives, apart from Mahādeva, nearness to you is hard to gain even for the kuravaka tree.
WordMeans

Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī are within reach; she belongs to Śiva.

VERSE 96 · DEEP DIVE

THE FAITHFUL ONE

🔎 In context

Learning, Sarasvatī, is open to any poet; wealth, Lakṣmī, to anyone who gets rich. But the Goddess belongs to Śiva alone. In poetic convention the kuravaka tree blossoms when embraced by a woman; even it cannot get close to her.

📜 Looking deeper

The verse praises her as the ideal faithful wife and also makes a theological point: Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī can be “won” in ordinary ways, but the supreme Goddess is reached only by union with Śiva, that is, by realising Śiva-consciousness.

🌱 For today

Some things cannot be bought or learned, only grown into.

↑ VERSE 96 · Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī are within reach; she belongs to Śiva.

VERSE 97

THE FOURTH
girāmāhurdevīṃ druhiṇagṛhiṇīmāgamavidohareḥ patnīṃ padmāṃ harasahacarīmadritanayāmturīyā kāpi tvaṃ duradhigamaniḥsīmamahimāmahāmāyā viśvaṃ bhramayasi parabrahmamahiṣi ‖97‖
Those who know the scriptures call you the goddess of speech, wife of Brahmā; Padmā, wife of Hari; the mountain’s daughter, companion of Hara. But you are some fourth one, hard to reach, of limitless greatness: Mahāmāyā, queen of the supreme Brahman, who sets the whole universe spinning.
WordMeans
girāṃ devīmthe goddess of speech
harasahacarīm adritanayāmthe mountain’s daughter, Śiva’s companion
turīyā kāpisome fourth one
niḥsīma-mahimāof limitless greatness
para-brahma-mahiṣiqueen of the supreme Brahman
She is the fourth, beyond all three.

VERSE 97 · DEEP DIVE

THE FOURTH

🔎 In context

A key verse for Śākta theology. The three great goddesses, Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī and Pārvatī, are each identified with her by scholars. The poet says: true, but you are a fourth beyond all three. Turīya, “the fourth”, is the Upaniṣadic name for the state beyond waking, dream and sleep.

📜 Looking deeper

Calling her Mahāmāyā, who “sets the universe spinning”, and queen of the supreme Brahman, the poet places her at the top of everything. Each of the three goddesses is a form of her; she herself is the fourth, beyond form.

🌱 For today

Every name points to something larger than the name.

↑ VERSE 97 · She is the fourth, beyond all three.

VERSE 98

WHEN WILL I DRINK?
kadā kāle mātaḥ kathaya kalitālaktakarasaṃpibeyaṃ vidyārthī tava caraṇanirṇejanajalamprakṛtyā mūkānāmapi ca kavitākāraṇatayākadā dhatte vāṇīmukhakamalatāmbūlarasatām ‖98‖
Tell me, Mother, when shall I, a seeker of knowledge, drink the water that has washed your feet, tinged with red lac? That water, by its nature, makes poets even of those born dumb; when will it take on, for me, the taste of the betel from the lotus mouth of Sarasvatī?
WordMeans
vidyārthīa seeker of knowledge, a student
caraṇa-nirṇejana-jalamthe water that washes your feet
mūkānām apieven of the dumb
kavitā-kāraṇatayābeing the cause of poetry
vāṇī-mukha-kamala-tāmbūlathe betel of Sarasvatī’s lotus mouth
A student asks for her foot-water.

VERSE 98 · DEEP DIVE

WHEN WILL I DRINK?

🔎 In context

The poet speaks as a humble student. The water that has washed the Goddess’s feet, tinged red by their lac, is said to make even the dumb into poets. He longs to drink it, so that it tastes to him like the betel in Sarasvatī’s mouth: the very source of eloquence.

📜 Looking deeper

Tradition links this verse with Mūkakavi, “the dumb poet”, who is said to have gained speech by the grace of Kāmākṣī at Kāñcī and composed the Mūka Pañcaśatī. Drinking the water that has washed the deity’s feet (pādatīrtha) is still a common act of devotion in temples.

🌱 For today

A student’s humility opens the door to learning.

↑ VERSE 98 · A student asks for her foot-water.

VERSE 99

THE FRUIT OF WORSHIP
sarasvatyā lakṣmyā vidhiharisapatno viharaterateḥ pātivratyaṃ śithilayati ramyeṇa vapuṣāciraṃ jīvanneva kṣapitapaśupāśavyatikaraḥparānandābhikhyaṃ rasayati rasaṃ tvadbhajanavān ‖99‖
Whoever worships you plays with Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī, a rival to Brahmā and Hari; with his handsome form he unsettles the fidelity of Rati; living long, the bonds that tie the soul destroyed, he tastes the essence called supreme bliss.
WordMeans
vidhi-hari-sapatnaḥa rival of Brahmā and Viṣṇu

VERSE 99 · DEEP DIVE

THE FRUIT OF WORSHIP

🔎 In context

The hymn’s summing up of its fruits. The devotee gains learning (Sarasvatī) and wealth (Lakṣmī), as if rivalling their husbands; beauty enough to stir Rati, wife of Kāma; a long life; and, above all, freedom from the bonds of the soul and the taste of supreme bliss, while still alive.

📜 Looking deeper

Paśu, pāśa, pati, the soul, its bonds and the Lord, are key terms of Śaiva thought. The verse ends the list of worldly gifts with liberation, the highest. True to Śrīvidyā, it does not set enjoyment and liberation against each other: both come from her.

🌱 For today

The world’s gifts and freedom need not be enemies.

↑ VERSE 99 · Learning, wealth, beauty, life, and supreme bliss.

VERSE 100

HER OWN WORDS, OFFERED BACK
pradīpajvālābhirdivasakaranīrājanavidhiḥsudhāsūteścandropalajalalavairarghyaracanāsvakīyairambhobhiḥ salilanidhisauhityakaraṇaṃtvadīyābhirvāgbhistava janani vācāṃ stutiriyam ‖100‖
Like waving a lamp before the sun, like offering the moon water from the moonstone that the moon itself makes flow, like satisfying the ocean with its own waters: this hymn of praise to you, Mother of all speech, is made of words that are yours.
WordMeans
pradīpa-jvālābhiḥ … nīrājanawaving lamps (before the sun)
candropala-jalawater from the moonstone

VERSE 100 · DEEP DIVE

HER OWN WORDS, OFFERED BACK

🔎 In context

The hymn ends with three images of perfect humility. A lamp waved before the sun adds no light; water given back to the moon, from the moonstone that the moon makes sweat, adds nothing; the ocean cannot be filled with its own water. So too this hymn: every word in it was hers to begin with.

📜 Looking deeper

In the Vaidika Vignanam text a closing verse follows, praising the Saundaryalaharī as the chief of hymns, composed by the Bhagavatpāda (Śaṅkara), and promising liberation to the one who recites it; then the colophon naming Śaṅkara as its author. This last verse of the poem itself remains its most loved: an offering returned to its source.

🌱 For today

Whatever we offer was given to us first.

↑ VERSE 100 · Her own words, offered back to her.
