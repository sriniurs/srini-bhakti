THE ŚRĪCAKRA

The Goddess as a diagram

ŚRĪCAKRA · OVERVIEW

FOREWORD

A point. Nine triangles crossing it. Two rings of petals, three circles and a square with four gates. This is the Śrīcakra, the most famous yantra in India and the centre of Śrīvidyā worship.

To the tradition it is not a picture but Lalitā herself, her palace and her body. This overview explains its parts, its nine enclosures and their goddesses, how to read it outward and inward, and where it lives in music and temples.

ZAPPY: Is the Śrīcakra a picture of the Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition says it IS the Goddess, in the form of a diagram. Not a picture of her body; her body as geometry.

The Śrīcakra is the most famous yantra in India and the centre of Śrīvidyā worship. To an outsider it is a striking geometric design; to the tradition it is the Goddess herself, her palace, her body and the map of the universe, all at once.

This overview introduces its parts, its nine enclosures, the goddesses who rule them, its two forms, its history and its place in music and temples. Nine further guides take the enclosures one by one, from the outer gate to the central point. A guide to the Bhāvanopaniṣad, which reads the Śrīcakra as the human body, will follow when its text is in hand.

As throughout this series, the geometry, deities, names and meanings are described in full. The ritual of Śrīcakra worship, with its mantras and procedures, is left to teachers.

THE NAME

Cakra, yantra, king
CakraWheel, circle
YantraAn instrument; a diagram used in worship
Śrīcakra · ŚrīyantraThe wheel, or diagram, of Śrī
Cakrarāja“King of cakras”: also the name of Lalitā’s chariot
Navayoni-cakra“Nine-womb wheel”: from the nine triangles
Navāvaraṇa“Nine enclosures”: the layers worshipped one by one

ZAPPY: Her chariot and her diagram have the same name?

PROF. BOLT: Cakrarāja. The chariot she rides to war and the diagram on the altar are one idea: her moving palace.

Indian worship uses three kinds of support for the Divine: the image (mūrti), the sacred sound (mantra) and the diagram (yantra). The Śrīcakra is the supreme example of the third, so revered that it is called the king of yantras.

Its name Cakrarāja is shared with Lalitā’s chariot in the Lalitopākhyāna, and that is no coincidence. Commentators read the chariot’s nine tiers as the Śrīcakra’s nine enclosures. The Goddess rides her yantra into battle.

Navayoni-cakra, “wheel of nine wombs”, refers to the nine triangles, read as sources of the world. Navāvaraṇa, “nine enclosures”, names the structure that worship follows.

The word yantra itself is instructive. It comes from a root meaning to hold, restrain or control, the same root found in words for machines and instruments. A yantra holds the mind steady on the Divine, as a machine holds and directs force.

THE NINE TRIANGLES

Four and five
UpwardFour triangles pointing up: Śiva, fire, the masculine
DownwardFive triangles pointing down: Śakti, water, the feminine
InterlockingTheir crossings form forty-three small triangles
Not all meetThe design is famously hard to draw so that every line meets exactly
MeaningThe world arises where Śiva and Śakti interpenetrate
NameNavayoni: nine sources, nine wombs

ZAPPY: Forty-three triangles from nine?

PROF. BOLT: Count them sometime. Nine big ones, crossing, make forty-three small ones, plus the one at the centre that holds the point.

The heart of the design is nine triangles: four pointing upward and five pointing downward. The upward ones are associated with Śiva and fire, the downward ones with Śakti and water. Interlocking, they produce a field of forty-three small triangles around a central one.

The image is a geometric statement of the Śiva-Śakti philosophy described in the Foundations. The world arises not from either principle alone but from their interpenetration. Five for Śakti and four for Śiva makes nine, and the extra Śakti triangle is read as the dynamism of the Goddess.

Drawing the triangles so that their lines meet exactly is famously difficult, a problem discussed later in this guide.

ZAPPY: Do I have to remember all those names?

PROF. BOLT: Just notice the pattern: each starts with ‘sarva’, all. All hopes, all fortune, all aims, all protection, all bliss. Each layer gives everything, in its own way.

Āvaraṇa 1: Trailokyamohana
Āvaraṇa 2: Sarvāśāparipūraka
Āvaraṇa 3: Sarvasaṅkṣobhaṇa

The nine enclosures, or āvaraṇas, run from the outer square to the central point. Each has a name beginning with sarva, “all”: the enchanter of the three worlds, the fulfiller of all hopes, the stirrer of all, the giver of all good fortune, the accomplisher of all aims, the protector of all, the remover of all ills, the giver of all attainments, and the one made of all bliss.

Each enclosure is a ring of goddesses, from twenty-eight in the first to the single couple at the centre. Worship moves through them in order, honouring each before moving inward.

The nine guides that follow this overview take one enclosure each: its shape, its presiding Tripurā, its class of yoginīs, its goddesses by name, its meaning, and the Dīkṣitar song that celebrates it.

WHO RULES EACH LAYER

4–6Tripuravāsinī · Tripurāśrī · Tripuramālinī; Sampradāya, Kulottīrṇa, Nigarbha yoginīs
7–9Tripurāsiddhā · Tripurāmbā · Mahātripurasundarī; Rahasya, Atirahasya, Parāpararahasya
PatternThe yoginī names grow more hidden as you move inward: manifest, hidden, more hidden… supreme secret
KhaḍgamālāThe hymn that names every deity of the Śrīcakra in order

ZAPPY: The goddesses get more secret as you go in?

PROF. BOLT: That's the point of an enclosure. The outer ones are ‘manifest’, anyone can see them. The inner ones are ‘secret’, ‘most secret’, until the centre, which is beyond secret.

Each enclosure has a presiding goddess, a cakreśvarī, and all nine are forms of Tripurā: Tripurā, Tripureśī, Tripurasundarī, Tripuravāsinī, Tripurāśrī, Tripuramālinī, Tripurāsiddhā, Tripurāmbā and, at the centre, Mahātripurasundarī, Lalitā herself.

Each enclosure also has a class of yoginīs, named for their degree of hiddenness: prakaṭa, manifest; gupta, hidden; guptatara, more hidden; sampradāya, of the tradition; kulottīrṇa, beyond the clan; nigarbha, inmost; rahasya, secret; atirahasya, most secret; and parāpararahasya, the supreme secret.

The Devī Khaḍgamālā, a popular hymn, names every deity of the Śrīcakra in order, enclosure by enclosure. Many devotees know the Śrīcakra first through that hymn.

The cakreśvarīs’ names follow a pattern: each is Tripurā with a different epithet: the ruler, the beautiful, the dweller, the glorious, the garlanded, the accomplished, the mother. Read in sequence, they describe one Goddess approached ever more closely, until at the centre she is simply Mahātripurasundarī, the great beauty of the three cities.

THREE GROUPS OF THREE

Creation, sustenance, dissolution
Outer threeEnclosures 1–3: the cakras of creation
Middle threeEnclosures 4–6: the cakras of sustenance
Inner threeEnclosures 7–9: the cakras of dissolution, returning to the source
TripurāThree in three: the many triads of Tripurā
PatternEach group of three echoes the whole
NoteGroupings are traditional; texts differ in detail

ZAPPY: So the Śrīcakra has a beginning, middle and end?

PROF. BOLT: Like a story. Creation at the edge, the world held in the middle, everything gathered back at the centre.

Śrīvidyā writers commonly group the nine enclosures into three sets of three. The outer three are the cakras of creation, where the One unfolds into the world. The middle three are the cakras of sustenance, where the world is held and protected. The inner three are the cakras of dissolution, where everything returns to its source.

The pattern of threes within threes echoes Tripurā’s name and the many triads of the tradition: three cities, three gods, three powers of will, knowledge and action, three states of waking, dream and sleep.

Texts differ in detail, and some lineages arrange the enclosures differently. The broad picture of creation, sustenance and dissolution is widely shared.

OUTWARD AND INWARD

Two ways to read it
CreationFrom the bindu outward: the one becomes many, layer by layer
WorshipFrom the gate inward: the many return to the one
The worshipperEnters by the outer square and travels in
The GoddessIs present at every layer, as different powers
The goalThe bindu: union with Lalitā and Kāmeśvara
Our seriesFollows the worshipper’s road: enclosure 1 to 9

ZAPPY: Which way should I read it?

PROF. BOLT: Both. It's a map of how the world came out, and the road back in.

The Śrīcakra can be read in two directions. In the order of creation, the bindu at the centre expands outward: the point becomes a triangle, the triangles multiply, the petals open, the circles and the square form the boundary of the world. This is the One becoming many.

In the order of worship, the movement is reversed. The worshipper enters through the gates of the outer square and travels inward, honouring each enclosure, until reaching the bindu. This is the many returning to the One.

This series follows the worshipper’s road. Guide 1 is the outer square; guide 9 is the point.

FLAT AND RAISED

Bhūprastāra and Meru
FlatDrawn, painted or engraved on a plate: bhūprastāra
RaisedA three-dimensional pyramid, each layer stepping up: the Meru
MaterialsGold, silver, copper, crystal, and stone in temples
Meru linkThe raised form echoes Mount Meru, on which Śrīpura stands
Walk-in MeruAt Devīpuram, a temple built as a Meru that devotees enter
CareAn old Śrīcakra is honoured, not thrown away

ZAPPY: A Śrīcakra you can walk into?

PROF. BOLT: At Devīpuram near Visakhapatnam, yes: a temple in the shape of the Meru form. The diagram became a building.

The Śrīcakra exists in two main forms. The flat form, bhūprastāra, is drawn, painted or engraved on a surface such as a metal plate. The raised form, the Meru, builds the layers upward into a stepped pyramid, with the bindu at the summit.

The Meru form echoes Mount Meru, on whose central peak the Lalitopākhyāna places Śrīpura. In the Meru the city on the mountain becomes a single object.

Śrīcakras are made of gold, silver, copper, crystal and stone. Old ones are never casually discarded; when worn smooth they are respectfully immersed in water, as a sacred image would be.

A third form, sometimes called kailāsa, is mentioned in some texts, combining features of the flat and raised designs. Lineages differ on which form they prefer for daily worship; many households keep a small flat plate, while temples often have a stone or metal Meru.

ZAPPY: The war chariot was a Śrīcakra on wheels?

PROF. BOLT: That's the reading. She rode her own yantra into battle.

Story: Śrīpura · the chariots

The Lalitopākhyāna describes the Goddess’s city in concentric rings and her chariot in nine tiers, each peopled by named goddesses. The Śrīcakra presents the same structure as a diagram. Commentators treat the three as one plan at three scales.

This is why the story and the yantra illuminate each other. Many of the goddesses who appear in the chariots of the war reappear in the enclosures of the Śrīcakra; the walls of Śrīpura correspond to its outer layers; the Cintāmaṇi palace and the couch of five Brahmas correspond to its centre.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma moves from story to yantra in its later names, naming the enclosures and their deities as places where the Goddess dwells.

ZAPPY: How old is it?

PROF. BOLT: As a fully drawn Śrīcakra, about a thousand years, historians say. Tradition traces it much further back.

The Śrīcakra as Śrīvidyā knows it is described in detail in the Vāmakeśvara Tantra, especially its second half, the Yoginīhṛdaya, and in the Tantrarāja. The Kāmakalāvilāsa gives its philosophical reading as the unfolding of consciousness.

Tradition holds that Ādi Śaṅkara installed Śrīcakras in the great temples of Kāñcī and Śṛṅgeri. Historians, as the Foundations guide on Śaṅkara explains, date the full Śrīvidyā system to the medieval Tantric period, around a thousand years ago.

Since then it has become one of the most widespread sacred images in India, found in temples, monasteries and homes, and increasingly abroad.

Outside India, the Śrīcakra has become one of the best-known Indian sacred designs, reproduced in books on sacred geometry, art and meditation. Much of that use is detached from its tradition. This series tries to keep the design and its meaning together.

A GEOMETRY PUZZLE

Why it is hard to draw
The problemMake the nine triangles cross so that their lines meet exactly at many points
TraditionManuals give step-by-step constructions
MathematiciansHave studied it as a problem of near-perfect concurrency
ResultPrecise versions need careful calculation; many printed ones are approximate
Our drawingA stylised version, not a measured one
LessonIts beauty is in precision; treat cheap printed versions with care

ZAPPY: So most pictures of it are slightly wrong?

PROF. BOLT: Many are. A perfect Śrīcakra is hard to draw. That's partly why making one is itself a devotional act.

The Śrīcakra’s geometry is genuinely difficult. The nine triangles must cross so that many sets of lines meet at single points, and so that the small triangles they form are well shaped. Traditional manuals give careful step-by-step constructions.

Modern mathematicians have studied the design as a problem in near-perfect concurrency, showing that precise versions require careful calculation and that many printed versions are approximations. The puzzle has fascinated both devotees and geometers.

The drawings in this series are stylised illustrations, meant to show the structure clearly, not measured constructions. Devotees who make or buy a Śrīcakra for worship usually seek one made with care by a traditional craftsman.

There is a devotional point in the difficulty. Making a correct Śrīcakra demands patience, precision and attention, the same qualities its worship asks for. Many craftsmen who make them for temples describe the work as a form of worship in itself.

IN MUSIC

ComposerMuttusvāmi Dīkṣitar (1775–1835), a Śrīvidyā initiate
GoddessKamalāmbā of Tiruvārūr
SongsAn invocation, nine songs for the nine enclosures, and a closing blessing
GrammarThe nine songs use the Sanskrit noun cases in order: “Kamalāmbā”, “to Kamalāmbā”, “by Kamalāmbā”…

ZAPPY: Songs that teach the Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: Nine of them, one per layer, in nine rāgas. A tour of the Goddess's palace you can sing.

Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar, one of the trinity of Carnatic music and an initiate of Śrīvidyā, composed a cycle of eleven songs to Kamalāmbā, the Goddess of Tiruvārūr: an invocation, nine songs for the nine enclosures, and a closing blessing. They are known as the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis.

The nine enclosure songs use the Sanskrit noun cases in order. The first addresses Kamalāmbā in the nominative, the second in the accusative, then instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, locative and vocative, and the ninth returns to the nominative. Each song names its enclosure, its Tripurā, its yoginīs and its deities.

Each āvaraṇa guide in this series describes its song: its opening words, its rāga as commonly sung, its case and what it names. A verse-by-verse guide to the whole cycle will follow when its text is supplied.

ZAPPY: Śrīcakras in earrings?

PROF. BOLT: Akhilāṇḍeśvarī's tāṭaṅkas, by tradition, carry Śrīcakras that calmed her fierce form. Her own guide comes in the Temples section.

Many of the great Goddess temples of the South worship a Śrīcakra along with the image. At Kāñcī Kāmākṣī it stands before the Goddess; at Śṛṅgeri it is worshipped in the Śāradā temple and the maṭha; at Tiruvānaikkāval, tradition says, Śaṅkara set Śrīcakras into the earrings of Akhilāṇḍeśvarī to calm her fierce form.

Kollūr Mūkāmbikā, Madurai Mīnākṣī and Tiruvārūr Kamalāmbā all have Śrīvidyā associations. Devīpuram, near Visakhapatnam, built in recent decades, is a walk-in Meru temple.

Each of these temples has, or will have, its own guide in the Temples section of this series.

Visitors to any of these temples will rarely see the Śrīcakra clearly. It is often covered with flowers and kumkum, set in the floor before the deity, or kept within the sanctum. Its presence is felt more than seen.

WHAT IT IS NOT

Clearing up mix-ups
Not a lucky charmSold as a wealth plate; the tradition sees it as the Goddess’s body
Not mere decorationA diagram for worship and meditation
Not only Hindu-geometry triviaIts meaning is the whole of Śrīvidyā’s philosophy
Not secretIts form is public; its full worship is taught by teachers
Not one-size-fits-allLineages differ on details of drawing and worship

ZAPPY: Can I keep one at home?

PROF. BOLT: Many people do, with respect: a clean place, a lamp, flowers. The tradition only asks that you treat it as her, not as an ornament.

Śrīcakra plates are widely sold as charms for wealth and success. The tradition does not deny that worship brings blessings, but it insists that the Śrīcakra is the Goddess’s own form, to be treated with the respect due to her image, not used as a lucky object.

Its form is entirely public: anyone may see it, draw it and study its geometry, and this series describes it in full. What lineages keep for initiation is the detailed ritual of its worship, with its mantras and procedures.

Details of its construction and worship differ between lineages, and readers should not be troubled by small variations between pictures.

A useful rule of thumb for readers: an account of the Śrīcakra that leads to reverence, study and meditation is close to the tradition; an account that promises quick results for money is not.
