SARVARAKṢĀKARA

“Protector of all”

ŚRĪCAKRA · ĀVARAṆA 6 OF 9

FOREWORD

Inside the outer ring of ten lies another ring of ten, smaller and closer to the centre. This is the sixth enclosure, Sarvarakṣākara, “the protector of all”.

Its goddesses are no longer just givers. They are all-knowing, all-powerful, the support of everything and protection itself. This guide names them and shows how the Goddess’s own nature begins to show through.

Śrī Tripuramālinyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Protection from what?

PROF. BOLT: From everything that could stop you reaching the centre. Including yourself.

ZAPPY: The inner ring of ten?

PROF. BOLT: Ten goddesses of protection. The last ring before the centre begins to close.

The sixth āvaraṇa is the inner of the two rings of ten triangles, called antardaśāra. Its name, Sarvarakṣākara, means “protector of all”, and its presiding goddess is Tripuramālinī, with the nigarbha or inmost yoginīs.

Its ten goddesses are all-knowing, all-powerful, giver of all sovereignty, made of all knowledge, destroyer of all disease, the support of all, remover of all fault, made of all bliss, protection itself, and giver of every desired fruit.

Its mudrā-goddess is Sarvamahāṅkuśā, “the great goad of all”, and its siddhi is Prākāmya, the fulfilment of will. It is the last of the three cakras of sustenance.

Dīkṣitar’s song for it is “Kamalāmbikāyās tava”, in Punnāgavarāḷi.

ZAPPY: Like a rakhi?

PROF. BOLT: Same word, rakṣā. A thread of protection. This ring is the Goddess tying one round the whole world.

Rakṣā means protection, guarding, safekeeping. The same word gives Rakṣā Bandhan, the festival at which a sister ties a protective thread on her brother’s wrist, and the many protective threads tied at temples.

Kara means maker or doer. The protector of all is the Goddess as the one who keeps everything safe: the world in its order, the seeker on the path.

After the fifth enclosure’s accomplishment comes the sixth’s protection. Having reached a goal, one needs to keep it.

Many devotees who pray to the Goddess for protection for their families are, in this picture, approaching her through the sixth enclosure.

ZAPPY: Why two rings of ten?

PROF. BOLT: One accomplishes, one protects. Getting there, and being kept safe once there.

The inner ten-cornered figure lies just inside the outer one, smaller and tighter. Together they form a double ring of twenty small triangles around the eight triangles of the seventh enclosure.

The twin rings make a pair: the outer accomplishes, the inner protects. In many lineages they are worshipped one after the other as a matched set.

From here the remaining enclosures are few and small: eight triangles, one triangle, one point. The yantra is closing toward its centre.

In a good drawing, the antardaśāra’s small triangles are noticeably more slender than those of the outer ring, a sign of how the design narrows.

ZAPPY: ‘Inmost’ yoginīs?

PROF. BOLT: Nigarbha: within the womb, the very inside. We're nearly at the heart.

The presiding goddess is Tripuramālinī, “she who is garlanded with the three cities” or “she who wears the three cities as a garland”. The image suggests the Goddess wearing the worlds lightly, as an ornament.

Her yoginīs are nigarbha, “inmost” or “within the womb”. The word suggests the protected interior where life is safest. Protection is the theme of the whole enclosure.

The mudrā-goddess Sarvamahāṅkuśā, “the great goad of all”, and the siddhi Prākāmya, the fulfilment of will, suggest guidance and fulfilment within a protected space.

All nine presiding goddesses are forms of one Tripurā, and devotees who recite their names in order, from Tripurā at the gate to Mahātripurasundarī at the centre, often describe it as approaching the same queen through ever more intimate titles, as a visitor to a palace is announced by ever higher officers.

ZAPPY: These are bigger than the last ring's.

PROF. BOLT: They are. Last time: gifts and rescues. Now: all-knowing, all-powerful, all-supporting, protection itself. The Goddess's own nature showing through.

The ten goddesses of this ring describe the Goddess’s own nature more than her gifts. Sarvajñā and Sarvaśakti, all-knowing and all-powerful, are attributes of the supreme reality. Sarvaiśvaryapradā gives all sovereignty; Sarvajñānamayī is made of all knowledge.

Sarvavyādhivināśinī destroys disease; Sarvapāpaharā removes fault. Sarvādhārasvarūpā is the support of all, the ground on which everything rests. Sarvānandamayī is made of all bliss, a foretaste of the centre, which is called Sarvānandamaya.

The last two are Sarvarakṣāsvarūpiṇī, protection itself, and Sarvepsitaphalapradā, giver of every desired fruit. All ten are named in the Devī Khaḍgamālā.

ZAPPY: So this ring shows who she really is?

PROF. BOLT: It starts to. All-knowing, all-powerful, the support of everything, bliss itself. The closer you get, the more she shows.

Compared with the fifth enclosure, the sixth goes deeper. The fifth listed gifts and rescues; the sixth names the Goddess’s own attributes: omniscience, omnipotence, sovereignty, knowledge, bliss. As the seeker approaches the centre, the Goddess reveals more of who she is.

The protection offered here is not a separate service but an expression of that nature. The all-knowing, all-powerful support of everything is, by her very being, protection.

A common bodily reading, from summaries of the Bhāvanopaniṣad, links the ten triangles with ten forms of the body’s inner fire, the warmth that digests, transforms and sustains life. Protection, in that reading, is the body’s own life kept burning.

For the devotee, the enclosure says: you are held. The one who is protection itself surrounds you.

ZAPPY: End of the middle section?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Sustenance ends with protection. Next come the three cakras of dissolution, where everything returns.

The sixth enclosure closes the cakras of sustenance. In creation’s order, it is the world held safe in being; in worship’s order, it is the seeker sheltered before the final approach.

Beyond it lie the three cakras of dissolution, where the many return to the one: the eight triangles, the central triangle and the bindu.

The journey so far: enchantment, gathering and stirring in the outer three; blessedness, accomplishment and protection in the middle three. What remains is healing, attainment and bliss.

In a temple, this is the antechamber of the sanctum: the last hall before the deity, quiet and protected.

Many devotees who never perform Śrīcakra worship still use the nine enclosures as a meditation: pausing at each ring in imagination, greeting its goddesses, and moving inward. The guides in this series are laid out to support that kind of reading, one enclosure at a time.

ZAPPY: Genitive: ‘of Kamalāmbā’?

PROF. BOLT: ‘Of you, Kamalāmbikā’: the song speaks of what belongs to her. Her protection, her glory.

Dīkṣitar’s sixth song is “Kamalāmbikāyās tava”, commonly sung in Punnāgavarāḷi. It uses the genitive case, speaking of what is Kamalāmbikā’s own, and names the inner ten-cornered enclosure, Tripuramālinī and the nigarbha yoginīs.

Punnāgavarāḷi is a gentle, slightly melancholy rāga, associated in folk practice with snake-charming. Its intimacy suits a layer of shelter and protection.

The genitive, “of you”, fits the enclosure’s theme: this is where the Goddess shows what belongs to her own nature.

Dīkṣitar was born at Tiruvārūr, the town of Kamalāmbā, and the cycle is closely tied to her temple there. The songs are performed widely, often during Navarātri, when some musicians sing one each night, and the whole cycle is a staple of concerts devoted to the Goddess.

ZAPPY: Is the fire-wall this layer?

PROF. BOLT: In spirit. After the night attack, the camp is ringed with protection. This enclosure is that ring drawn in the yantra.

After the night attack in Part 4, the officers are ashamed of leaving the queen exposed. In Part 5, Jvālāmālinī rings the whole camp with a wall of fire, and the officers arrange the chariots so that the queen is protected on every side. The sixth enclosure is that protecting ring.

The story also shows the Goddess as healer. After the night battle, her merciful glance heals the wounded Nityās. In the final duel she answers Bhaṇḍa’s weapon of great diseases with the three names of Viṣṇu.

In Śrīpura, the Cintāmaṇi palace is surrounded by the quarters of its guardians and officers, close to the queen. The sixth enclosure corresponds to that inner guard.

Readers who want the whole story can turn to the six-part Lalitopākhyāna retelling in this series. The Śrīcakra guides and the story guides are written to be read side by side: the city, the chariots and the war on one hand, the yantra and its enclosures on the other.

THE INNER READING

The enclosure within
Ten firesA common bodily reading: ten forms of the body’s inner fire, from digestion to transformation
ProtectionThe practitioner guarded from within

KnowledgeThe all-knowing goddess as inner insight
Guide to comeThe Bhāvanopaniṣad’s reading

ZAPPY: Fires inside me?

PROF. BOLT: The body's warmth, digestion, transformation. Ten fires, ten goddesses, keeping you alive and safe.

In the bodily reading, the ten triangles are ten forms of inner fire: the warmth of digestion, of transformation, of the body’s processes. The Goddess as protector keeps them burning.

Many teachers also read this enclosure as the protection of practice itself. Near the centre, the seeker is vulnerable to subtle obstacles; the Goddess’s protection holds them steady.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad’s detailed reading will be set out in its own guide.

For a devotee, the simplest response to this enclosure is trust: to rest in the Goddess’s protection rather than anxiously guarding oneself.
