SARVASAṄKṢOBHAṆA

“Stirrer of all”

ŚRĪCAKRA · ĀVARAṆA 3 OF 9

FOREWORD

Inside the sixteen petals opens a smaller lotus of eight. This is the third enclosure, Sarvasaṅkṣobhaṇa, “the stirrer of all”, the last of the outer three.

Its eight goddesses all bear the name Anaṅga, “bodiless”, the name of Kāma after Śiva burned him. This guide shows how the god of love, burned at the start of Lalitā’s story, lives on in her palace as eight powers of love.

Śrī Tripurasundaryai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Eight petals now?

PROF. BOLT: Eight petals, eight goddesses, all named after the bodiless god of love.

The third āvaraṇa is the eight-petalled lotus inside the sixteen. Its name, Sarvasaṅkṣobhaṇa, means “stirrer of all”, and its presiding goddess is Tripurasundarī, with the guptatara or more hidden yoginīs.

Its eight goddesses are named after Anaṅga, “the bodiless one”: Kāma, the god of love, after Śiva burned his body. Each is a power of love: its flower, its girdle, its intoxication, its longing, its mark, its speed, its goad, its garland.

Its mudrā-goddess is Sarvākarṣiṇī, “she who attracts all”, and its siddhi is Mahimā, greatness. It is the last of the three cakras of creation.

Dīkṣitar’s song for it is “Śrī Kamalāmbikayā kaṭākṣito’ham”, in Śaṅkarābharaṇam.

ZAPPY: Why ‘stirring’? Isn't spiritual life about calm?

PROF. BOLT: Calm comes later. First the heart has to be stirred, moved by love. This layer is the shake that gets you going.

Saṅkṣobhaṇa means stirring, agitating or shaking. It may seem a strange quality for a spiritual layer, since the goal is often described as stillness. But the tradition sees a stage before stillness when the heart must be moved, shaken out of indifference by love.

The second enclosure gathered the faculties. The third sets them in motion. Gathered but unmoved, the seeker would stay where they are; stirred by love, they begin to travel inward.

In creation’s order, too, stirring is what turns the inner powers into a world. Desire, the first stirring, sets everything moving outward.

The name of the presiding goddess, Tripurasundarī, “the beautiful one”, fits a layer of love: beauty is what stirs the heart.

ZAPPY: Smaller lotus inside the bigger one?

PROF. BOLT: Each step in is smaller and nearer the centre. Sixteen petals, then eight, then the triangles begin.

The eight-petalled lotus lies inside the sixteen-petalled one, its petals often a deeper red. Within it, the triangles of the inner Śrīcakra begin.

Eight is the number of the directions, including the intermediate ones, and of many groups of goddesses: the eight Mothers, the eight Lakṣmīs, the eight goddesses of speech who appear further inward. Here it is the eight powers of love.

The progression of the outer three enclosures is clear in the shapes: a square with gates, then a large open lotus, then a smaller, more concentrated one. The journey narrows as it deepens.

In many temple Śrīcakras, the eight petals are where the eye first rests: the outer square and sixteen petals frame them, and the dense lattice of triangles lies just inside. Painters often give this lotus the strongest colour of the whole design.

ZAPPY: ‘More hidden’ yoginīs now?

PROF. BOLT: Guptatara: more hidden than the last. The powers here are subtler than the senses: the movements of love inside.

The presiding goddess of the third enclosure is Tripurasundarī, the beautiful one of the three cities. It is the name by which Śrīvidyā’s Goddess is best known, and it appears here, at the end of the outer cakras, as if the seeker were first catching sight of her beauty.

Its yoginīs are guptatara, “more hidden”. After the manifest powers of the gate and the hidden powers of experience, these are the subtler powers of feeling and longing.

The mudrā-goddess Sarvākarṣiṇī, “she who attracts all”, and the siddhi Mahimā, “greatness”, both suit a layer of love: attraction, and the heart enlarged by it.

ZAPPY: Who is ‘the bodiless one’?

PROF. BOLT: Anaṅga: Kāma, the god of love, after Śiva burned his body. Love without a body. All eight goddesses are his powers, held by the Goddess.

All eight goddesses bear the prefix Anaṅga. Anaṅgakusumā is love’s flower; Anaṅgamekhalā its girdle, the embrace; Anaṅgamadanā its intoxication; Anaṅgamadanāturā the one made love-sick by it; Anaṅgarekhā its line or mark; Anaṅgaveginī its swiftness; Anaṅgāṅkuśā its goad; Anaṅgamālinī its garland.

Together they describe love’s whole course: its beauty, its embrace, its overwhelming force, its longing, the trace it leaves, its speed, its urgency and its adornment.

All eight are named in the Devī Khaḍgamālā, recited as the third enclosure.

ZAPPY: So this layer is about love?

PROF. BOLT: Love turned toward the Goddess. The same Kāma who was burned in the story lives here, bodiless, as eight powers that move the heart.

The key to this enclosure is the name Anaṅga. In the Lalitopākhyāna, Kāma is burned by Śiva and loses his body; from his ash comes Bhaṇḍa. At the end of the story Lalitā restores him. In between, Kāma is anaṅga, bodiless, and in the Śrīcakra his bodiless powers stand on eight petals as goddesses.

Bodiless love is love freed from mere physical desire: longing, devotion, delight, the pull of the heart toward what it loves. The tradition does not throw desire away; it refines it and turns it to the Goddess.

Commentators give more technical readings too, linking the eight goddesses to faculties of action and expression, or to states of mind. These vary; the central idea of love made subtle is common to all.

For the devotee, the enclosure says: let longing for the Goddess move you. Love is the vehicle of the journey.

ZAPPY: Last of the creation cakras?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Creation in this picture ends with love: the world is stirred into being by desire. And the seeker's return begins with love too.

The third enclosure completes the cakras of creation. In creation’s order, it is where the inner powers first stir into outward movement; in worship’s order, it is where the gathered seeker is moved by love to go further.

From here the Śrīcakra changes character. The lotuses give way to triangles, the cakras of creation to the cakras of sustenance, and the yoginīs of the hidden kind to those of the tradition.

In a temple, this would be the threshold of the inner sanctum’s courtyard: quieter, closer, with the deity almost in view.

The journey so far: enchantment recognised at the gate, the self gathered on sixteen petals, the heart stirred on eight.

A pattern is visible across the three outer enclosures. At the gate, the world’s powers; on sixteen petals, the person’s faculties; on eight, the heart’s longing. World, self, love: the outer cakras move from outside to inside the seeker.

ZAPPY: Instrumental case?

PROF. BOLT: ‘By Kamalāmbā’: ‘I am blessed by Kamalāmbā's glance.’ The third song says what she does to you.

Dīkṣitar’s third song is “Śrī Kamalāmbikayā kaṭākṣito’ham”, commonly sung in Śaṅkarābharaṇam. It uses the instrumental case: “I am blessed by the glance of Kamalāmbikā”. It names the enclosure, Tripurasundarī and the Anaṅga goddesses.

Śaṅkarābharaṇam, one of the great parent rāgas of Carnatic music, is grand and bright, a fitting sound for the layer where the Goddess’s beauty first appears.

The kaṭākṣa, the sidelong glance, is a favourite image of the Goddess’s grace in Śrīvidyā poetry: a single glance that changes everything. It is also the glance that, in the Lalitopākhyāna, heals the Nityās and creates Gaṇeśa.

Listeners who follow the cycle in order will hear the third song as the end of the first movement. After it, the songs for the triangles begin, and the music, like the Śrīcakra, grows more concentrated.

ZAPPY: So Kāma ends up in the Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: His powers do, as eight goddesses on eight petals. The god who started the whole story is part of her palace.

The Lalitopākhyāna begins with Kāma burned by Śiva’s third eye and ends with Lalitā restoring him to life. The third enclosure holds the middle of that arc: Kāma as Anaṅga, bodiless but powerful, his powers serving the Goddess.

Lalitā herself holds Kāma’s weapons, the sugarcane bow and the five flower arrows, given to her at her wedding. The Anaṅga goddesses are the same power distributed in her palace.

In Śrīpura, between the walls, are gardens of heavenly trees. The eight-petalled lotus is the innermost of the Śrīcakra’s gardens before the triangles begin.

There is a pleasing irony in it. The god whose burning produced the demon Bhaṇḍa is, in the Goddess’s palace, part of her court. What was a cause of disaster becomes, in the right hands, a ring of goddesses of devotion.

ZAPPY: So I need to be ‘stirred’?

PROF. BOLT: Gathered, then stirred. A collected mind with no longing goes nowhere. This layer adds the love that moves it.

In the inner reading, the third enclosure is the heart moved by longing. Many teachers say this stirring is necessary: a calm but cold practice goes nowhere, and devotion is the fuel of the journey.

Bodiless love also suggests a maturing of desire. The seeker is not asked to kill desire, as Śiva killed Kāma, with disastrous results in the story. Desire loses its body, its grasping, and becomes longing for the Goddess.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad’s bodily reading of this lotus will be set out in its own guide.

For a devotee without a technical practice, the third enclosure suggests something simple: let yourself love. Music, poetry, temple festivals and the Goddess’s beauty in images are all ways the heart is stirred, and the tradition counts them as part of the path.
