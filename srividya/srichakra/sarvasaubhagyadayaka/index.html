SARVASAUBHĀGYADĀYAKA

“Giver of all good fortune”

ŚRĪCAKRA · ĀVARAṆA 4 OF 9

FOREWORD

Past the lotuses, the triangles begin. Their outermost ring has fourteen small triangles, and this is the fourth enclosure, Sarvasaubhāgyadāyaka, “giver of all good fortune”.

Its fourteen goddesses run from stirring and delighting to granting every aim and, last of all, destroying the pairs of opposites. This guide names them and asks what good fortune means in Śrīvidyā.

ZAPPY: Now we're in the triangles?

PROF. BOLT: The outermost ring of them: fourteen small triangles, fourteen goddesses of good fortune.

The fourth āvaraṇa is the outermost ring of the inner triangles: fourteen small triangles formed where the tips of the nine great triangles cross. It is called Sarvasaubhāgyadāyaka, “giver of all good fortune”, and its presiding goddess is Tripuravāsinī, with the sampradāya or traditional yoginīs.

Its fourteen goddesses each bear a name beginning with sarva, all: she who stirs all, melts all, attracts all, delights all, and so on, to she who destroys all pairs of opposites.

Its mudrā-goddess is Sarvavaśaṅkarī, “she who brings all under control”, and its siddhi is Īśitva, lordship. It opens the three cakras of sustenance.

Dīkṣitar’s song for it is “Kamalāmbikāyai”, in Kāmbhoji.

ZAPPY: Good fortune, like luck?

PROF. BOLT: Bigger than luck. Saubhāgya is blessedness: a life that's whole and auspicious. Śrīvidyā is even called the Saubhāgya tradition by some.

Saubhāgya is one of the richest words in Śrīvidyā. It means good fortune and auspiciousness, but also the blessed state of a happily married woman, whose husband lives and whose household flourishes. Some writers call Śrīvidyā itself the saubhāgya-vidyā, the knowledge of blessedness.

The Goddess as giver of saubhāgya is Lalitā the married queen, joined to Kāmeśvara. Her blessedness is not renunciation but fulfilment.

Bhāskararāya’s great commentary on the Sahasranāma is titled Saubhāgyabhāskara, “the sun of saubhāgya”, which shows how central the word is to the tradition.

The name of this enclosure thus sums up Śrīvidyā’s outlook: the Goddess gives every kind of blessedness, worldly and spiritual together.

ZAPPY: Where do these fourteen triangles come from?

PROF. BOLT: From the nine big triangles crossing. Their outer tips make this ring of fourteen.

The fourteen-cornered figure, caturdaśāra, is not drawn separately. It appears where the nine great triangles cross, their outer points forming a ring of small triangles around the inner design.

Fourteen is the number of worlds in Purāṇic cosmology, seven above and seven below, and some readings take the ring as the whole cosmos. Other readings, especially the Bhāvanopaniṣad’s, take it as fourteen channels of the subtle body.

This is the first enclosure built from triangles rather than petals or lines. From here inward, the design is the interplay of Śiva and Śakti triangles.

Its position, just inside the eight petals, makes it the threshold between the lotus gardens and the triangular heart of the yantra.

ZAPPY: ‘Traditional’ yoginīs?

PROF. BOLT: Sampradāya: of the lineage. From here on the powers are the ones a tradition hands down.

The presiding goddess of the fourth enclosure is Tripuravāsinī, “she who dwells in the three cities”. The name suggests the Goddess at home, settled, residing: a fitting ruler for the first cakra of sustenance.

Its yoginīs are sampradāya, “of the tradition” or “handed down”. From this layer inward, the powers are those that lineages transmit, a reminder that the inner Śrīcakra is approached with a teacher.

The mudrā-goddess Sarvavaśaṅkarī, “she who brings all under control”, and the siddhi Īśitva, lordship, both speak of sovereignty, the queen governing her realm.

All nine presiding goddesses are forms of one Tripurā, and devotees who recite their names in order, from Tripurā at the gate to Mahātripurasundarī at the centre, often describe it as approaching the same queen through ever more intimate titles, as a visitor to a palace is announced by ever higher officers.

ZAPPY: Some of these sound like the mudrā-goddesses.

PROF. BOLT: Several names repeat: stirring, melting, attracting. The powers at the gate return here as fortune-givers. The tradition likes echoes.

The first seven goddesses describe powers of the Goddess over all things: to stir them, melt them, attract them, delight them, enchant them, still them and expand them. Several echo the mudrā-goddesses of the first enclosure, as the tradition often repeats powers at different depths.

Sarvāhlādinī, “she who delights all”, is a favourite. Āhlāda is the deep, welling joy of devotion, and the Goddess who gives it gives one of the highest forms of good fortune.

Sarvastambhinī and Sarvajṛmbhiṇī, she who stills and she who expands, form a pair: the Goddess holds the world steady and lets it grow.

ZAPPY: The last one destroys opposites?

PROF. BOLT: Pleasure and pain, heat and cold, praise and blame. The fourteenth goddess ends the see-saw. That's real good fortune.

The second seven turn toward gifts: control, delight, intoxication, the accomplishment of every aim, the fullness of every wealth, the whole body of mantra, and finally the destruction of all pairs of opposites.

Sarvamantramayī, “she who is made of all mantras”, connects with the Sahasranāma’s teaching that the Goddess is sound itself. Every sacred word is a form of her.

Sarvadvandvakṣayaṅkarī ends the list. The pairs of opposites, pleasure and pain, gain and loss, honour and dishonour, are what the Bhagavad Gītā asks the wise to rise above. The fourth enclosure’s final gift is that freedom.

All fourteen are named in the Devī Khaḍgamālā.

ZAPPY: So this ring is all the good stuff?

PROF. BOLT: All of it, from delight to wealth. And it ends by taking you past ‘good’ and ‘bad’ altogether.

Read together, the fourteen describe saubhāgya in full. It begins with the Goddess’s power over the world, continues with delight and every kind of wealth and success, includes the sacred word, and ends beyond the pairs of opposites.

The arrangement is instructive. Worldly blessings and spiritual freedom are not opposed; they lie on the same ring. The tradition’s famous claim that enjoyment and freedom both lie in the devotee’s hand is drawn here as geometry.

Bodily readings, especially the Bhāvanopaniṣad’s, link the fourteen triangles with fourteen channels of the subtle body. Cosmic readings link them with the fourteen worlds. Both see the ring as a complete set: everything held in the Goddess’s blessing.

For a devotee, the lesson is that good fortune is the Goddess’s gift and is welcome, and that its highest form is a heart no longer tossed between opposites.

ZAPPY: Sustenance means keeping the world going?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The outer three made the world; these three hold it. And for the seeker, these layers steady and protect the journey.

The fourth enclosure opens the cakras of sustenance. In creation’s order, these middle layers hold the world in being once the inner powers have produced it. In worship’s order, they steady and protect the seeker who has passed the outer layers.

After the gathering of the second enclosure and the stirring of the third, the fourth brings blessedness: a life made whole and settled, the ground for deeper work.

Its yoginīs are of the tradition: from here inward, the journey is guided by lineage and teacher.

The next two enclosures continue the theme of sustenance: the accomplishment of aims and protection.

Many devotees who never perform Śrīcakra worship still use the nine enclosures as a meditation: pausing at each ring in imagination, greeting its goddesses, and moving inward. The guides in this series are laid out to support that kind of reading, one enclosure at a time.

ZAPPY: Dative: ‘to Kamalāmbā’?

PROF. BOLT: ‘Salutations to Kamalāmbikā.’ The fourth song offers itself to her.

Dīkṣitar’s fourth song is “Kamalāmbikāyai”, commonly sung in the rāga Kāmbhoji. It uses the dative case, offering salutations to Kamalāmbikā, and names the fourteen-cornered enclosure, Tripuravāsinī and the sampradāya yoginīs.

Kāmbhoji is a stately, expansive rāga, well suited to a layer of royal blessedness. Many listeners count this among the grandest songs of the cycle.

The dative, “to Kamalāmbikā”, has a devotional feel: the song is an offering, given to the Goddess.

Dīkṣitar was born at Tiruvārūr, the town of Kamalāmbā, and the cycle is closely tied to her temple there. The songs are performed widely, often during Navarātri, when some musicians sing one each night, and the whole cycle is a staple of concerts devoted to the Goddess.

ZAPPY: Is this the wedding layer, then?

PROF. BOLT: Not literally, but saubhāgya is marital happiness too. Lalitā's union with Kāmeśvara is the blessedness this ring gives.

Saubhāgya in its marital sense points straight to the Lalitopākhyāna’s wedding. The gods give Lalitā to Kāmeśvara, crown the couple and name her Kāmeśvarī. Her blessedness as a married queen is the model of the saubhāgya this enclosure gives.

In Śrīpura, the walls inside the metal walls are of gems, where the court lives in splendour. The fourth enclosure, the first ring of triangles, corresponds in spirit to those jewelled inner courts.

Many of the powers named here appear among the goddesses on the tiers of Lalitā’s chariot in the war, a reminder that the yantra and the story share one cast.

Readers who want the whole story can turn to the six-part Lalitopākhyāna retelling in this series. The Śrīcakra guides and the story guides are written to be read side by side: the city, the chariots and the war on one hand, the yantra and its enclosures on the other.

ZAPPY: So good fortune is part of spiritual life?

PROF. BOLT: In Śrīvidyā, very much. A blessed, steady life is not a distraction from the path; it's part of it.

In the inner reading, after the heart has been gathered and stirred, life itself is steadied and blessed. The practitioner’s ordinary circumstances become a support for the journey rather than an obstacle.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad, as commonly summarised, links the fourteen triangles with fourteen channels of the subtle body. Its own guide will set out that reading from a supplied text.

The last goddess’s gift, freedom from the pairs of opposites, is a classic mark of spiritual maturity in Indian thought. Here it comes as a gift, not merely as an achievement.

For a devotee, the enclosure suggests gratitude: noticing the good fortune already given, and holding it lightly, without being thrown by its ups and downs.
