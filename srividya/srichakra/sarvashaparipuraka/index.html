ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

SARVĀŚĀPARIPŪRAKA

“Fulfiller of all hopes”

ŚRĪCAKRA · ĀVARAṆA 2 OF 9

FOREWORD

Inside the square and its three circles opens a lotus of sixteen petals. This is the second enclosure, Sarvāśāparipūraka, “the fulfiller of all hopes”.

On each petal stands a goddess whose name ends in ākarṣiṇī, “she who attracts”: of desire, intellect, the senses, memory, the self and the body. This guide names all sixteen and shows how they gather the whole person toward the Goddess.

Śrī Tripureśyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A lotus inside the square?

PROF. BOLT: Sixteen petals, sixteen goddesses, each pulling something toward the Goddess.

The second āvaraṇa is the sixteen-petalled lotus that opens just inside the Śrīcakra’s three circles. It is called Sarvāśāparipūraka, “fulfiller of all hopes”, and its presiding goddess is Tripureśī, with the gupta or hidden yoginīs.

Each of its sixteen petals holds a goddess of attraction, an ākarṣiṇī. Together they draw the faculties of the person, the inner sense of self, the five senses, mind, memory, even the body, toward the Goddess.

Its mudrā-goddess is Sarvavidrāviṇī, “she who melts all”, and its siddhi is Laghimā, lightness. It belongs with the first and third enclosures to the cakras of creation.

Dīkṣitar’s song for this enclosure is “Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re”, one of the best-known of the cycle.

THE NAME

“fulfiller of all hopes”
SarvaAll
ĀśāHope, longing; also a direction, a quarter of space
ParipūrakaFulfilling completely
TogetherThe fulfiller of all hopes
Double senseIt fills every longing, and every direction of space
MoodGenerous: the Goddess answering desire

ZAPPY: Āśā means hope AND direction?

PROF. BOLT: Both. So the name means she fills every hope, and she fills every direction. Nowhere and no wish left empty.

Āśā is a lovely double word. It means hope or longing, and it also means a direction, a quarter of the sky. The name can be read as “she who fills every hope” or “she who fills every direction”, and commentators enjoy both.

Paripūraka is intensive: not just filling, but filling completely, to the brim. The enclosure is the Goddess’s answer to longing, and it leaves nothing half-filled.

After the first enclosure’s enchantment, this one is generous. The world that dazzled at the gate becomes, inside the wall, a place where every hope is answered.

Many devotees who pray to the Goddess for help with a specific longing are, in this picture, approaching her through the second enclosure.

ZAPPY: Why sixteen petals?

PROF. BOLT: Sixteen is the moon's number in this tradition: fifteen phases plus the one that never changes. And the sixteen vowels, the breath of every word.

The sixteen-petalled lotus sits within three concentric circles, which separate it from the square of the first enclosure. In most drawings the petals are coloured pink or red and point outward.

Sixteen is a significant number in Śrīvidyā. The moon has sixteen digits, kalās, fifteen that wax and wane and a sixteenth that never changes. The Sanskrit alphabet has sixteen vowels. Ṣoḍaśī, “the sixteen”, is a name of the Goddess.

The lotus form marks a change from the first enclosure. The square was solid, earthly, walled. The lotus is soft and opening: the Goddess’s palace begins to blossom as the seeker moves inward.

The three circles between the square and the lotus are not usually counted as a separate enclosure, though some lineages honour deities on them. They mark a threshold: once over them, the seeker has left the earth-city and entered the Goddess’s garden.

ZAPPY: Why ‘hidden’ yoginīs here?

PROF. BOLT: We've stepped inside the outer wall. What was manifest at the gate becomes a little hidden here: the powers inside experience, not out in the world.

The presiding goddess of the second enclosure is Tripureśī, “ruler of the three cities”. Where Tripurā at the gate was the plain name, Tripureśī adds lordship: the Goddess as ruler.

Its yoginīs are gupta, “hidden”. After the manifest yoginīs of the gate, the powers here are inward ones: the faculties of experience rather than the forces of the world.

The mudrā-goddess Sarvavidrāviṇī, “she who melts all”, suits a layer where the faculties dissolve their scattered attachments and flow toward one centre.

Some writers link each cakreśvarī to a stage of the Goddess’s self-revelation. Tripureśī, the ruler, is the Goddess as the one who governs every faculty of experience, which is exactly what this enclosure gathers.

ZAPPY: Each one ‘attracts’ something?

PROF. BOLT: Ākarṣiṇī means ‘she who draws’. Each goddess draws one part of us toward the Goddess: desire, intellect, ego, each of the senses.

The first eight goddesses draw the deepest inner faculties and the senses: desire, intellect, the sense of “I”, and the five sense objects, sound, touch, form, taste and smell.

Ākarṣiṇī comes from the root kṛṣ, to draw or pull, the same root as in Kṛṣṇa’s name in some etymologies. Each goddess pulls one faculty away from its scattered objects and toward the Goddess.

The order begins with desire, kāma, because desire moves everything else. Once desire is drawn toward the Goddess, the intellect and the ego follow, and then the senses.

ZAPPY: Even the body gets drawn in?

PROF. BOLT: Everything. Mind, courage, memory, name, the self, the body. By the sixteenth petal there's nothing of you left outside.

The second eight draw the subtler and more personal faculties: mind, courage, memory, name, the seed, the self, nectar and the body.

Nāma, name, is striking: even one’s name and identity are drawn in. Bīja, seed, suggests the deepest potential. Amṛta, nectar, the immortal essence. And the last is śarīra, the body itself: nothing is left behind.

All sixteen are named in the Devī Khaḍgamālā hymn, where devotees recite them in a single breath as the second enclosure.

ZAPPY: So this layer collects all of me?

PROF. BOLT: That's the idea. The first enclosure showed you the world. This one gathers you, every faculty, and points it inward.

Read together, the sixteen ākarṣiṇīs describe a whole human being: inner faculties, five senses, mind and memory, identity and the body. The enclosure gathers all of it.

This fits Śrīvidyā’s way. It does not ask the seeker to shut down the senses or deny desire. It turns them, one by one, toward the Goddess. The senses are not enemies but powers to be drawn home.

The link with the moon’s sixteen digits adds a sense of growth. As the moon fills night by night, the person fills with the Goddess, faculty by faculty, until full.

Some writers also link the sixteen to the sixteen vowels of the alphabet, the breath that carries every word. The seeker’s whole voice is gathered here.

ZAPPY: Is this still the outer part?

PROF. BOLT: Still among the cakras of creation, but inside the walls. You've passed the gate and entered the first garden.

The second enclosure is the middle of the three cakras of creation. In creation’s order, the One has unfolded through the inner layers and is about to take solid form in the square. In worship’s order, the seeker has just passed the gate and steps into the first ring inside.

Coming after the first enclosure’s enchantment, the second’s work is gathering. Before going deeper, the scattered self must be collected.

In temple terms, this is the first inner court: still busy, but already within the walls.

The next enclosure, the eight-petalled lotus, takes the gathered faculties and stirs them toward the Goddess with the power of love.

RāgaKalyāṇī, as commonly sung
CaseAddresses Kamalāmbā in the accusative case
ComposerMuttusvāmi Dīkṣitar (1775–1835)
ContentNames this enclosure, its Tripurā, its yoginīs and its goddesses
Our approachDescribed, not reproduced

ZAPPY: What does ‘accusative’ mean here?

PROF. BOLT: The object form: ‘Worship Kamalāmbā!’ The second song tells you to do something to her: bhaja, worship.

Dīkṣitar’s second song is “Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re”, commonly sung in the rāga Kalyāṇī. It addresses the Goddess in the accusative case: “worship Kamalāmbā”. It names the enclosure, its presiding Tripureśī and its hidden yoginīs, and refers to the sixteen petals.

It is one of the most frequently performed songs of the whole cycle, and many concert-goers know it without knowing its place in the Śrīcakra.

Kalyāṇī, an auspicious and expansive rāga, suits a layer named for the fulfilment of all hopes.

The full song will be treated verse by verse in the Navāvaraṇa guide once its text is supplied.

IN THE STORY

Śrīpura, the chariots and the war
ŚrīpuraGardens between walls; this lotus is the first garden inside the gate
NityāsThe moon goddesses of the war share the sixteen-fold pattern
AttractionLalitā rules by drawing, not forcing: her noose is attraction
KāmaThe goddesses of attraction recall the love-god’s power, held by the Goddess

ZAPPY: Is this the noose in action?

PROF. BOLT: A good way to see it. Her noose catches desire; these sixteen goddesses draw every faculty in.

Śrīpura has gardens between its walls: groves of wish-fulfilling and heavenly trees. The second enclosure, just inside the outer square, is the Śrīcakra’s first garden: a lotus of sixteen petals.

The fifteen Nityās who fight the night battle in the Lalitopākhyāna, with Lalitā as the sixteenth, share this enclosure’s sixteen-fold pattern, the moon’s digits. The goddesses of the war and the goddesses of the yantra mirror each other.

The theme of attraction recalls Lalitā’s noose, which the Sahasranāma calls attachment held in her hand. Here that power gathers the seeker in.

Readers who know the Kāmeśvara guide will recall that kāma, desire, in Śrīvidyā’s philosophy is first of all the divine will to create. The first goddess of this enclosure, Kāmākarṣiṇī, draws that power home: the will that sent the world outward is turned back toward its source.

THE INNER READING

The enclosure within
The personThe sixteen petals as the sixteen parts of a human being, in some readings
PracticeGathering the scattered faculties
SensesNot shut off but turned toward the Divine
MoonThe mind waxes toward fullness, digit by digit
BhāvanopaniṣadGives its own reading of this lotus (guide to come)

ZAPPY: So meditation here means gathering myself?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. You can't go inward while half of you is wandering. The sixteen attractions call everything home.

In the inner reading, the sixteen petals are the sixteen parts of the person, gathered for the journey. The practice suggested is collection: bringing the scattered faculties back from their many objects.

This is not repression. The senses are not shut; they are turned. Sound, touch, form, taste and smell become ways of attending to the Goddess.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad gives a detailed bodily reading of each enclosure, which its own guide will set out when the text is in hand.

For a devotee with no technical practice, the enclosure suggests a simple discipline: before prayer, gather yourself. Notice where your attention has wandered and call it home.
