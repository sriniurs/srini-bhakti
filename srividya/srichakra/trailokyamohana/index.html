ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

TRAILOKYAMOHANA

“Enchanter of the three worlds”

ŚRĪCAKRA · ĀVARAṆA 1 OF 9

FOREWORD

Every Śrīcakra is framed by a square of three lines with four gates. This is the first enclosure, Trailokyamohana, the “enchanter of the three worlds”, and it is where the journey to the Goddess begins.

In its three lines stand twenty-eight goddesses: the ten powers of yoga, the eight Mothers and the ten mudrā-goddesses. This guide names them, explains them and shows why the tradition places them at the gate.

ZAPPY: Is this the outer wall of the Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: The square with four gates. Where every journey to the Goddess begins.

The first āvaraṇa is the outer frame of the Śrīcakra: a square drawn in three parallel lines, broken at the centre of each side by a T-shaped gate. It is called the bhūpura, the “earth-city”, and the enclosure as a whole is called Trailokyamohana, the enchanter of the three worlds.

Twenty-eight goddesses stand here, ten on the outer line, eight on the middle and ten on the inner. Its presiding goddess is Tripurā, and its yoginīs are the prakaṭa, the manifest ones. It is the most open and visible layer of the whole yantra.

This guide follows the pattern of all nine enclosure guides: name, shape, presiding goddess, the goddesses by name, their meaning, place on the road, Dīkṣitar’s song, links to the story, and the inner reading.

THE NAME

“enchanter of the three worlds”
TrailokyaThe three worlds: earth, the middle air, heaven
MohanaEnchanting, bewildering
TogetherThe enchanter of the three worlds
Whose enchantmentThe Goddess’s māyā, which makes the one appear as the world
At the gateThe first thing met is the spell that the journey must see through

ZAPPY: Why is the first layer about enchantment?

PROF. BOLT: Because the world we start in is the enchanted one. You enter her palace through the spell that makes everything look separate.

Trailokya, the three worlds, is the traditional Indian way of saying “everything”: earth, the space between, and heaven. Mohana means enchanting or bewildering. The first enclosure is named for the Goddess’s power to enchant the whole of existence.

That enchantment is māyā, the power by which the one reality appears as a world of many separate things. It is not evil; it is the Goddess’s play. But it is also what hides her. The journey to the centre begins by recognising it.

Some commentators read mohana in a gentler sense: the Goddess is so beautiful that she enchants the three worlds. Both readings fit a goddess called “the beautiful one of the three cities”.

There is a practical side to the name too. Many devotees who pray to the Goddess for success, love or influence are, in the Śrīvidyā picture, asking for gifts of this first enclosure. The tradition does not scorn such prayers; it simply reminds the seeker that the gate is not the palace.

ZAPPY: Why a square on the outside?

PROF. BOLT: The square is the earth: four directions, solid ground. The journey inward starts from where we stand.

The square is the oldest Indian symbol of the earth, with its four directions. The Śrīcakra’s square is drawn with three parallel lines, and each line is a ring where a group of goddesses stands. The four gates face the four directions, as in a temple or a royal city.

In Śrīpura, the city of the Lalitopākhyāna, the outer walls are of metal and have great gates with towers. The bhūpura is the same wall drawn small.

Everything outside the square is the world at large. Everything inside belongs to the Goddess’s palace. Crossing the line is the first act of worship.

ZAPPY: Why are her yoginīs called ‘manifest’?

PROF. BOLT: Because this is the outer layer, where the Goddess's powers show themselves openly, as the visible world.

Each enclosure of the Śrīcakra has a cakreśvarī, a presiding goddess. In the first it is Tripurā, the plainest form of the name: the Goddess of the three cities, before any further epithet.

Its yoginīs are called prakaṭa, “manifest” or “openly visible”. As the guides move inward, the yoginī names grow more hidden: gupta, guptatara and so on, until the centre, which is beyond all secrecy. The outermost layer is the Goddess as the world shows her.

Lineage lists also assign one of the ten mudrā-goddesses and one of the ten siddhis to each enclosure. Here they are Sarvasaṅkṣobhiṇī, “she who stirs all”, and Aṇimā, the power of becoming minute.

ZAPPY: The first goddesses are superpowers?

PROF. BOLT: The eight famous powers of yoga, and two more. The tradition puts them at the outermost gate: they're the first thing you meet, and the first thing you must walk past.

The outer line holds the siddhis, the attainments of yoga. Eight are classical: becoming minute, light, vast; lordship; mastery; fulfilment of will; and in some lists the power to obtain anything. Śrīvidyā adds others to make ten, including bhukti (enjoyment) and icchā (will).

The yoga traditions have long been ambivalent about these powers. Patañjali calls them obstacles to the highest goal even as he describes them. Placing them on the outermost line of the Śrīcakra expresses the same view: they are real, they are the Goddess’s, and they belong at the edge, not the centre.

The Lalitopākhyāna gives Aṇimā a small role in the war: in the night attack of chapter 25, the Śaktis led by Aṇimā encircle the army.

ZAPPY: The Mothers from the Devī Māhātmya?

PROF. BOLT: The same Mothers, with Mahālakṣmī as the eighth. Vārāhī is here too: the same goddess who commands Lalitā's army.

The middle line holds the eight Mothers, the Mātṛkās. Seven are the familiar Mothers of the Devī Māhātmya, the powers of Brahmā, Maheśvara, Kumāra, Viṣṇu, Varāha, Indra and the fierce Cāmuṇḍā; the eighth here is Mahālakṣmī.

Vārāhī, the boar-faced Mother, is the same goddess who commands Lalitā’s army. The Deities guide to Vārāhī follows both her lives.

Some lineages link the eight Mothers with eight passions or failings the seeker must master, such as desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride and envy. That reading is commentators’, and lists vary.

ZAPPY: Mudrā-goddesses? Aren't mudrās hand gestures?

PROF. BOLT: In worship they are. Here each mudrā is also a goddess, one for each enclosure. We name them; the gestures are for teachers.

The inner line holds the ten mudrā-goddesses. In ritual, a mudrā is a seal or gesture; in the Śrīcakra each is also a goddess, and their names describe powers of the Goddess over the world: to stir, to melt, to attract, to subdue, to intoxicate, to goad, to move in space, to be the seed, to be the source, and the threefold.

The first nine are each associated with one enclosure, and the tenth, Sarvatrikhaṇḍā, with the whole. In worship they are honoured with gestures that belong to initiation. We give their names and meanings only.

All twenty-eight goddesses of the first enclosure are named in order in the Devī Khaḍgamālā hymn, widely recited by devotees.

ZAPPY: So the superpowers are a test?

PROF. BOLT: Many teachers say so. Powers are real, the texts say, but they're the gatehouse, not the palace. Keep walking.

Read together, the three lines make a picture of the world as it first meets the seeker: the powers people chase, the great forces of the gods, and the Goddess’s own seals of power. All are honoured; none is the goal.

The number twenty-eight has no special symbolism in most commentaries; it is simply the sum of the three groups. What matters is the order: outermost the powers, then the Mothers, then the seals that open the way inward.

Devotees who recite the Khaḍgamālā often say that greeting these goddesses at the gate is like greeting the guards and courtiers of a palace: respectful, but not stopping there.

Some teachers give a simple rule for this enclosure: bow to every power you meet, take nothing from it, and walk on. The rule applies as much to everyday life, its ambitions and passions, as to meditation.

ZAPPY: Is this the easiest layer?

PROF. BOLT: The outermost, so the most familiar: earth, the Mothers, powers people chase. Easier to see, harder to get past.

The first enclosure is the beginning of the worshipper’s road and the end of creation’s. In the order of creation, everything unfolds from the bindu outward, and the square is the last and most solid layer: the earth. In the order of worship, the square is where the seeker stands and enters.

It belongs to the cakras of creation, the outer three enclosures, which show the One unfolding into the world.

Its gates face the four directions, so the seeker may enter from anywhere. Tradition says the Goddess may be approached from any starting point.

Temple architecture echoes the same pattern. A South Indian temple’s outermost prākāra, with its great gopuras, is busy, open and full of the world: shops, crowds, festivals. The quieter inner courts and the dark sanctum lie further in. The Śrīcakra’s first enclosure is that outer court.

ZAPPY: Which case is ‘nominative’?

PROF. BOLT: The plain form: ‘Kamalāmbā protect us’. The first song names her directly. Each song after uses the next case.

Dīkṣitar’s song for the first enclosure is “Kamalāmbā saṃrakṣatu”, commonly sung in the rāga Ānandabhairavī. It addresses the Goddess in the nominative case: Kamalāmbā, may she protect us. It names the enclosure, Trailokyamohana, its presiding Tripurā and its manifest yoginīs.

The choice of Ānandabhairavī, a rāga full of tenderness, suits the beginning of the journey: a warm welcome at the gate.

The song is described here rather than reproduced. A verse-by-verse guide to all eleven Navāvaraṇa songs will be built from a supplied text.

Concert-goers who know the Navāvaraṇa cycle often hear it as a journey. The first song, in a warm and welcoming rāga, sets out from the gate; the later songs, in graver rāgas, move toward the centre. Knowing the Śrīcakra makes the cycle’s design audible.

SiddhisAṇimā leads the Śaktis around the army in the night battle (ch. 25)

ZAPPY: Aṇimā was in the war?

PROF. BOLT: The text says the Śaktis led by Aṇimā encircled the army. The powers at the gate are also guards in the field.

In the Lalitopākhyāna, Śrīpura’s outermost walls are of black iron, bronze and copper, with gates and towers and guardians. The bhūpura is the same structure in miniature.

The Mothers fight in Lalitā’s army, and Vārāhī leads it. The siddhis appear too: in chapter 25, the Śaktis led by Aṇimā surround the army against the night attack.

Commentators who read Cakrarāja, Lalitā’s nine-tiered chariot, as a moving Śrīcakra place such goddesses on its lowest tiers.

Readers of the six-part Lalitopākhyāna retelling will find the connection natural: the Goddess’s city, chariot and diagram share one plan. Walking through the first enclosure is walking through Śrīpura’s outer gate.

THE INNER READING

The enclosure within
EarthThe body and the waking world
SiddhisAmbitions for power, honoured and set aside
MothersStrong passions, given to the Goddess
EnchantmentThe spell of separateness, recognised at the gate
BhāvanopaniṣadReads the outer square as the body’s outer layers (guide to come)

ZAPPY: So entering the Śrīcakra means seeing through the enchantment?

PROF. BOLT: That's the first step. You notice the world is her spell, and that is what lets you start walking in.

In the inner reading, the square is the body and the waking world, the solid ground where every seeker stands. The three lines are the outermost layers of the person.

The siddhis are ambitions for power; the Mothers are the strong passions; the mudrā-goddesses are the first disciplines that open the way inward. Passing through them is the first stage of practice: honouring what is strong in us, and not mistaking it for the goal.

The Bhāvanopaniṣad gives a detailed reading of the Śrīcakra as the body. Its guide will follow when its text is supplied.

Some lineages link each enclosure with a state of consciousness or a stage of practice. For the first, the common reading is the waking state and the beginning of discipline: the seeker who has just turned toward the Goddess, still surrounded by the world.
