AGASTYA AND LOPĀMUDRĀ

The first listeners of Lalitā’s story · explained and illustrated

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

HOW THIS WORKS: Agastya and Lopāmudrā, the first listeners of Lalitā’s story: Agastya’s birth and deeds, Lopāmudrā as princess and Vedic seer, their dialogue in Ṛgveda 1.179, their place in the Lalitopākhyāna and the Śrīvidyā lineage, Agastya in the Rāmāyaṇa and the south, and the star Canopus.

FOREWORD

Every telling of Lalitā’s story begins with the same two words: “Agastya said.” The sage Agastya, born from a pot, humbler of the Vindhya and drinker of the ocean, is the one to whom Hayagrīva told the Lalitopākhyāna and the thousand names. Beside him sits his wife Lopāmudrā, a seer of the Ṛgveda in her own right.

This first guide in the teachers series meets them both: in the Vedas, the epics and the Purāṇa, in the Śrīvidyā lineage where a whole school bears Lopāmudrā’s name, in the south that claims Agastya as its own, and in the southern sky.

ZAPPY: A husband and wife both teachers of Śrīvidyā?

PROF. BOLT: Both, Zappy. And in the Ṛgveda it’s Lopāmudrā who speaks first.

Agastya and Lopāmudrā are the first two names any reader of the Lalitā Sahasranāma meets. Every edition opens with the frame: Agastya asks, Hayagrīva answers. Lopāmudrā sits beside him, a Vedic seer in her own right.

This guide follows them through the Ṛgveda, the Mahābhārata, the Rāmāyaṇa and the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, then into the Śrīvidyā lineage, the Tamil south and the southern sky. It is the first of the Teachers and texts guides in this series.

As everywhere in the series, we tell stories, history and meaning. The vidyās linked with their names, the Kādi and Hādi forms, are given by a teacher and are not explained here.

WHO WAS AGASTYA

The sage born from a pot

ZAPPY: Born from a pot? How does that work?

PROF. BOLT: It’s a sacred birth, not a biological one. His water-pot (kamaṇḍalu) stays his emblem forever.

Birth. The Ṛgveda (7.33) links his birth, with Vasiṣṭha’s, to the gods Mitra and Varuṇa and a jar.

Names. Kumbhayoni, Kumbhasambhava, Ghaṭodbhava: “born from a pot”

Size. Remembered as short of stature but immense in power.

Vedic seer. Hymns 1.165 to 1.191 of the Ṛgveda are ascribed to him.

Character. Fiery, austere, but kind to those who come to him.

Everywhere. He appears in the Vedas, both epics, many Purāṇas and Tamil tradition.

The Ṛgveda’s account is brief and mysterious: Agastya and Vasiṣṭha are both connected with Mitra and Varuṇa, and with a jar into which their seed fell at the sight of the nymph Urvaśī. Later texts make the jar his signature, and his names, Kumbhayoni and Ghaṭodbhava, all mean “pot-born”.

Twenty-seven hymns of the first book of the Ṛgveda, 1.165 to 1.191, are ascribed to him. Some of them are dialogues, including a famous exchange between Indra and the Maruts. He is one of the few figures who stretches from the oldest Vedic layer to living temple worship today.

His character in the stories is consistent: fierce in austerity, quick to act, but always on the side of the suffering. He is the sage gods and kings turn to when nothing else works, from lowering a mountain to clearing an ocean.

HIS FAMOUS DEEDS

From the Mahābhārata

ZAPPY: He ate a demon?

PROF. BOLT: Vātāpi turned himself into food, planning to burst out of the guest. Agastya said ‘be digested’, and he was.

The Vindhya. The mountain grew to block the sun; Agastya asked it to bow until he returned, and never came back.

The ocean. He drank the sea so the gods could find the demons hiding in it.

Vātāpi. The demon brothers Ilvala and Vātāpi killed guests by trickery; Agastya simply digested Vātāpi.

Going south. After the Vindhya he settled in the south, and stayed.

A later legend. When the gods crowded north for Śiva’s wedding, he was sent south to balance the earth.

Source. The first three are told in the Mahābhārata’s forest book (Vana Parva)

The Vindhya story is the one children remember. The mountain, jealous of Meru, grew so tall it blocked the sun’s path. Agastya, its teacher, asked it to lower itself so he could cross to the south, and to stay low until he came back. He never returned, and the Vindhya is still waiting.

The ocean story is from the same book. Demons called Kāleyas attacked the sages by night and hid in the sea by day. The gods asked Agastya for help, and he drank the ocean dry, exposing them. The Vātāpi story shows his humour: a demon disguised as a meal is no match for a sage’s digestion.

A later, mainly southern story adds that when all the gods gathered in the north for the wedding of Śiva and Pārvatī, the earth tilted under their weight, and Śiva sent Agastya south to balance it. Many southern temples say he saw the wedding there in a vision instead.

WHO WAS LOPĀMUDRĀ

A princess made for a sage

ZAPPY: She asked for comforts first? Bold!

PROF. BOLT: And he agreed. Their story is about a real partnership, not a silent wife.

Origin. In the Mahābhārata, Agastya forms her from the finest qualities of all creatures.

Upbringing. He places her as a daughter in the house of the king of Vidarbha.

Marriage. She chooses the forest life with him, giving up her royal clothes.

Her condition. Before starting a family she asks for a fitting home; he goes to seek wealth honestly.

Son. Their son is Dṛḍhasyu, also called Idhmavāha.

Her name. Lopa + mudrā: often read as “she in whom the marks of beauty were lost”, i.e. beyond compare.

The Mahābhārata tells that Agastya, seeing his ancestors suffering because he had no son, resolved to marry. Finding no woman suited to him, he formed one from the finest parts of every creature and placed her with the childless king of Vidarbha. She grew up a princess of great beauty and learning, and when Agastya asked for her, she went to him willingly.

Her condition before starting a family is one of the most human moments in the epic. She asked for a home fit for both of them, with the comforts she had known. Agastya did not refuse; he went to kings for wealth, and when they could spare none without harming their subjects, he obtained it from the demon Ilvala. That is how the Vātāpi episode begins.

Her name is read in different ways. One common explanation is that the marks (mudrā) of beauty in other creatures were lost (lopa) when they were gathered into her, so that she was beyond comparison. Other writers give other readings.

IN THE ṚGVEDA

Hymn 1.179

ZAPPY: A woman seer in the Ṛgveda?

PROF. BOLT: Several. Lopāmudrā is one of the best known, and Śrīvidyā never forgot her.

The hymn. A short dialogue between Lopāmudrā and Agastya.

Who speaks first. Lopāmudrā, in the opening verses.

Theme. The tension between long austerity and married life.

Her standing. She is counted among the women seers (ṛṣikās) of the Ṛgveda.

Agastya’s answer. He agrees that both paths can be held together.

Our note. The hymn is very old and its meaning is debated; this is a common reading.

Ṛgveda 1.179 is only six verses long. In the opening verses Lopāmudrā speaks of the years of toil and austerity that have worn them both, and asks for love. Agastya answers that the gods favour those who keep both their vows and their householder’s life. The hymn is admired for its candour and its sense of two equal voices.

Lopāmudrā is listed among the women seers of the Ṛgveda, alongside figures such as Ghoṣā, Apālā and Viśvavārā. That a woman seer should later give her name to a school of Śrīvidyā is one of the tradition’s quiet glories.

The hymn is short and old, and scholars read it differently. Some see a debate between austerity and family life; others a ritual dialogue. Our reading follows the common one, and we flag it as such in the sources.

IN THE LALITOPĀKHYĀNA

The listener

ZAPPY: So everything we read about Lalitā was told to Agastya?

PROF. BOLT: In the story’s frame, yes. He’s the first listener. We’re all sitting behind him.

Setting. Agastya, travelling, grieves at the suffering of people.

At Kāñcī. He worships Viṣṇu, who sends Hayagrīva to teach him.

The story. Hayagrīva tells him the whole Lalitopākhyāna.

The hymns. At its end he receives the Lalitā Sahasranāma, and later the Triśatī.

Lopāmudrā. She is with him; the tradition says she too became a great devotee.

Every printed edition. Begins “Agastya said… Hayagrīva said…”

Read about Hayagrīva and his role as Agastya’s teacher. In brief: Agastya, wandering the earth, is moved by the suffering of people trapped in desire and ignorance, and asks how they can be freed. At Kāñcī, Viṣṇu sends Hayagrīva, who tells him the story of Lalitā as the remedy, ending with her thousand names.

Lopāmudrā’s presence matters to the tradition. Commentators note that Śrīvidyā’s great story was told to a couple, and that a woman heard it from the beginning. It is part of why Śrīvidyā has always been open to women as worshippers and teachers.

The Lalitā Triśatī, a hymn of three hundred names, is also told to Agastya by Hayagrīva in the same frame. Our guide to the Triśatī on this site sets out that story.

AGASTYA AT KĀÑCĪ

Where the teaching was given

ZAPPY: Why Kāñcī?

PROF. BOLT: It’s one of the seven cities of liberation, and the earthly home of Lalitā. A fitting place for her story to be told.

See: Kāñcī Kāmākṣī · Hayagrīva

Place. The Lalitopākhyāna sets Agastya’s meeting with Hayagrīva at Kāñcī.

Worship. He worships at Kāñcī before Hayagrīva appears.

Kāmākṣī. Kāñcī’s Goddess is Lalitā enthroned; see our temple guide.

Durvāsa. Another early teacher of Śrīvidyā, also honoured at Kāñcī.

Meaning. The great teaching given in a southern city, not a distant heaven.

Read more. Kāñcī Kāmākṣī and Hayagrīva.

In the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa’s frame, Agastya, grieving at the world’s suffering, comes to Kāñcī and worships there. Viṣṇu, pleased, sends Hayagrīva, his horse-headed form and the god of knowledge, to teach him. Kāñcī is thus the place where Lalitā’s story was first told on earth.

For Śrīvidyā readers the choice of city is no accident. Kāñcī is the home of Kāmākṣī, Lalitā enthroned, and of the Śrīcakra tradition our temple guide describes. The story and the temple point to each other.

Durvāsa, another of the early teachers, is also remembered at Kāñcī, where his shrine faces Kāmākṣī’s. The city gathers Śrīvidyā’s founders in one place.

IN THE ŚRĪVIDYĀ LINEAGE

Twelve great worshippers

ZAPPY: What’s the difference between Kādi and Hādi?

PROF. BOLT: They’re two forms of the root mantra, taught in different lineages. We name them and stop there.

Twelve. The Tantras name twelve great worshippers of Lalitā, each with a form of her vidyā.

Among them. Manu, Candra, Kubera, Lopāmudrā, Manmatha, Agastya, Agni, Sūrya, Indra, Skanda, Śiva and Durvāsas.

Two schools. The two main lines are Kādi, linked with Manmatha (Kāma), and Hādi, linked with Lopāmudrā.

Her honour. A whole school of Śrīvidyā bears a woman seer’s name.

What we give. Names and history only; the vidyās themselves are taught by a guru.

The list of twelve great worshippers comes from Tantric sources and is repeated by later teachers. Each is said to have worshipped Lalitā with a particular form of her root vidyā. Most of these forms are no longer in use; two survive as the main schools of Śrīvidyā practice, Kādi and Hādi.

The Kādi line is associated with Manmatha, Kāma, the god of love, and the Hādi line with Lopāmudrā. The two names come from the first syllable of each form of the mantra. Beyond that, this series says nothing about their content: the mantras are given by a guru, in person, and not in a book or on a website.

Agastya and Lopāmudrā are thus honoured twice in Śrīvidyā: as the first listeners of its central story, and as founders of practice.

IN THE RĀMĀYAṆA

Guide to Rāma

ZAPPY: The Āditya Hṛdaya was Agastya’s?

PROF. BOLT: He taught it to Rāma on the battlefield. People still recite it every morning.

Forest. Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa visit Agastya’s hermitage in the Daṇḍaka forest.

Gifts. He gives Rāma a great bow, inexhaustible quivers and a sword.

Advice. He directs them to settle at Pañcavaṭī.

At Laṅkā. In the last battle he teaches Rāma the Āditya Hṛdaya, a hymn to the sun.

The result. Rāma, refreshed, defeats Rāvaṇa.

Allies. Hanumān and the vānara army fight at Rāma’s side throughout.

In the Araṇya Kāṇḍa, Rāma comes to Agastya’s hermitage in the Daṇḍaka forest. The sage welcomes him, gives him a great bow, two quivers that never empty and a sword, and suggests the place called Pañcavaṭī for their stay. From there the story moves towards Sītā’s abduction.

In the Yuddha Kāṇḍa, as Rāma stands exhausted before the final duel with Rāvaṇa, Agastya appears and teaches him the Āditya Hṛdaya, a hymn to the sun. Rāma recites it, regains his strength and wins. The hymn is still recited daily by many, and its frame keeps Agastya present at the climax of the epic.

Agastya’s gifts to Rāma come back at the end of the epic: the bow and quivers are the weapons Rāma carries into the final battle, and the Āditya Hṛdaya gives him the strength to use them.

AGASTYA IN THE SOUTH

Tamil and Kannada memory

ZAPPY: The Kāverī came out of his pot?

PROF. BOLT: That’s how Kodagu tells it, at Talakāverī. Karnataka’s great river begins with Agastya’s kamaṇḍalu.

Tamil. Tradition makes him the father of Tamil grammar and a teacher of Tolkāppiyar.

His hill. The Podhigai hills (Agastyamalai) in the Western Ghats are called his home.

Kāverī. A Kodagu legend: the Kāverī was held in Agastya’s pot until Gaṇeśa, as a crow, tipped it over.

Siddha medicine. Tamil Siddha tradition honours him as its first teacher.

Our note. These are regional traditions; historians date the works ascribed to him much later.

Agastya is perhaps more beloved in the south than in the north. Tamil tradition makes him the first grammarian of Tamil, author of a lost grammar called Agattiyam, and teacher of Tolkāppiyar, whose Tolkāppiyam survives. Historians date these works much later than the Vedic sage, but the devotion is real and old.

In Kodagu, the Kāverī’s origin at Talakāverī is told through Agastya. In one popular version, the sage carried the river in his water-pot until Gaṇeśa, in the form of a crow, overturned it, and the Kāverī flowed out across the land. For readers in Karnataka, Agastya is never far away.

Tamil Siddha medicine also honours Agastya as its first teacher, and many medical and alchemical works carry his name. The Podhigai hills in the Western Ghats, called Agastyamalai, are said to be his home, and pilgrims still climb to his shrine there.

THE STAR AGASTYA

Canopus in the southern sky

ZAPPY: Can I see him from Mysuru?

PROF. BOLT: Canopus is visible from south India on winter evenings, low in the south. It’s worth looking for.

Canopus. The second-brightest star in the night sky, low in the south from India.

Name. Indian astronomy calls it Agastya.

Season. Its rising was said to mark the end of the rains.

Clear water. Old texts say its rising makes waters pure and clear.

Bṛhat Saṃhitā. Varāhamihira gives a chapter to Agastya’s rising.

Fitting. The sage who went south, and stayed, shines in the south.

Canopus is the brightest star of the southern constellation Carina and the second-brightest in the whole sky. From north India it barely rises; from the south it is easier to see, low on the horizon. Indian astronomers named it after the sage who went south.

Old texts link its rising, at the end of the rainy season, with clear skies and pure water. Varāhamihira’s Bṛhat Saṃhitā gives the subject a chapter of its own. It is a lovely piece of cultural astronomy: the sage who cleared the way south still shines over it.

A small practical note for readers in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu: Canopus is best seen on clear winter evenings, from January to March, low in the southern sky, below the bright star Sirius. Away from city lights it is unmistakable.

WHAT THEY MEAN

Partnership on the path

ZAPPY: What’s the lesson for me?

PROF. BOLT: That you don’t have to leave home to walk this path. Agastya and Lopāmudrā walked it together.

Together. Husband and wife share the teaching; neither is merely the other’s follower.

Householders. Śrīvidyā has always been open to householders.

Agastya. Compassion: he sought the teaching because others were suffering.

Lopāmudrā. A woman’s voice at the root of a lineage.

The pot. A small vessel holding a whole ocean or a river: a little form holding great power.

Commentators like to point out that Śrīvidyā’s first listeners were not a lone renouncer but a married couple. The tradition has always included householders, and it does not treat worldly life as an obstacle. Agastya and Lopāmudrā are the model: austere and devoted, but at home together.

Agastya’s motive matters too. He did not ask for the teaching for himself. He asked because he saw people suffering. Śrīvidyā’s whole story begins with that compassion.

The pot is the last image worth keeping. A small water-pot holds an ocean, a river, a sage’s whole power. Śrīvidyā says the same of the human heart: a small space that can hold the Goddess.

GLOSSARY

Words used in this guide

Kamaṇḍalu. A sage’s water-pot.

Ṛṣikā. A woman seer of Vedic hymns.

Vidarbha. An ancient kingdom in central India.

Kādi / Hādi. The two main lines of Śrīvidyā.

Āditya Hṛdaya. “Heart of the sun”, a hymn in the Rāmāyaṇa.

Canopus. A bright southern star, called Agastya.

Daṇḍaka. The forest of Rāma’s exile.

Vānara. The forest people of the Rāmāyaṇa, Hanumān’s kin.

Agastya’s name is often explained as “he who moves (gam) the mountain (aga)”, a reference to the Vindhya story. Like many such explanations, it is traditional rather than strictly historical.
