BHĀSKARARĀYA

The light on the thousand names

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

If one name stands for Śrīvidyā scholarship, it is Bhāskararāya. His Saubhāgya Bhāskara, finished in 1728, is the commentary through which almost everyone reads the Lalitā Sahasranāma, including this site.

This guide tells his life, from the Deccan to a village on the Kāverī, describes his great works, and shows how he reads a single name.

ZAPPY: Have I read him already?

PROF. BOLT: Every time you read a Lalitā Sahasranāma deep dive on this site, Zappy. ‘What the commentary adds’ is Bhāskararāya.

Bhāskararāya is the great commentator of Śrīvidyā. Readers of this site have already met him many times: the “What the commentary adds” sections of our ten-part Lalitā Sahasranāma guide draw on his Saubhāgya Bhāskara throughout.

He was a scholar of astonishing range, writing on Vedānta, logic, grammar and poetry as well as on the Goddess. He was also an initiated Śrīvidyā teacher, and his line continues through his pupils.

This guide tells his life, describes his main works, and shows how he reads a name. Two of his books are about the initiated mantra and worship; we name them and say what they cover, but do not describe their content.

HIS LIFE

From the Deccan to the Kāverī
FamilyA Maharashtrian Brahmin family; his father was Gambhīrarāya Bhāratī
BirthplaceGiven as Bhāgā in Maharashtra, or Hyderabad
DatesUsually given as 1690–1785; some put his active years earlier
KāśīStudied there as a young man
LogicLearned Navya-nyāya from Gaṅgādhara Vājapeyī at Tiruvālaṅgāḍu
SettledOn the Kāverī bank opposite his teacher; the village became Bhāskararājapuram

ZAPPY: He moved to be near his teacher?

PROF. BOLT: Across the river from him, and stayed for life. Respect for a teacher was that serious.

Bhāskararāya came from a Maharashtrian Brahmin family; his father was Gambhīrarāya Bhāratī. Sources give his birthplace as Bhāgā in Maharashtra or as Hyderabad, and his dates usually as 1690–1785, though some scholars put his active life earlier.

He studied as a young man at Kāśī, and later learned Navya-nyāya, the new logic, from Gaṅgādhara Vājapeyī, who lived at Tiruvālaṅgāḍu in the Kāverī delta.

To stay near his teacher, he settled in the village opposite on the other bank of the Kāverī. It came to be called Bhāskararājapuram, and it was his home for the rest of his life.

INITIATION

Bhāsurānandanātha
GuruŚivadatta Śukla of Surat, initiated name Prakāśānandanātha
His nameBhāsurānandanātha, “lord of radiant bliss”
TraditionA Śaṅkara-linked Śrīvidyā line, according to his biographers
BiographyWritten by his pupil Umānandanātha
Royal patronA Thanjavur Maratha king (the sources say Serfoji) gave him land
Our ruleWe give names; not the initiation itself

ZAPPY: Why a new name at initiation?

PROF. BOLT: It marks a second birth. The ‘-ānandanātha’ ending is the Śrīvidyā sign of an initiate.

He was initiated into Śrīvidyā by Śivadatta Śukla of Surat, whose initiated name was Prakāśānandanātha. Bhāskararāya’s own initiated name was Bhāsurānandanātha.

His pupil Umānandanātha wrote an account of his life, which is the main source for the stories told about him.

A Maratha king of Thanjavur, named Serfoji in the sources, invited him to the south and gave him land. Since Serfoji II ruled much later, the king meant is more likely Serfoji I.

THE SAUBHĀGYA BHĀSKARA

The light on the thousand names
WhatA full commentary on the Lalitā Sahasranāma
FinishedĀśvina śukla navamī, 1728, by his own record
MethodExplains each name, often in several ways: story, philosophy, mantra, grammar
CountFixes the exact division into 1,000 names
SourcesQuotes Vedas, Purāṇas, Tantras and Upaniṣads on almost every page
Used hereOur ten-part Sahasranāma guide draws on it throughout

ZAPPY: Why does the count need fixing?

PROF. BOLT: Because one word can split two ways. Bhāskararāya’s count is the one almost everyone follows.

The Saubhāgya Bhāskara is a commentary on every name of the Lalitā Sahasranāma. By his own record it was finished on Āśvina śukla navamī in 1728, the ninth day of Navarātri.

He explains each name from several angles: its place in the Lalitopākhyāna, its grammar, its philosophy, its links with the Śrīcakra and with other texts. He quotes the Vedas, Upaniṣads, Purāṇas and Tantras constantly.

He also fixes the count of the thousand names, deciding where one name ends and the next begins. His count is the one followed by almost all modern editions, including the one used on this site.

THE SETUBANDHA

A bridge across the Tantra
WhatA commentary on the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava, part of the Vāmakeśvara Tantra
SubjectOuter and inner worship of Tripurasundarī
FinishedOn Śivarātri, 1748, by tradition
StatusRegarded as his magnum opus
Name“Building the bridge”, like Rāma’s causeway to Laṅkā
Our ruleIts ritual detail is for initiates; we name its subject only

ZAPPY: A bridge to where?

PROF. BOLT: Across a hard text. Without it, the Tantra is an ocean few could cross.

The Setubandha is a commentary on the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava, the part of the Vāmakeśvara Tantra that deals with the outer and inner worship of Tripurasundarī. Many regard it as his greatest work.

Its name means “building the bridge”, recalling Rāma’s causeway to Laṅkā: it carries the reader across a difficult text. Tradition dates its completion to Śivarātri, 1748, though other dates are also given.

Because its subject is initiated worship, we give only its title and topic. Readers interested in its scholarship can consult Douglas Brooks’s studies.

THE VARIVASYĀ RAHASYA

The secret of worship
What167 verses with his own commentary, the Prakāśa
SubjectThe meaning of the Śrīvidyā mantra and its worship
LevelsMeanings from the outer and literal to the most inward
EditionEdited and translated by S. Subrahmanya Sastri (Adyar, 1934)
Our ruleThis series does not give or decode the mantra
Why mention itBecause it is one of his three central works

ZAPPY: If it’s about the mantra, why tell me about it?

PROF. BOLT: So you know it exists and what it does. The mantra itself is given by a guru; that’s the rule of this series.

The Varivasyā Rahasya, “the secret of worship”, is a work of 167 verses with Bhāskararāya’s own commentary, the Prakāśa. It explains the meanings of the Śrīvidyā mantra at many levels, from the outer to the most inward.

It was edited and translated into English by S. Subrahmanya Sastri for the Adyar Library in 1934, and remains an important scholarly source.

This series does not give, assemble or decode the mantra. We mention the book so that readers know what it is and why it matters in the tradition.

THE GUPTAVATĪ

On the Devī Māhātmya
WhatA commentary on the Devī Māhātmya
Coverage224 of its 579 verses
StyleConcise; explains hard verses and links them to Śrīvidyā
UseRead by reciters of the Devī Māhātmya at Navarātri
LinkOur Navarātri guide explains the text’s place in the festival
Name“The hidden one”

ZAPPY: He wrote on Durgā too?

PROF. BOLT: On the Devī Māhātmya, yes. For him, Cāmuṇḍā and Lalitā were one Goddess.

The Guptavatī is Bhāskararāya’s commentary on the Devī Māhātmya, the great Navarātri text. It is selective, commenting on 224 of the text’s 579 verses, the ones that most need explaining.

For Bhāskararāya there was no gap between the Goddess of the Devī Māhātmya and Lalitā. The Guptavatī often draws out Śrīvidyā meanings from the Devī Māhātmya’s verses.

Reciters of the Devī Māhātmya still consult it. Our Navarātri and Mysuru Dasarā guide explains the text’s place in the festival.

ZAPPY: Over forty books?

PROF. BOLT: Brooks calls him ‘an encyclopedic writer’. He seems to have known everything and written about most of it.

His Khadyota, “the firefly”, is a commentary on the Gaṇeśa Sahasranāma, regarded as authoritative by Gāṇapatyas, the worshippers of Gaṇeśa.

He also commented on the Tripurā Upaniṣad and the Bhāvanā Upaniṣad, two short Upaniṣads central to Śrīvidyā; the second will have its own guide in this series once a text is supplied.

Beyond the Goddess he wrote on logic, Mīmāṃsā, grammar and Śiva’s names, and composed devotional poems. Over forty works are ascribed to him.

STORIES ABOUT HIM

The tradition’s memory

MeaningThe Goddess, not the householder, received the honour
The challengeAsked to name the “sixty-four crore yoginīs” of a hymn, he began listing them from behind a curtain
RevelationDevotees say the Goddess revealed meanings to him
Our noteThese are pious stories, told by later devotees
UseThey show how highly he was regarded

ZAPPY: Did he really list millions of names?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the story. Whatever happened, it says his learning seemed boundless to those around him.

Devotees tell many stories about him. In one, a sannyāsī passing his house was offended that the householder did not rise to bow to him. The story says that when the sannyāsī’s staff was set before him, it burned up. Devotees read it as a sign that the Goddess herself was present in him.

In another, a scholar named Nārāyaṇa Bhaṭṭa challenged him to name the “sixty-four crores of yoginīs” mentioned in a hymn. Bhāskararāya is said to have begun listing them from behind a curtain, until the challenger gave up.

These are pious stories, told to show how highly he was regarded. We give them as tradition.

HOW HE READS A NAME

A Bhāskararāya lesson
ContextWhere the name sits in the story of the Lalitopākhyāna
GrammarHow the word breaks up and what each part means
PhilosophyWhat it says about Brahman and the Self
RitualHow it echoes the Śrīcakra and its worship
Several meaningsHe often gives two or three and lets each stand
Proof textsHe backs each with a quotation

ZAPPY: Why give several meanings?

PROF. BOLT: Because a great name holds several truths. He trusts the reader to see more than one.

A Bhāskararāya commentary on a name usually moves through several layers. First, where the name sits in the story; then how the word is built; then what it says about Brahman and the Self; then how it echoes the Śrīcakra and its worship.

He often gives two or three meanings for one name and does not insist on one. Each is supported by a quotation from scripture.

Readers of our Sahasranāma deep dives have seen this in action. The method trains the reader to look more slowly and more widely at every word.

ZAPPY: If I read one of his books, which?

PROF. BOLT: The Saubhāgya Bhāskara, in a good translation, alongside the Sahasranāma. Start with one name a day.

Bhāskararāya’s count and readings of the Lalitā Sahasranāma are the standard today. His pupils carried his teaching, and Umānandanātha’s Nityotsava, a manual of worship following the Paraśurāma Kalpasūtra, is still in use.

In the twentieth century, Douglas Renfrew Brooks’s books made him widely known among scholars outside India, describing him as a thinker with the Vedic and Tantric traditions at his fingertips.

He was a householder, not a monk, and his life shows that the tradition’s deepest scholarship could come from a married teacher in a Kāverī village.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: That every deep dive you’ve read on the Sahasranāma rests on a man who finished his commentary on Navarātri’s ninth day in 1728.

Bhāskararāya treated learning as worship. His commentaries are not dry; they are full of delight in the Goddess and her names.

He reads slowly and widely, and teaches his readers to do the same. A single name, in his hands, opens onto the whole of the tradition.

And he joins things that are often kept apart: Veda and Tantra, logic and devotion, scholarship and practice.

Makhin, part of his title, means one who performs Vedic sacrifices. Bhāskararāya was a Vedic ritualist as well as a Tantric teacher, and saw no conflict between the two.
