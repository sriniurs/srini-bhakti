DATTĀTREYA AND PARAŚURĀMA

The avadhūta and the axe-bearer

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Two names stand behind two of Śrīvidyā’s key texts. Dattātreya, the avadhūta, the three gods in one, is said to have taught Paraśurāma, the warrior with the axe, the worship of Tripurā. The ritual manual called the Paraśurāma Kalpasūtra bears the student’s name.

Their dialogue on the Self, the Tripurā Rahasya, is one of the great works of Indian philosophy. This guide meets teacher and student and sets the scene for the four Tripurā Rahasya guides that follow.

Śrī Dattātreyāya namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: How did a warrior become a seeker?

PROF. BOLT: By losing, Zappy, and then asking the right teacher.

ZAPPY: A warrior learning from a wandering sage?

PROF. BOLT: The fiercest fighter of the Purāṇas, taught by the gentlest teacher. That’s this pair, Zappy.

Dattātreya and Paraśurāma are, for Śrīvidyā, the teacher and student behind two very different kinds of text. One is the Paraśurāma Kalpasūtra, a terse manual of worship. The other is the Tripurā Rahasya, a long, story-filled dialogue on the nature of the Self.

This guide introduces both figures, explains how the tradition traces its texts back to them, and sets the scene for the four Tripurā Rahasya guides that follow.

As always in this series, we describe the texts and their history. The rites the Kalpasūtra sets out are taught by a guru, and this guide does not give them.

WHO IS DATTĀTREYA

The given one, son of Atri
ParentsThe sage Atri and his wife Anasūyā, model of faithfulness
BirthBrahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva “gave” themselves as her sons; hence Datta, “given”
NameDatta + Ātreya, “son of Atri”
FormLater shown with three heads and six arms, with a cow and four dogs
BrothersIn some accounts, Soma (the moon) and Durvāsa are his brothers
FestivalDatta Jayantī, on the full moon of Mārgaśīrṣa

ZAPPY: Three heads?

PROF. BOLT: In later art, yes: Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva in one. The three-headed image is first described in the Marathi Guru Caritra, around 1550.

Dattātreya is born to the sage Atri and his wife Anasūyā, whose faithfulness was so great that the three gods themselves came to her as sons. The name Datta, “given”, refers to that gift; Ātreya means son of Atri.

He is usually counted as a form of Viṣṇu, but also as the three gods in one. In later art, especially from Maharashtra, he has three heads and six arms, and is followed by a cow and four dogs.

Datta Jayantī, his birthday, is kept on the full moon of Mārgaśīrṣa, in November–December, especially in Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra.

THE AVADHŪTA

Free of every rule
AvadhūtaOne who has “shaken off” all ties and conventions
Twenty-four gurusIn the Bhāgavata, he says he learned from earth, air, sky, water, fire, moon, sun and more
Avadhūta GītāA famous song of the free soul, ascribed to him
DogsHis four dogs are often read as the four Vedas
CowThe wish-granting cow, the earth or dharma
PlacesGirnār, Gāṇagāpur and Mahur are great Datta centres

ZAPPY: He learned from a python and a bee?

PROF. BOLT: Among others. The python waits for what comes; the bee takes a little from every flower. Every creature taught him something.

Dattātreya is the great avadhūta, the one who has shaken off all ties, rules and conventions. Stories show him wandering, sometimes in odd company, testing those who would learn from him.

In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa he tells King Yadu of his twenty-four teachers in nature, among them the earth for patience, the python for contentment, the bee for taking only a little, and the child for innocence.

The Avadhūta Gītā, a famous Advaita song of the free soul, is ascribed to him. His four dogs are often read as the four Vedas following him.

DATTĀTREYA’S MANY LINES

Who claims him
Datta sampradāyaA Marathi devotional tradition, with Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha and Nṛsiṃha Sarasvatī
NāthasRevered as Ādi Guru; scholars think he was adopted by them later
MahānubhāvasCount him among their incarnations of God
ŚrīvidyāHonoured as a first teacher of the Kalpasūtra tradition
MysuruJayacāmarājendra Wodeyar wrote a book on him (1957)
One figureYogi, avadhūta, devotee and tantric teacher at once

ZAPPY: He belongs to everyone?

PROF. BOLT: Almost. That’s part of his charm: the guru who refuses to be owned.

Many traditions claim Dattātreya. The Datta sampradāya of Maharashtra and Karnataka honours Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha and Nṛsiṃha Sarasvatī as his incarnations, and reads the Marathi Guru Caritra.

The Nātha yogis revere him as Ādi Guru, the first teacher, though scholars such as James Mallinson think he was adopted into their lineage around the eighteenth century. The Mahānubhāvas count him among their incarnations of God.

In Śrīvidyā he heads the tradition of the Kalpasūtra. The Mysuru Maharaja Jayacāmarājendra Wodeyar, met in our Dasarā guide, wrote a book on him in 1957.

WHO IS PARAŚURĀMA

Rāma with the axe
ParentsThe sage Jamadagni and Reṇukā
AvatāraCounted as the sixth avatāra of Viṣṇu
AxeGiven by Śiva, after long worship
RevengeKārtavīrya’s line killed his father; he destroyed the warrior class again and again
TeacherOf Bhīṣma, Droṇa and Karṇa in the Mahābhārata
ImmortalOne of the cirañjīvīs, said to live still on Mahendra mountain

ZAPPY: He’s still alive?

PROF. BOLT: So tradition says. That’s why he can teach in many ages: Bhīṣma, Karṇa, and in the Tripurā Rahasya, Haritāyana.

Paraśurāma, “Rāma with the axe”, is the son of the sage Jamadagni and Reṇukā, and is counted as the sixth avatāra of Viṣṇu. He received his axe from Śiva.

When the warrior Kārtavīrya’s line wronged his family and killed his father, he destroyed the warrior class again and again, twenty-one times in the usual telling. In the Mahābhārata he teaches arms to Bhīṣma, Droṇa and Karṇa.

He is one of the cirañjīvīs, the long-lived ones, and is said to dwell still on Mahendra mountain. Coastal Kerala and Konkan are said to have been reclaimed from the sea by his axe.

REṆUKĀ, THE GODDESS

A mother honoured as Śakti
Epic storyAt Jamadagni’s order Paraśurāma beheaded Reṇukā, then asked a boon to bring her back
Tripurā RahasyaAfter Jamadagni’s death, Dattātreya met Paraśurāma carrying her and worshipped her as Śakti
AdviceDattātreya told the young man to seek him later
WorshipReṇukā is a goddess in her own right in Karnataka and Maharashtra (Yellammā)
MeaningThe student first met his teacher through his mother
Our noteThe two stories come from different texts

ZAPPY: Dattātreya worshipped Paraśurāma’s mother?

PROF. BOLT: As the Goddess, yes. That’s how the Tripurā Rahasya’s story of teacher and student begins.

The epic story of Reṇukā is a hard one: at his father’s order, Paraśurāma beheads her, then uses the boon he is granted to bring her back to life.

The Tripurā Rahasya tells a different episode. After Jamadagni is killed, Reṇukā resolves to follow him, and Paraśurāma carries both bodies toward the Ganges. On the way, an avadhūta, Dattātreya, honours Reṇukā as Śakti incarnate and tells the young man to come to him later.

Reṇukā is worshipped as a goddess in her own right in Karnataka and Maharashtra, often as Yellammā. In the Tripurā Rahasya, the student first meets his teacher through her.

THE WARRIOR HUMBLED

Meeting Rāma
RāmāyaṇaAfter Rāma breaks Śiva’s bow, Paraśurāma challenges him
ResultRāma strings Viṣṇu’s bow; Paraśurāma bows and withdraws
Tripurā RahasyaHe returns crestfallen, wondering what life is for
SaṃvartaHe meets the avadhūta Saṃvarta, who tells him to go to Dattātreya
GandhamādanaHe finds Dattātreya meditating there
Twelve yearsHe worships Tripurā for twelve years, then comes back with questions

ZAPPY: Losing to Rāma made him a seeker?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Defeat cracked his pride, and that’s where the teaching could get in.

In the Rāmāyaṇa, after Rāma breaks Śiva’s bow at Sītā’s wedding, Paraśurāma confronts him and offers him Viṣṇu’s bow. Rāma strings it easily, and Paraśurāma, humbled, withdraws to the mountains.

The Tripurā Rahasya takes up the story from there. Paraśurāma returns crestfallen, meets the avadhūta Saṃvarta, and is sent to Dattātreya, whom he finds meditating on Gandhamādana.

Dattātreya initiates him into the worship of Tripurā, which he practises for twelve years on Mahendra hill. Then he returns with deeper questions, and the Jñāna Khaṇḍa begins.

THE PARAŚURĀMA KALPASŪTRA

A manual of Śrīvidyā worship
WhatA book of sūtras on the worship of Lalitā and her retinue
OriginFrom the Datta Saṃhitā, said to be 18,000 verses
AbridgedParaśurāma condensed it to 6,000 sūtras in fifty parts, tradition says
SurvivesA shorter text in ten sections, compiled by his pupil Sumedhā
CoversGaṇapati, Bālā, Lalitā, Śyāmalā, Vārāhī, fire rites and general rules
Our ruleWe name its parts; we do not describe its rites

ZAPPY: Why only ten sections?

PROF. BOLT: What survives is a summary of a summary, the tradition says. It’s terse enough to need a commentary on every line.

The Paraśurāma Kalpasūtra is one of the main ritual manuals of Śrīvidyā. Tradition says it began as the Datta Saṃhitā, a vast work by Dattātreya, which Paraśurāma condensed into six thousand sūtras in fifty sections.

What survives is shorter: ten sections, compiled by Paraśurāma’s pupil Sumedhā. They cover the worship of Gaṇapati, Bālā, Lalitā, Śyāmalā and Vārāhī, fire rites, and general rules.

The sūtras are extremely terse, almost code, and cannot be understood without commentary and a teacher. We give their outline only.

READING THE KALPASŪTRA

Nityotsava and Rāmeśvara
NityotsavaBy Umānandanātha, a pupil of Bhāskararāya, 18th century
PurposeSets out the daily and festival worship the sūtras outline
RāmeśvaraA commentary (vṛtti) of 1831 on the sūtras
ModernEdited by A. Mahadeva Sastri (Baroda, 1923) and others
Sūtra sayingsThe Tantras are not outside the Veda; the student must be pure-minded
Teacher’s bookMeant to be read with a guru, not alone

ZAPPY: So I can’t just read it and practise?

PROF. BOLT: No. It says so itself. The book is a map for teacher and student together.

Two later works make the Kalpasūtra usable. The Nityotsava of Umānandanātha, a pupil of Bhāskararāya (our next guide), sets out the daily and festival worship the sūtras outline. Rāmeśvara’s commentary of 1831 explains the sūtras line by line.

The Kalpasūtra makes some general points that matter for understanding Śrīvidyā’s self-image: that the Tantras are not outside the Veda, and that the student must be pure in mind.

A. Mahadeva Sastri’s edition of 1923 made the text and the Nityotsava widely available to scholars.

THE TRIPURĀ RAHASYA

Their great dialogue
Name“The secret of Tripurā”; also the Haritāyana Saṃhitā
Three partsMāhātmya (greatness), Jñāna (knowledge) and Caryā (practice, lost)
FrameHaritāyana tells Nārada what Dattātreya taught Paraśurāma
Jñāna KhaṇḍaAbout 2,160 verses on the Self, in 22 chapters
Ramaṇa MaharṣiValued it highly and often quoted it
NextFour guides, one per quarter of the Jñāna Khaṇḍa

ZAPPY: What’s the ‘secret’?

PROF. BOLT: That Tripurā, the Goddess, is the pure consciousness behind waking, dream and sleep. The four next guides unpack it.

The Tripurā Rahasya, “the secret of Tripurā”, is also called the Haritāyana Saṃhitā after its writer. It is said to have three parts: the Māhātmya Khaṇḍa on the Goddess’s greatness, the Jñāna Khaṇḍa on knowledge, and the Caryā Khaṇḍa on practice, which has not been found.

The Jñāna Khaṇḍa, about 2,160 verses in 22 chapters, is a dialogue between Dattātreya and Paraśurāma on the Self. It teaches through stories: a prince and his wise wife, a city in a hill, a debate at King Janaka’s court.

Ramaṇa Maharṣi valued it highly and often quoted it. A pupil of his translated it into English in the 1930s; our four guides follow that translation’s chapters.

HARITĀYANA AND NĀRADA

Who wrote it down
HaritāyanaSumedhā, son of Hārita; hence the patronymic
Past lifeA boy who died saying the sacred syllable “Aiṃ”, reborn as Sumedhā, the story says
MaduraiHe was worshipping Mīnākṣī when Nārada came to ask for the work
BlessingBrahmā blessed him to write four chapters a day
First readerNārada
KalpasūtraThe same Sumedhā is named as compiler of the Kalpasūtra

ZAPPY: Mīnākṣī again!

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The writer of the Tripurā Rahasya was at Madurai when he was asked to begin.

See: Madurai Mīnākṣī

The Introduction to the Ramanasramam translation tells Haritāyana’s story. A boy named Alarka died saying the sacred syllable “Aiṃ”, and for that merit was reborn as Sumedhā, son of Hārita; Haritāyana is his patronymic.

Paraśurāma passed the teaching to him. When Nārada came from Brahmā’s world to ask for the work, Haritāyana was worshipping Mīnākṣī at Madurai and said he could not remember it all. Brahmā blessed him to write four chapters a day, and Nārada was its first reader.

The same name, Sumedhā, is given for the compiler of the Kalpasūtra, so tradition makes one pupil responsible for both the manual and the dialogue.

WHAT THEY TEACH

StudentEven a great warrior must become a beginner

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: That Paraśurāma, after all his victories, sat down and asked a question. That’s where wisdom starts.

Dattātreya is the teacher who belongs to no single school, and so to all of them. Paraśurāma is the student who had to lose before he could learn.

Together they stand behind both sides of Śrīvidyā: ritual worship, set out in the Kalpasūtra, and knowledge of the Self, set out in the Tripurā Rahasya. The tradition does not see these as rivals.

The four guides that follow tell the Tripurā Rahasya’s stories in order, from Paraśurāma’s first question to the end.

Saṃvarta, the avadhūta who sends Paraśurāma to Dattātreya, is a brother of Bṛhaspati, teacher of the gods. Like Dattātreya, he appears in several Purāṇas as an unconventional, wandering sage.
