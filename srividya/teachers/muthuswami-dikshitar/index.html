MUTTUSVĀMI DĪKṢITAR

The Śrīvidyā composer

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar put Śrīvidyā into music. Initiated at Kāśī, signing every song “Guruguha”, he walked from temple to temple composing in Sanskrit, and wrote the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, the Śrīcakra in eleven songs.

This guide tells his life from Tiruvārūr to Kāśī, Tiruttani and Ettayapuram, describes his song-sets and his music, and explains how his songs survived.

Śrī Guruguhāya namaḥ,

AT A GLANCE

SignatureGuruguha, a name of Murugan, in every song
ŚrīvidyāInitiated as Cidānandanātha; his songs are full of Śrīvidyā learning
Famous setThe Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, on the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra

ZAPPY: A composer in a teachers series?

PROF. BOLT: He taught Śrīvidyā to millions who never read a Tantra, Zappy, by singing it.

Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar is the youngest of the three great composers of Carnatic music, and the one most deeply rooted in Śrīvidyā. He was an initiated upāsaka, and his songs, mostly in Sanskrit, are full of the tradition’s learning.

His Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, walking through the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra, are the best-known Śrīvidyā music ever written. Our Tiruvārūr guide lists them with their rāgas.

This guide tells his life, from Tiruvārūr to Kāśī and back, describes his song-sets and his music, and ends at Ettayapuram, where he died as his pupils sang to Mīnākṣī.

FAMILY AND CHILDHOOD

ZAPPY: He heard Western music?

PROF. BOLT: As a boy, at Madras. Later he wrote simple songs to European tunes for his brother’s violin.

Muttusvāmi was born at Tiruvārūr in 1775 or 1776, the son of Rāmasvāmi Dīkṣitar, a composer credited with creating the rāga Haṃsadhvani. His parents had prayed for a child at Vaidīśvaran Koil, and named him after its Murugan, Muttukumāra.

The family moved to Manali, near Madras, under the patronage of the Mudaliar zamindars. There the boys heard the band of the British garrison at Fort St George, an unusual experience for a Carnatic musician of the time.

His brothers, Cinnasvāmi and Bālusvāmi, were musicians too. Bālusvāmi took up the violin, and Muttusvāmi later wrote simple songs to European tunes to help him.

ZAPPY: Dhrupad in Carnatic music?

PROF. BOLT: You can hear it in his slow, stately songs. Kāśī gave him the north’s grandeur.

Cidambaranātha Yogī, a wandering ascetic who had initiated Rāmasvāmi into Śrīvidyā, heard the boy sing and asked to take him as a disciple. Rāmasvāmi agreed, and the yogi took Muttusvāmi north to Kāśī, where he stayed about six years.

There he learned music, philosophy and yoga, and heard the dhrupad style of north Indian music, whose slow grandeur shaped his own. He was initiated into Śrīvidyā with the name Cidānandanātha.

His guru told him to worship Annapūrṇā, the Goddess of Kāśī, all his life. A family legend says that before he left, the yogi sent him into the Gaṅgā, and he came up holding a vīṇā.

TIRUTTANI AND THE FIRST SONG

Guruguha
PlaceTiruttani, the Murugan hill-temple near Tirupati
LegendAn old man put sugar candy in his mouth and vanished: Murugan himself
First song“Śrīnāthādi guruguho jayati” in Māyāmāḷavagauḷa
Names in itHis guru’s name (Śrīnātha) and his own (Cidānanda)
SignatureGuruguha, the Guru in the cave of the heart, from then on
SetThe Guruguha vibhakti kṛtis, eight songs in the eight cases

ZAPPY: Why ‘Guruguha’?

PROF. BOLT: Guha is Murugan, who dwells in the cave (guhā) of the heart. Guru-guha honours both his teacher and his god.

Returning south after his guru’s death, he went to Tiruttani, the Murugan hill-temple near Tirupati. The story says an old man put sugar candy in his mouth and vanished; Dīkṣitar understood it was Murugan himself.

He at once composed his first song, “Śrīnāthādi guruguho jayati”, in Māyāmāḷavagauḷa. It names both his guru, Śrīnātha, and himself, Cidānanda, in its words.

From then on he signed every song “Guruguha”: the Guru who dwells in the cave (guhā) of the heart, a name of Murugan. He followed with the Guruguha vibhakti kṛtis, eight songs in the eight grammatical cases.

Temple by temple

MāyavaramThe Abhayāmbā vibhakti kṛtis

ZAPPY: Did he write for every temple he visited?

PROF. BOLT: Almost. His songs are a pilgrim’s map of South India.

Much of his life was spent travelling from temple to temple, composing for each deity. Kāñcī, Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, Chidambaram, Vaidīśvaran Koil, Māyavaram, Tiruvārūr, Thanjavur, Madurai and many more appear in his songs.

At Māyavaram he composed the Abhayāmbā vibhakti kṛtis; at Tiruvārūr, the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa, the Nīlotpalāmbā and Tyāgarāja sets, the Navagraha kṛtis and the songs for sixteen forms of Gaṇapati.

One count finds about seventy-four temples named in his songs. Together they form a map of South Indian pilgrimage set to music.

Lineage“Worshipped by Durvāsa”: the Kādi line’s mark

Rāga namesThe rāga’s name hidden in the words of each song

ZAPPY: Can a song teach Śrīvidyā?

PROF. BOLT: It can teach its map and its love. The rest needs a teacher. Dīkṣitar gave us the map in music.

Dīkṣitar’s Śrīvidyā learning shows everywhere. In the Navāvaraṇa kṛtis he names, enclosure by enclosure, the presiding goddesses, yoginīs, siddhis and mudrās as the Śrīvidyā texts describe them.

He calls Kamalāmbā “worshipped by Durvāsa”, the mark of the Kādi line. He weaves phrases from the Lalitā Sahasranāma into his lyrics, and hides the name of each song’s rāga in its words.

The songs are public, sung in concerts and homes everywhere. The initiated worship they describe remains with the guru, and this series follows the same line.

ZAPPY: Why did he love sets?

PROF. BOLT: A set lets him walk round a subject completely: every case, every planet, every enclosure. It’s a scholar’s kind of devotion.

See: Tiruvārūr Kamalāmbā for the Navāvaraṇa kṛtis and their rāgas

Dīkṣitar loved to compose in sets. The vibhakti sets put a deity’s name in each of the eight Sanskrit cases; the Navāvaraṇa set walks through the Śrīcakra; the Navagraha set gives one song to each planet.

The Guruguha set at Tiruttani, the Abhayāmbā set at Māyavaram and the Kamalāmbā, Nīlotpalāmbā and Tyāgarāja sets at Tiruvārūr are the best known. The Ṣoḍaśa Gaṇapati songs honour sixteen forms of Gaṇapati around Tiruvārūr.

A set lets him cover a subject completely, from every side. It is a scholar’s devotion: thorough, ordered and complete.

HIS MUSIC

What makes it his
VīṇāGamakas (graces) shaped by the vīṇā
TempoMostly slow and majestic
RāgasAbout 193; he used the older Veṅkaṭamakhin scheme
All seventy-twoSongs in each of the 72 melas, by tradition
TālasThe only composer with songs in all seven basic tālas
NottusvarasAbout 39 simple songs to European tunes, for his brother’s violin

ZAPPY: Why is his music called ‘difficult’?

PROF. BOLT: Slow tempo, dense Sanskrit, rare rāgas. But once you hear it, it’s like walking into a temple.

His music is shaped by the vīṇā, his instrument. Its gamakas, the slides and oscillations between notes, give his songs their characteristic weight.

He used about 193 rāgas, following the older Veṅkaṭamakhin system, and is credited with songs in all seventy-two melas. He is said to be the only composer with songs in all seven basic tālas.

He also wrote about thirty-nine nottusvaras, short Sanskrit songs set to European tunes, to help his brother learn the violin. They were first printed in 1833.

ZAPPY: Did the rain really come?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the family memory. What’s certain is that the song is still sung for rain today.

See: Madurai Mīnākṣī

In his last years he lived at Ettayapuram under the patronage of its zamindar. Tradition says that during a drought he sang “Ānandāmṛtakarṣiṇi” in the rāga Amṛtavarṣiṇī, “the one who showers nectar”, and rain fell. The song is still sung for rain.

On Deepavali day, 21 October 1835, after his daily worship, he asked his pupils to sing his song “Mīnākṣi me mudam dehi” in Gamakakriyā. He passed away as they sang.

He had no son; his music was carried on by the line of his brother Bālusvāmi. A memorial stands at Ettayapuram.

HOW HIS SONGS SURVIVED

GroupsNavāvaraṇa songs sung in groups each Navarātri

ZAPPY: Without that book?

PROF. BOLT: We might have lost much of him. One devoted grand-nephew saved a treasure.

Subbarāma Dīkṣitar, Bālusvāmi’s adopted son, published the Saṅgīta Sampradāya Pradarśinī in Telugu in 1904, recording 229 of Muttusvāmi’s songs with notation. It remains a key source.

Today about 480 of his songs are identified, though scholars debate some ascriptions. They are a pillar of the Carnatic concert repertoire.

During Navarātri, groups across South India sing his Navāvaraṇa kṛtis together, often one each night, as our Navarātri guide describes.

THE TRINITY

DīkṣitarThe Śrīvidyā scholar, in Sanskrit
TiruvārūrAll three born there
MeetingHe is said to have met Tyāgarāja at Tiruvaiyāru
TogetherFounders of classical Carnatic music

ZAPPY: Who’s the greatest?

PROF. BOLT: Musicians refuse to choose. Each is a different temple: Śyāma Śāstri’s love, Tyāgarāja’s longing, Dīkṣitar’s grandeur.

Śyāma Śāstri, Tyāgarāja and Dīkṣitar are the Trinity of Carnatic music. All three were born at Tiruvārūr within about fourteen years, and their songs form the core of the classical repertoire.

Each has his own voice. Śyāma Śāstri, priest of the golden Kāmākṣī, sang mostly to the Goddess; Tyāgarāja, in Telugu, sang to Rāma; Dīkṣitar, mostly in Sanskrit, sang to every deity, with Śrīvidyā at the heart.

Tradition says Dīkṣitar met Tyāgarāja at Tiruvaiyāru. Whether or not they did, their music has lived side by side ever since.

WHAT HE TEACHES

For the reader

GuruHis signature honours his teacher in every song
Public and secretHe shared the map, kept the rites

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Guruguha: the Guru hidden in the cave of the heart. That’s where his music points.

Dīkṣitar shows that music can be worship and scholarship at once. His songs are offerings, and they are also exact descriptions of temples, deities and the Śrīcakra.

He shared the map of Śrīvidyā openly in song while keeping its initiated rites private, a balance this series tries to follow.

And his signature, Guruguha, is a lesson on its own: the Guru is hidden in the cave of the heart, and every song points there.

A kṛti is the main song form of Carnatic music, usually in three sections: pallavi, anupallavi and caraṇam. Dīkṣitar often uses a samaṣṭi caraṇam, a single closing section, in his songs.
