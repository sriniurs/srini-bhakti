FOREWORD

The Tripurā Rahasya is Śrīvidyā’s great book of knowledge. In it, Dattātreya answers Paraśurāma, who has worshipped the Goddess for twelve years and still feels something missing.

Part 1 covers Chapters I–IV: the question, the trap of mere duty, the key word vicāra, and the prince Hemacūḍa and his wise wife Hemalekhā.

NarratorHaritāyana (Sumedhā), Paraśurāma’s pupil, telling it to Nārada

ZAPPY: A Śrīvidyā book that isn’t about ritual?

PROF. BOLT: This one is about waking up, Zappy. The Goddess here is pure awareness.

The Tripurā Rahasya is a Sanskrit work in which Dattātreya teaches Paraśurāma about the nature of the Self. Tripurā, the Goddess, is here not mainly a figure to worship but consciousness itself, the awareness in which everything appears.

It teaches through stories. A prince and his wise wife, a scholar humbled by a woman ascetic, a king who finds a world inside a hill: each story leads to the same point.

This series retells the Jñāna Khaṇḍa, the part on knowledge, in four parts. Part 1 covers Chapters I to IV.

THE FRAME

A story inside a story
Outer frameHaritāyana tells the work to the sage Nārada
Inner frameDattātreya teaches Paraśurāma
InnermostCharacters in Dattātreya’s stories teach each other
Place of tellingTradition sets Haritāyana’s writing near Madurai
Three partsMāhātmya (glory), Jñāna (knowledge), Caryā (practice, lost)
Which we readThe Jñāna Khaṇḍa, in 22 chapters

ZAPPY: Why so many frames?

PROF. BOLT: Each teacher was once a student. The frames show knowledge passed hand to hand.

The work has nested frames. Haritāyana, a pupil of Paraśurāma, tells it to the sage Nārada. Within his telling, Dattātreya teaches Paraśurāma; within Dattātreya’s teaching, characters teach each other.

The whole is said to have three parts: the Māhātmya Khaṇḍa on the Goddess’s glory, the Jñāna Khaṇḍa on knowledge, and the Caryā Khaṇḍa on practice, which has not survived.

Our Dattātreya and Paraśurāma guide tells how these two became the founders of a Śrīvidyā line. Here we read what passed between them.

PARAŚURĀMA’S QUESTION

Chapter I
Who he isThe axe-bearing Brahmin warrior, now at peace after his wars
His practiceTwelve years of worship of Tripurā on Mahendra mountain
His uneaseRitual done, yet something still missing
He goesTo his guru Dattātreya on Gandhamādana
He asksWhat is the highest good, beyond what worship gives?
Datta’s replyA long answer, told mostly through stories

ZAPPY: Twelve years of worship and still unsatisfied?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the honest start of this book. Practice ripens into a question.

Paraśurāma, after his wars against the kṣatriyas, has spent twelve years worshipping Tripurā on Mahendra mountain. His worship is complete, but he is not at peace.

He goes to his guru Dattātreya on Gandhamādana and asks what lies beyond what worship gives. Dattātreya praises the question: it shows his worship has ripened.

The rest of the book is Dattātreya’s answer, given mostly through stories.

THE TRAP OF MERE DUTY

Chapter II
Most peopleDo what they’re told, chase pleasure, fear pain
Action aloneCannot give lasting happiness; its fruits run out
VicāraDiscrimination: asking what is real and what passes
Frog in a wellThinks its well is the whole world
BeginningVicāra starts with grace and with dissatisfaction
CompanyKeep company with the wise

ZAPPY: So doing your duty is bad?

PROF. BOLT: Not bad, but not enough. Datta wants Paraśurāma to stop being a frog in a well.

Dattātreya begins by describing how most people live: following custom, chasing pleasure, avoiding pain, never asking what any of it is for. Action alone, he says, cannot give lasting happiness, because its results run out.

He compares such a person to a frog in a well, which thinks its small circle of water is the whole world.

The way out is vicāra, inquiry. It begins with grace, which shows itself as a real dissatisfaction, and grows through the company of the wise.

WHAT IS VICĀRA?

The key word of the book
MeaningInquiry, careful thinking about what is real
Not argumentNot winning debates; looking at experience itself
The first stepSeeing that worldly happiness never lasts
Its causeGrace, shown as a sincere longing
Its fruitFreedom while living
In the bookEvery story turns on someone beginning to inquire

ZAPPY: How do I start?

PROF. BOLT: Ask what in you stays the same while everything else changes. Then keep asking.

Vicāra is the key word of the Tripurā Rahasya. It means careful inquiry into what is real: not argument for its own sake, but looking at experience itself.

The book’s stories nearly all turn on the moment someone begins to inquire. Before that moment, a character is lost in the world; after it, freedom is possible.

Ramaṇa Maharṣi’s teaching of self-inquiry, “Who am I?”, is close to this, which is one reason he valued the book.

HEMACŪḌA GOES HUNTING

Chapter III
PrinceHemacūḍa, son of the king of Daśārṇa
StormLost in the forest in a storm during a hunt
HermitageFinds the ashram of the sage Vyāghrapāda
HemalekhāThe sage’s foster-daughter, beautiful and calm
MarriageHe falls in love; the sage gives her in marriage
PuzzleShe does not care for pleasures as he does

ZAPPY: A love story in a book on the Self?

PROF. BOLT: The best teaching in this book comes from Hemalekhā. The love story is how she gets to teach.

Dattātreya tells the story of Hemacūḍa, son of the king of Daśārṇa. Out hunting, the prince is caught in a storm and finds shelter at the hermitage of the sage Vyāghrapāda.

There he meets Hemalekhā, the sage’s foster-daughter. He falls in love; the sage agrees, and they are married and return to the palace.

But Hemalekhā does not take part in the pleasures of court life as he does. The prince is puzzled, and the puzzle starts the teaching.

HEMALEKHĀ’S INDIFFERENCE

Chapter IV
Prince’s questionWhy doesn’t she enjoy their life as he does?
Her answerShe wonders what pleasure is, and finds no answer
BeautyLies in the mind of the one who looks
PleasureEnds; and its loss brings sorrow
DispassionComes from seeing this clearly, not from forcing it
NextShe tells him a story to explain

ZAPPY: She talks him out of loving her?

PROF. BOLT: No, into seeing clearly. He loves her more for it, and begins to ask questions himself.

When Hemacūḍa asks why she seems indifferent, Hemalekhā answers that she has been trying to find out what pleasure really is, and cannot find it.

She points out that beauty is not in the object but in the mind that sees it; that every pleasure ends; and that its end brings sorrow. Seeing this clearly, desire loosens by itself.

To make this clearer, she tells him a story, the parable of the city with ten gates, which begins Part 2.

DISPASSION

Vairāgya
MeaningLoosened desire; not hatred of the world
SourceSeeing that pleasures are fleeting
NotRunning away, or pretending not to want
HemalekhāLives fully in the palace, but is not owned by it
HemacūḍaAt first thinks she is cold; later sees she is free
LessonClear seeing comes first, letting go follows

ZAPPY: Does dispassion mean being dull?

PROF. BOLT: Hemalekhā isn’t dull. She’s the liveliest person in the book.

Vairāgya, dispassion, is often misunderstood as dislike of the world. Hemalekhā shows something else: she lives in the palace, with her husband, fully present, yet nothing there owns her.

It comes from seeing clearly, not from forcing oneself to give things up. Forcing, the book suggests, only drives desire underground.

Hemacūḍa at first thinks she is cold. Later he understands that she is free, and wants that freedom for himself.

A WOMAN TEACHER

A notable feature
HemalekhāTeaches her husband, the prince
LaterAn unnamed woman ascetic humbles the scholar Aṣṭāvakra
The GoddessHerself teaches the sages in the Vidyā Gītā
In ŚrīvidyāWisdom is the Goddess; women teachers fit naturally
RareFew classical texts give women this role so often
Coming upAll three in the later parts of this series

ZAPPY: Is that unusual?

PROF. BOLT: Quite. In the Tripurā Rahasya the wisest voices are often women’s.

A striking feature of the Tripurā Rahasya is how often women are the teachers. Hemalekhā teaches her husband; an unnamed woman ascetic exposes the pride of the young scholar Aṣṭāvakra; and in the Vidyā Gītā, the Goddess herself teaches the sages.

In Śrīvidyā, wisdom is the Goddess, so women teachers fit naturally into the book’s world.

These episodes come in Parts 3 and 4 of this series.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: The frog thinks its well is the world. Climb out and look.

Part 1 sets up the book’s whole movement. Practice ripens into a real question; mere duty fails to satisfy; inquiry begins.

Hemalekhā shows what inquiry does: it reveals that pleasure and beauty are in the mind, and desire loosens by itself.

And the frame reminds us that teachers come in many forms: a guru on a mountain, a wife in a palace.

Where the facts come from

Sources differ / unverified: Chapter numbers follow the Ramanasramam translation. Summaries and renderings are our own plain-English retellings, not quotations. The text is traditionally ascribed to Haritāyana; modern scholars date it to the medieval period. Only the Jñāna Khaṇḍa is covered in this series.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
