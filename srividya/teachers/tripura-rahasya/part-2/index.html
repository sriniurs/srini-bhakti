TRIPURĀ RAHASYA · 2

The city with ten gates · explained and illustrated

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

HOW THIS WORKS: Tripurā Rahasya Part 2 (Chapters V–X): Hemalekhā’s parable of the city with ten gates and its key, faith and argument, the mirror of awareness, and Hemacūḍa’s awakening.

FOREWORD

In Part 2, Hemalekhā tells her husband a parable of a city with ten gates, then gives its key: the city is the body, and the trapped heroine is consciousness itself.

Chapters V–X follow Hemacūḍa from doubt to a glimpse of the Self, and then to the harder lesson: freedom with eyes open.

Śrī Tripurāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Who is the woman in the city?

PROF. BOLT: You are, Zappy, or rather your awareness.

ZAPPY: Is this the part where someone gets enlightened?

PROF. BOLT: Hemacūḍa does, Zappy, and then has to learn that it doesn’t stop at the door of his meditation room.

Part 2 covers Chapters V to X, the heart of the Hemacūḍa story. Hemalekhā tells a parable, explains it, and guides her husband to recognise the Self.

Then comes the harder lesson: Hemacūḍa can find peace only with his eyes closed. Hemalekhā teaches him to find it in the middle of life.

Throughout, Dattātreya is still speaking to Paraśurāma; the story is his answer to Paraśurāma’s question.

THE CITY WITH TEN GATES

Chapter V
HeroineA woman who has a friend, but falls into bad company
Her husbandA weak man, ruled by his household
The householdA restless son, an unsteady daughter-in-law and her five sons
Greedy onesA hungry woman with fierce sons who devour everything
The cityWith ten gates, guarded by a tireless watchman
The rescueWhen the friend is trusted again, the confusion ends

ZAPPY: Who are these people?

PROF. BOLT: Hemalekhā tells the story first and gives the key later. Try guessing before you turn the page.

Hemalekhā tells of a woman who has a good friend but falls into bad company, and marries a weak husband who is ruled by his household: a restless son, an unsteady daughter-in-law with five sons, a greedy woman with fierce sons.

They live in a city with ten gates, kept going by a tireless watchman. The household brings the woman nothing but trouble.

Only when she listens to her old friend again does the confusion end. Hemalekhā does not explain the story at once; Hemacūḍa, and the reader, are left to guess.

THE KEY

Chapter VIII
MotherConsciousness itself, Tripurā
Her friendIntellect, which can lead her home
Darkness and follyIgnorance and delusion
The sonThe restless mind; his wife, thought
Five grandsonsThe five senses
The cityThe body, with its ten openings, kept alive by breath

ZAPPY: So the whole family lives inside me?

PROF. BOLT: All of it. The story is a map of your own head.

In Chapter VIII Hemalekhā gives the key. The woman is consciousness itself; her friend is the intellect; her bad company is ignorance and delusion.

The restless son is the mind, his wife is thought, and her five sons are the senses. The greedy woman is desire, with anger and greed as her sons. The city with ten gates is the body, and the watchman is the breath.

The meaning: consciousness, entangled with the mind and senses, forgets itself. The intellect, rightly used, leads it home.

FAITH AND ARGUMENT

Chapter VI
HemacūḍaWants proof before he will believe
HemalekhāSome trust is needed to begin
Kauśika and ŚuṅgaA story of a sage and a logician who argues that all is void
Endless proofsEvery proof needs a standard, which needs proof, without end
LessonArguing never ends; inquiry into experience does
FaithNot blind belief: enough trust to look

ZAPPY: Isn’t faith the opposite of inquiry?

PROF. BOLT: Here it’s the start of it. You trust the map enough to walk the road and check.

Hemacūḍa wants proof. Hemalekhā replies that some trust is needed to begin any inquiry, and tells a story about a sage, Kauśika, and a logician, Śuṅga, who argued that everything is void.

Argument, she shows, can go on forever: every proof needs a standard, which itself needs proof. That regress never ends.

Inquiry into one’s own experience is different. It does not need to win an argument; it needs only enough trust to look.

GOD AS INTELLIGENCE

Chapter VII
QuestionWhat is the Goddess really?
AnswerPure awareness, in which all things appear
MirrorA mirror shows many images, yet is never changed by them
WorldImages in the mirror of consciousness
MārkaṇḍeyaA story of the sage is used to illustrate
TripurāCalled so because she runs through three states and three worlds

ZAPPY: The Goddess is a mirror?

PROF. BOLT: Like one. Everything appears in her, and none of it stains her.

What, then, is the Goddess? Hemalekhā answers that she is pure awareness, in which all things appear. The world is like images in a mirror: many and changing, while the mirror is unchanged.

A story of the sage Mārkaṇḍeya is used to show how vast worlds can appear in consciousness.

The name Tripurā, “of the three cities”, is read here as the awareness running through the three states of waking, dream and sleep.

HEMACŪḌA INQUIRES

Chapter IX
RetreatHe sits alone in a crystal room of the palace
MethodTurns attention back from the senses and thoughts
GlimpseA vast, bright stillness, beyond words
ReturnHe opens his eyes and the world seems to cloud it
HemalekhāExplains: the Self is not gained, only recognised
Ever presentIt was there before, and is there now

ZAPPY: He got it, and then lost it?

PROF. BOLT: He thought so. Hemalekhā tells him he never lost it; he only looked away.

Hemacūḍa now practises. He sits alone in a crystal room of the palace and turns his attention back from the senses and from thoughts.

He has a glimpse of something vast and still, beyond words. When he opens his eyes, the world rushes back and seems to hide it, and he is distressed.

Hemalekhā explains that the Self is not something gained in meditation and lost afterward. It is always present; only attention moves.

THE FOURTH STATE

Turīya
Three statesWaking, dream, deep sleep
FourthTurīya: the witness present in all three
Not a fourth roomMore like the light in which the three rooms are seen
SleepShows awareness without objects
UpaniṣadsThe Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad names it
HereHemalekhā’s teaching rests on it

ZAPPY: Was I aware in deep sleep?

PROF. BOLT: You wake up and say ‘I slept well’. Something was there to know it.

Indian philosophy speaks of three states, waking, dream and deep sleep, and a fourth, turīya, which is not a further state but the awareness present in all three.

Deep sleep is a clue: there are no objects, yet on waking we know we slept well. Something was present to know it.

The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad names turīya, and Hemalekhā’s teaching rests on it.

SAMĀDHI WITH EYES OPEN

Chapter X
ProblemHemacūḍa can only find peace with his eyes closed
HemalekhāFreedom that needs a closed door is not complete
PracticeRecognise the same awareness in all activity
ResultSahaja: natural, unbroken stillness
JīvanmuktiLiberation while living, working and ruling
HemacūḍaBecomes free and rules wisely

ZAPPY: Can you be enlightened and busy?

PROF. BOLT: That’s Hemalekhā’s whole point. A king can be free on his throne.

Hemacūḍa can now rest in stillness, but only with his eyes shut. Hemalekhā tells him that a freedom which needs a closed door is not complete.

She teaches him to recognise the same awareness in all activity: eating, talking, ruling. This is sahaja, the natural state, unbroken by action.

Hemacūḍa becomes a jīvanmukta, liberated while living, and rules his kingdom wisely.

THE CITY OF WISDOM

Ripples of one awakening
His familyThe king and the prince’s brothers learn from them
The cityEveryone becomes wise, even the servants
ParrotsSaid to repeat words of wisdom in the gardens
VisitorsSages come and marvel
LessonSatsaṅga, good company, comes first
DattātreyaTells Paraśurāma: seek the wise

ZAPPY: Even the parrots?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the story’s joke and its lesson: wisdom spreads like a song in a house.

The story ends with wisdom spreading. The king, Hemacūḍa’s brothers and the whole court learn from Hemalekhā and Hemacūḍa, until even the servants are wise.

The story adds that even the parrots in the gardens repeated words of wisdom, and visiting sages marvelled at the city.

Dattātreya draws the lesson for Paraśurāma: satsaṅga, the company of the wise, comes first.

WHAT THIS PART TEACHES

For the reader
MapThe mind’s family, from the parable
TrustEnough to look for yourself
MirrorAwareness is untouched by what appears in it
RecogniseDon’t try to gain what you already are
Open eyesFreedom in daily life, not only in meditation
CompanyLive among the wise

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: You don’t get the Self. You stop overlooking it.

Part 2 gives the book’s central teaching in story form. The mind and senses entangle awareness; the intellect, used rightly, untangles it.

The Self is not reached but recognised. And recognition must reach into daily life, or it is not complete.

Hemalekhā, a wife in a palace, is the clearest teacher in the book.

Citi, consciousness, is one of the Goddess’s names in the Lalitā Sahasranāma. The Tripurā Rahasya’s whole teaching could be read as a commentary on that one name.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: The Tripurā Rahasya, Jñāna Khaṇḍa, tr. Swami Sri Ramananda Saraswathi (Munagala S. Venkataramaiah), Sri Ramanasramam, 1938; later editions with a foreword by A. W. Chadwick; Wikipedia, “Tripura Rahasya”; Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad on the four states.

Sources differ / unverified: Chapter numbers follow the Ramanasramam translation. Summaries and renderings are our own plain-English retellings, not quotations. The text is traditionally ascribed to Haritāyana; modern scholars date it to the medieval period. Only the Jñāna Khaṇḍa is covered in this series. The English names of the parable’s characters follow the Ramanasramam translation’s renderings.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
