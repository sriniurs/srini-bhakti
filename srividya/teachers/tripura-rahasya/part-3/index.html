TRIPURĀ RAHASYA · 3

Worlds, dreams and a necklace never worn

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Part 3 of the Tripurā Rahasya shakes the solid world: a picture on a screen, a world inside a hill where time runs differently, dreams as real as waking.

Then, at King Janaka’s court, the young champion Aṣṭāvakra is undone by one question from a woman ascetic, and learns the difference between knowing about and knowing.

ZAPPY: Worlds inside hills?

PROF. BOLT: It’s the book’s science fiction chapter, Zappy. It’s there to shake your sense of what’s solid.

Part 3 covers Chapters XI to XVI. Dattātreya leaves the Hemacūḍa story and uses new tales to loosen Paraśurāma’s sense of what is solid: worlds within worlds, time that stretches, dreams that feel like waking.

Then comes the story of Aṣṭāvakra at King Janaka’s court, a lesson on the difference between knowing about the Self and knowing it.

Each story makes the same point from a new side: awareness is the one constant.

A PICTURE ON A SCREEN

Chapter XI
Rival viewsDattātreya weighs other schools’ accounts of the world
His imageThe world is like a picture on the screen of the Self
ScreenUnchanged by the picture
PictureReal as a picture; not separate from the screen
ViśvāmitraA story of the sage creating a new heaven is used as an example
LessonCreation is a power of consciousness

ZAPPY: Is the world fake, then?

PROF. BOLT: Not fake. Real as a picture is real. The question is what it’s painted on.

Dattātreya examines other schools’ accounts of the world, and offers his own image: the world is like a picture painted on the screen of the Self.

The picture is real as a picture; it is not separate from the screen, and the screen is not changed by it. A story of the sage Viśvāmitra creating a new heaven is used to show creation as a power of consciousness.

This is not a claim that the world is fake. It is a claim about what the world is made of.

A WORLD INSIDE A HILL

Chapter XII
CharactersKing Suṣeṇa, and Mahāsena, son of the sage Gaṇa
DiscoveryMahāsena finds a whole world inside a hill
InsidePeople, kings and lives, unaware of the world outside
TimeA day outside is ages inside
ReturnComing back, he finds everything changed
LessonSpace and time belong to the mind that sees them

ZAPPY: Like a film where years pass in minutes?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The Tripurā Rahasya got there first.

In Chapter XII a story tells of King Suṣeṇa and Mahāsena, son of the sage Gaṇa. Mahāsena discovers a whole world inside a hill, with its own kings and people, unaware of anything outside.

Time runs differently there: a single day outside equals ages within. When he returns, he finds his own world changed.

The point is that space and time are not fixed containers; they belong to the mind that experiences them.

DREAM AND WAKING

Chapter XIII
DreamFeels real while it lasts
WakingFeels real while it lasts
TestWhat remains the same in both? Awareness
DifferenceOf length and agreement, not of kind
UpshotDon’t take either as final
ComfortThe dreamer is never harmed by the dream

ZAPPY: Is my life a dream?

PROF. BOLT: The book says: look at what knows both dreaming and waking. That is never a dream.

Chapter XIII compares dream and waking. While a dream lasts it seems real; so does waking. The difference is in length and in how far experiences agree with each other, not in kind.

What remains through both is awareness, which knows the dream and knows waking.

So the book says neither should be taken as final. The dreamer is never harmed by the dream, however frightening it seems.

WILL AND CREATION

Chapter XIV
SourceCreation begins as will within awareness
ŚivaPure “I”
ŚaktiIts power to know and act
Sadāśiva, Īśvara“I am this”, “this I am”
MāyāThe sense of separation
Same schemeAs in Kashmir Śaiva and Śrīvidyā thought

ZAPPY: Why five steps?

PROF. BOLT: To show how one awareness seems to become many, without ever really breaking.

Chapter XIV explains how creation arises within awareness as will. It uses a scheme of principles, or tattvas: Śiva, pure “I”; Śakti, its power; Sadāśiva and Īśvara, two stages of “I” and “this” coming apart; and Māyā, the sense of separation.

The same scheme is found in Kashmir Śaiva and Śrīvidyā philosophy.

Its purpose is to show how one awareness seems to become many without ever truly dividing.

JANAKA’S SACRIFICE

AṣṭāvakraThe young son of Kahola, who arrives and wins
VictoryHe frees the scholars and is honoured
PrideHe grows proud of his learning
ComingSomeone unexpected

ZAPPY: Drowned for losing a debate?

PROF. BOLT: Old stories had high stakes. This one is about what learning is, and isn’t.

Chapter XV tells of a great sacrifice held by King Janaka of Videha. A son of Varuṇa defeats scholar after scholar in debate, and the losers are drowned.

The young Aṣṭāvakra, son of Kahola, arrives and wins, freeing the scholars. He is honoured by all, and grows proud of his learning.

This story also appears in the Mahābhārata; the Tripurā Rahasya continues it in its own way.

THE WOMAN ASCETIC

Chapter XV
ArrivalAn unnamed woman ascetic comes to the court
Her questionShe asks Aṣṭāvakra about the Self
His answerFluent, learned, quoted from books
Her verdictSecond-hand knowledge, not his own experience
Pearl necklaceLike describing a necklace one has never worn
ThenShe vanishes, leaving him shaken

ZAPPY: She beat the champion?

PROF. BOLT: With one question. He could talk about the Self, but had not found it.

Then an unnamed woman ascetic arrives at the court. She asks Aṣṭāvakra about the Self, and he answers fluently from the books.

She tells him his knowledge is second-hand: like someone describing a pearl necklace he has never worn. Then she disappears.

Aṣṭāvakra, champion of debates, is shaken. For the first time he sees the gap between talking about the Self and knowing it.

JANAKA TEACHES AṢṬĀVAKRA

Chapter XVI
AṣṭāvakraHumbled, asks Janaka for help
Two kinds of knowingIllumination itself versus thinking about it
SleepAwareness without objects shows the way
Tarred mirrorA dirty mirror shows nothing; clean it and it shines
PracticeLet thought rest and notice what remains
ResultAṣṭāvakra finds what he had only described

ZAPPY: The king teaches the scholar?

PROF. BOLT: A king who knew from experience. In this book, rank and learning don’t decide who teaches.

Humbled, Aṣṭāvakra turns to Janaka, who teaches him. Janaka distinguishes illumination itself from deliberation about it: thinking about light is not light.

He points to deep sleep, where awareness rests without objects, and uses the image of a mirror covered in tar: clean it, and it shines by itself.

Aṣṭāvakra lets thought rest, and finds what he had only described.

LEARNING AND KNOWING

ZAPPY: So I shouldn’t read these guides?

PROF. BOLT: Read them, then shut the tab and look. That’s what the ascetic would say.

A theme runs through this part: learning is useful, but it is not the goal. The Self must be known directly.

Pride in learning is a special trap for scholars. The cure is humility, which here arrives through a woman ascetic and a king.

For readers of guides like this one, the lesson is plain: read, and then look for yourself.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Don’t describe a necklace you’ve never worn.

Part 3 loosens the reader’s hold on what seems solid: the world as a picture, time as stretchable, dream and waking as alike.

It then turns to the reader’s own learning, and warns against mistaking description for knowledge.

The image of the necklace never worn is one of the most memorable in the book.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: The Tripurā Rahasya, Jñāna Khaṇḍa, tr. Swami Sri Ramananda Saraswathi (Munagala S. Venkataramaiah), Sri Ramanasramam, 1938; later editions with a foreword by A. W. Chadwick; Wikipedia, “Tripura Rahasya” and “Ashtavakra”.

Sources differ / unverified: Chapter numbers follow the Ramanasramam translation. Summaries and renderings are our own plain-English retellings, not quotations. The text is traditionally ascribed to Haritāyana; modern scholars date it to the medieval period. Only the Jñāna Khaṇḍa is covered in this series. The Aṣṭāvakra of this text is the sage of the Mahābhārata’s Janaka story; the Aṣṭāvakra Gītā is a separate work.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.
