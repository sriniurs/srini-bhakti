ZAPPY: Does Paraśurāma ever get it?

PROF. BOLT: He does, Zappy, and the Goddess herself has the last great word.

Part 4 covers the last six chapters. Having taught through stories, Dattātreya turns to the practical: why glimpses of the Self fade, how they become steady, and what holds us back.

At the summit comes the Vidyā Gītā, in which the Goddess herself teaches a gathering of sages. The book ends with grace, karma, and a summary.

We close with a page on Ramaṇa Maharṣi, whose love of the book brought it to readers all over the world.

GLIMPSES THAT FADE

Chapter XVII
ProblemMoments of clarity come and go
CauseOld habits of mind still pull
Three stepsHearing, reflecting, abiding
HearingThe teaching from a guru or text
ReflectingRemoving doubts by thinking it through
AbidingResting in what has been understood, until it is steady

ZAPPY: I keep getting it and losing it.

PROF. BOLT: Everyone does at first. Abiding is what makes it stay.

Paraśurāma, like Hemacūḍa, finds that moments of clarity come and go. Dattātreya explains that old habits of mind keep pulling attention outward.

The remedy is the classical three steps: śravaṇa, hearing the teaching; manana, reflecting on it until doubts are gone; and nididhyāsana, resting in what has been understood.

Abiding is what turns glimpses into a steady state.

THE WISHING JEWEL

Chapter XVII
StoryA man who finds a wish-fulfilling jewel, Cintāmaṇi
MistakeHe doubts it, and trades it for something showy
MeaningWe hold the Self and swap it for passing things
DoubtIs the thief
RemedyReflection removes doubt
LessonValue what you already have

ZAPPY: Who would throw away a wishing jewel?

PROF. BOLT: All of us, every day, says Dattātreya.

Dattātreya tells of a man who finds the wish-fulfilling jewel, Cintāmaṇi, but doubts its worth and trades it for something showy.

We do the same, he says: we already hold the Self, and we trade it for passing things because we doubt its value.

Doubt is the thief here, and reflection, manana, is the guard.

MĀYĀ

Chapter XVIII
QuestionIf awareness is one, why do we see many?
MagicianA conjuror shows things that are not there
Rope and snakeIn dim light a rope is taken for a snake
MāyāThe power by which the one seems many
Not separateMāyā is the Goddess’s own power
EndingSeeing the rope ends fear of the snake

ZAPPY: Is māyā bad?

PROF. BOLT: It’s her play. The trouble is only in being fooled by it.

If awareness is one, why do we see many things? Dattātreya answers with māyā, the power by which the one seems many.

He uses familiar images: the magician who shows things that are not there, and the rope taken for a snake in dim light. When the rope is seen, the fear ends by itself.

In Śrīvidyā, māyā is not an enemy; it is the Goddess’s own power, her play. The trouble lies only in being fooled.

OLD TENDENCIES

Chapter XIX
VāsanāsHabits of mind left by past experience
Three kindsFrom faults, from actions, from desires
ObstacleThey pull attention back outward
RemedySteady abiding wears them away
JaḍabharataThe sage who lived like a simpleton
CyavanaAnother example from the stories

ZAPPY: Why didn’t Jaḍabharata act wise?

PROF. BOLT: He didn’t need to. Freedom doesn’t always look impressive from outside.

Vāsanās are habits of mind left by past experience. The book describes three kinds: those from faults, those from past actions, and those from desires.

They are what pulls attention outward even after the Self has been glimpsed. Steady abiding wears them away.

Stories of Jaḍabharata, the sage who lived like a simpleton, and of the sage Cyavana are used as examples.

THREE KINDS OF KNOWER

Chapter XIX
HighestFree even in the busiest action
MiddleFree, steadier when at rest
LowestFree, but still liable to be pulled by habit
All threeHave truly recognised the Self
DifferenceIn how far old tendencies have worn away
HemacūḍaGrew from one kind to another

ZAPPY: Even the enlightened have grades?

PROF. BOLT: In steadiness, yes. The Self is the same; the settling takes time.

The book describes three kinds of jñānī, knower of the Self. The highest remains free in the busiest action; the middle is free but steadier at rest; the lowest is free but still liable to be pulled by habit.

All three have truly recognised the Self. The difference lies in how far old tendencies have worn away.

This is a realistic picture: recognition may be sudden, but settling takes time.

THE VIDYĀ GĪTĀ

Chapter XX
SceneSages gather in Brahmā’s hall, unable to agree
AppearanceThe Goddess appears as light
She speaksAs awareness itself, beyond all forms
Dual and non-dualWorship of her as other is a step toward knowing her as one’s Self
ObstaclesLack of faith, desire and dullness
TeachingA raft to cross, not a thing to cling to

ZAPPY: The Goddess herself teaches?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. In a book about her, she finally speaks in her own voice.

Chapter XX, the Vidyā Gītā, is the summit of the book. Sages gather in Brahmā’s hall and cannot agree about the highest truth. The Goddess appears as a great light and speaks.

She teaches that she is awareness itself, beyond all forms. Worship of her as other than oneself is a valid step that leads to knowing her as one’s own Self.

She names the obstacles, lack of faith, desire and dullness, and says that all teaching is a raft for crossing, not a thing to cling to.

GRACE

Chapter XXI
Chief causeGrace, more than effort
Two princesRukmāṅgada and Hemāṅgada
A ghoulVasumān seizes one and poses riddles
The wise brotherAnswers the riddles and frees him
LessonWisdom, given by grace, frees others too
EffortStill needed; grace shows itself as sincere longing

ZAPPY: If grace does it, why try?

PROF. BOLT: Because the longing to try is grace showing up. Don’t waste it.

Chapter XXI insists that grace is the chief cause of freedom. Effort matters, but the very longing that drives effort is grace showing itself.

A story illustrates this: two princes, Rukmāṅgada and Hemāṅgada. One is seized by a ghoul, Vasumān, who poses riddles; the other, who is wise, answers them and frees his brother.

Wisdom given by grace frees not only its owner but others too.

KARMA AND THE CLOSE

Chapter XXII
Stored karmaBurned by knowledge
New karmaDoes not bind the free
Begun karmaRuns out like an arrow already shot
SummaryDattātreya restates the whole teaching
HaritāyanaTells Nārada that reading the book brings wisdom
ParaśurāmaFreed, and later the teacher of this line

ZAPPY: Why can’t knowledge stop the arrow?

PROF. BOLT: It’s already flying. But it no longer matters to the one who knows.

Chapter XXII explains karma in three kinds. Stored karma is burned by knowledge; new actions no longer bind the free; and karma that has already begun to bear fruit runs its course, like an arrow already shot.

Dattātreya then sums up the whole teaching. Paraśurāma, freed, becomes a teacher of the line our Dattātreya and Paraśurāma guide describes.

In the outer frame, Haritāyana tells Nārada that whoever reads the book with care will become wise.

ZAPPY: Why would Ramaṇa love a Śrīvidyā book?

PROF. BOLT: Because its Goddess is the Self he taught. Names differ; the pointing is the same.

Ramaṇa Maharṣi of Tiruvaṇṇāmalai valued the Tripurā Rahasya highly and often quoted it. His disciple Munagala Venkataramaiah, later Swami Ramananda Saraswathi, translated the Jñāna Khaṇḍa into English, and Sri Ramanasramam published it in 1938.

Later editions carry a foreword by A. W. Chadwick, an English disciple of Ramaṇa. The translation brought the book to readers all over the world.

Ramaṇa’s teaching of self-inquiry is close to the book’s vicāra, which is why a Śrīvidyā text found a home in a modern Advaita ashram.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember from all four parts?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess you worship is the awareness you are. Hemalekhā knew; now you do too.

Across its twenty-two chapters, the Tripurā Rahasya makes one movement: from worship to inquiry, from inquiry to recognition, from recognition to freedom in daily life.

Its Goddess is not only a figure on a throne; she is the awareness in which everything appears, the one constant through waking, dream and sleep.

And it gives that teaching through stories, many of them with women as the wisest voices.

Anugraha, grace, is one of the five acts of the Goddess in Śrīvidyā thought, alongside creation, preservation, dissolution and concealment. The Lalitā Sahasranāma names her as the one who performs all five.
