Temple tour, five forms, legends and festivals: Kāmākṣī, Kāñcī (18 Śakti Pīṭhas guide)

This site already has a full guide to Kāmākṣī as one of the eighteen great Śakti Pīṭhas: the temple, its five forms, its legends and its year. This guide does not repeat that tour. It looks at Kāmākṣī the way a Śrīvidyā devotee does.

For Śrīvidyā, Kāñcī is the earthly home of Lalitā. Kāmākṣī holds Lalitā’s four weapons, sits on Lalitā’s five-Brahma throne, and receives worship first through the Śrīcakra set before her. Śrīvidyā teachers and composers have gathered round her for centuries.

We cover what that means: her names in the Sahasranāma, the meaning of Kāmakoṭi, the Gāyatrī maṇḍapa, the Śrīcakra and its full-moon worship, Durvāsa, the local Bhaṇḍa story, Śaṅkara, Mūka, Dīkṣitar and Śyāma Śāstri.

KĀMĀKṢĪ IS LALITĀ

Reading the image
Four weaponsNoose (attachment), goad (anger), sugarcane bow (mind), five flower arrows (senses), as in the Sahasranāma’s opening
SeatA throne of the five Brahmas, the same couch as Lalitā’s in Śrīpura
PosePadmāsana, the seated yogic posture, facing south-east
ParrotOn her hand or shoulder in many images
NameKā (Sarasvatī) and Mā (Lakṣmī) as her two eyes, a well-known devotional reading
DoctrineFor Kāñcī’s priests and the maṭha, Kāmākṣī is Śrīvidyā Parameśvarī herself

ZAPPY: Same weapons as Lalitā?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly the same. That’s how you know you’re looking at Tripurasundarī, whatever the local name.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma begins by describing the Goddess holding a noose of attachment, a goad of anger, a bow of sugarcane that is the mind, and five flower arrows that are the five subtle elements. Kāmākṣī holds exactly these.

She sits in padmāsana on a throne described as made of the five Brahmas, the same seat on which Lalitā sits with Kāmeśvara in Śrīpura. In a famous devotional reading of her name, kā is Sarasvatī and mā is Lakṣmī, and akṣī means eyes: the two goddesses are her eyes.

Kāñcī’s own tradition calls her Śrīvidyā Parameśvarī, the supreme Goddess of Śrīvidyā. For a Śrīvidyā devotee there is no doubt about whom one is seeing.

IN THE SAHASRANĀMA

Same line“Sudhāsāgara madhyasthā”: dwelling in the ocean of nectar
Śloka 118“Śrīvidyā … kāmakoṭikā”: the Kāmakoṭi Goddess, in the same line as Śrīvidyā itself
KāmakoṭiA name used for Kāñcī’s Goddess and for its maṭha
CautionThe Sahasranāma does not name Kāñcī; the link is made by tradition
Read moreOur Lalitā Sahasranāma guide, Parts 1 and 6

ZAPPY: Does the Sahasranāma mention Kāñcī?

PROF. BOLT: Not by place. But ‘Kāmākṣī’ and ‘Kāmakoṭikā’ are both there, and Kāñcī has claimed them for centuries.

Two names in the Lalitā Sahasranāma are tied to Kāñcī by tradition. In śloka 23 she is “Kāmākṣī kāmadāyinī”, the lovely-eyed one who grants desires. In śloka 118, in the same line as “Śrīvidyā” and “the sixteen-syllabled vidyā”, she is “Kāmakoṭikā”.

The Sahasranāma itself does not name Kāñcī. The connection is made by the temple and its commentators, and it has been accepted for a long time.

Our Lalitā Sahasranāma guide covers both ślokas in its verse-by-verse parts, with Bhāskararāya’s commentary on each.

KĀMAKOṬṬAM, KĀMAKOṬI

What the old names mean
KāmakoṭṭamTamil: “the shrine (koṭṭam) of Kāma(kṣī)”
KāmakoṣṭhaThe Sanskrit form; śilpa texts use it for a Goddess shrine in a temple
KāmakoṭiPopularly read as “the summit of desire” or “ten million desires”
Early useTamil hymns mention Kāñcī’s Kāmakoṭṭam
ScholarsSome think Kāñcī’s shrine was so famous that “kāmakoṭṭam” came to mean any Goddess shrine
MaṭhaThe Kāñcī Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham takes its name from it

ZAPPY: Koṭṭam, koṣṭha, koṭi: which is right?

PROF. BOLT: All three are used. Koṭṭam and koṣṭha mean the shrine; koṭi is the devotional reading. Each name carries a different idea.

In early Tamil sources the Goddess’s shrine at Kāñcī is called Kāmakoṭṭam, “the shrine of Kāma”. The Sanskrit equivalent, kāmakoṣṭha, is used in some śilpa and Āgama texts for a Goddess shrine within a temple complex.

Kāmakoṭi, the name of the maṭha and a title of the Goddess, is often explained devotionally as “the peak of desire”, the Goddess who fulfils all desires and is herself the final object of desire.

Scholars who study Kāñcī’s inscriptions have argued about the history of these words. One view is that Kāñcī’s Kāmakoṭṭam was so famous that the word came to mean any Goddess shrine in South Indian temples.

THE GĀYATRĪ MAṆḌAPA

A hall built like a mantra
HallThe sanctum hall, said in the Kāmākṣī Rahasya to be built by the gods
WallsFour, for the four Vedas
PillarsTwenty-four, for the 24 syllables of the Gāyatrī
CentreKāmākṣī, as the hidden heart of the Gāyatrī (rahasya gāyatrī)
Three formsHer form is taught as gross, subtle and causal
Before herThe Śrīcakra, receiving worship first

ZAPPY: The building is a mantra?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the idea: the outer Gāyatrī made of stone, and the Goddess as its secret core.

The sanctum is called the Gāyatrī maṇḍapa. Tradition says its four walls are the four Vedas and its twenty-four pillars the twenty-four syllables of the Gāyatrī mantra, so that the hall itself is a mantra in stone.

At its centre sits Kāmākṣī, described in temple tradition as the rahasya gāyatrī, the hidden heart of the Gāyatrī, which Śrīvidyā identifies with its own vidyā. Her form is taught at three levels: gross, subtle and causal.

The Gāyatrī itself is public and recited by millions. What Śrīvidyā calls its secret heart is received from a teacher, and this series does not give it.

THE ŚRĪCAKRA BEFORE HER

Worship begins here
FormA Śrīcakra carved in stone, set in a trough before the image
CoveredUsually hidden under fresh flowers
OrderWorship of the Goddess begins with the Śrīcakra
Full moonA Navāvaraṇa pūjā is offered to the main Kāmākṣī on full-moon days, the temple’s festival list says
Who set itTradition credits Durvāsa, and Śaṅkara (who set or reconsecrated it)
Our ruleWe describe the worship; we do not give its procedure

ZAPPY: Why worship the diagram first?

PROF. BOLT: Because in Śrīvidyā the Śrīcakra is the Goddess’s body. Worshipping it is worshipping her, enclosure by enclosure.

In front of the Goddess, in a trough-like setting, is a Śrīcakra carved in stone. It is usually covered with fresh flowers, so visitors may not see it clearly. In this temple, worship of the Goddess begins with the Śrīcakra.

The temple’s festival calendar, published by the Kāñcī maṭha, says that a Navāvaraṇa pūjā is offered to the main image on every full moon: worship of the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra, from the outer square to the central point.

Tradition gives two founders: Durvāsa, the sage who first worshipped here, and Śaṅkara, who set up or reconsecrated the present Śrīcakra.

DURVĀSA, THE FIRST TEACHER

The fiery sage of Śrīvidyā
WhoThe quick-tempered sage of the Purāṇas, counted as a form of Śiva
HereHis shrine faces the main sanctum
TeacherRegarded as a founding teacher of Śrīvidyā, in the Kādi line
HymnsThe Lalitā Stavaratna (Āryā Dviśatī), the Tripurā Mahimna Stotra and others are ascribed to him
LegendCursed to lose speech, he was partly freed by Kāmākṣī’s grace
AttendantsListed with Kāmadeva and Lopāmudrā among the Goddess’s great devotees

ZAPPY: Durvāsa, of the famous curses?

PROF. BOLT: The same. In Śrīvidyā he’s also a great teacher. Dīkṣitar calls Kāmalāmbā ‘worshipped by Durvāsa’, which marks his own Kādi line.

Durvāsa is best known in the Purāṇas for his temper and his curses. In Śrīvidyā he is also one of the great early teachers, counted in the Kādi line, alongside Agastya, Lopāmudrā and others.

Several important Śrīvidyā hymns are ascribed to him. The Lalitā Stavaratna, also called the Āryā Dviśatī, describes Śrīpura and the Goddess’s court in two hundred verses. The Tripurā Mahimna Stotra is another.

At Kāñcī his shrine faces the main sanctum. A local legend says a curse made him mute, and Kāmākṣī’s grace partly freed him, a story that echoes the later tale of Mūka.

AppearanceThe Goddess comes through the bila (cave opening) and kills him
The bilaA gateway near the Tapas Kāmākṣī shrine marks the spot
CompareThe Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa sets the war at Śrīpura, after Lalitā rises from the fire
Read moreOur Lalitopākhyāna guide, Parts 1–6

ZAPPY: Two places for one war?

PROF. BOLT: Kāñcī has its own version. Many temples tell the great stories as happening on their own ground. The Goddess is everywhere.

Kāñcī tells its own version of the war against Bhaṇḍa. The gods, tormented by the demon, do penance as parrots in campaka trees; the Goddess appears through the bila, the cave opening, and destroys him.

The bila is marked by a gateway near the shrine of Tapas Kāmākṣī, the Goddess in penance. The parrot, perched on Kāmākṣī’s hand or shoulder in many images, may recall the same story.

The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa’s Lalitopākhyāna, retold in Part B of this series, sets the war at Śrīpura after Lalitā rises from the sacrificial fire. Local temple traditions often bring the great stories home in this way.

ŚAṄKARA AND KĀMĀKṢĪ

The Kāñcī account
StoryThe Goddess was fierce; Śaṅkara set the Śrīcakra before her and she became gentle
VariantSome say the fierce form was Kālikāmbā at the Ādi Kāmākṣī shrine nearby
UtsavaThe festival image is said to come out from Śaṅkara’s shrine in the temple
SaundaryalaharīTradition links its composition with Kāñcī
MaṭhaThe Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham holds Śaṅkara spent his last years here
Our stanceTradition stated as tradition; see our Śaṅkara guide for the history debate

ZAPPY: Did Śaṅkara really calm her?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the Kāñcī tradition, and the temple honours it. The historical question is open; our Foundations guide on Śaṅkara sets out both sides.

The best-known Kāñcī story about Śaṅkara says that the Goddess was once fierce and frightening, and that Śaṅkara calmed her by installing the Śrīcakra before her. Another version says the fierce form was Kālikāmbā, worshipped at the Ādi Kāmākṣī shrine nearby.

Tradition also links the Saundaryalaharī with Kāñcī, and the temple’s festival image is said to be taken out from Śaṅkara’s shrine within the complex, in memory of his role.

The Kāñcī Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham holds that Śaṅkara spent his last years here. Other maṭhas and many historians disagree. Our Foundations guide, Śaṅkara and Śrīvidyā, sets out the debate.

THE MŪKA PAÑCAŚATĪ

Five hundred verses from a mute poet
PoetMūka, “the mute”, whose real name is not known
LegendKāmākṣī’s grace gave him speech, and he poured out 500 verses
Five hundredsĀryā, Pādāravinda, Stuti, Kaṭākṣa and Mandasmita śatakas
ŚrīvidyāThe Āryā śataka is full of Śrīvidyā images: Kāmeśvara, the bow and arrows, the Kāmakoṭi seat
Glance and smileTwo whole hundreds on her sidelong glance and her gentle smile
TraditionSome link Mūka with a head of the Kāñcī maṭha

ZAPPY: A hundred verses on a smile?

PROF. BOLT: A hundred, Zappy. For Mūka, Kāmākṣī’s smile was the whole of Śrīvidyā made visible.

The Mūka Pañcaśatī, “Mūka’s five hundred”, is one of the great Sanskrit hymns to Kāmākṣī. Its author is known only as Mūka, the mute; legend says Kāmākṣī’s glance gave him speech, and the verses poured out.

Its five hundreds are the Āryā śataka, in the āryā metre; the Pādāravinda śataka on her lotus feet; the Stuti śataka of praise; the Kaṭākṣa śataka on her glance; and the Mandasmita śataka on her smile.

The Āryā śataka in particular is full of Śrīvidyā imagery: Kāmeśvara, the sugarcane bow and flower arrows, the Kāmakoṭi seat, the Goddess as the Upaniṣads’ goal. Some traditions identify Mūka with an early head of the Kāñcī maṭha.

DĪKṢITAR AT KĀÑCĪ

NīrajākṣiIn Hindoḷam; her two eyes as Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī
Śrī Sarasvati hiteIn Mañji; Kāmākṣī dwelling in the Kāmakoṭi pīṭha
Ekāmreśa nāyikeIn Śuddha Sāveri; the consort of Ekāmreśvara

ZAPPY: Dīkṣitar wrote for Kāmākṣī too?

PROF. BOLT: Many songs, Zappy. The Navāvaraṇa kṛtis are for Kamalāmbā at Tiruvārūr, but Kāñcī has its own set.

Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar (1775–1835), a Śrīvidyā upāsaka, composed many songs at Kāñcī. Kañjadalāyatākṣi in the rāga Manohari is the best known. Nīrajākṣi in Hindoḷam describes her two eyes as Sarasvatī and Lakṣmī.

Śrī Sarasvati hite in Mañji places her in the Kāmakoṭi pīṭha. Ekāmreśa nāyike in Śuddha Sāveri addresses her as consort of Ekāmreśvara. Avyājakaruṇākaṭākṣi and Kanakāmbari are for the golden Kāmākṣī.

His best-known Śrīvidyā work, the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, belongs to Tiruvārūr, the fourth temple in this series. Dīkṣitar himself will have a guide in the Teachers section.

ŚYĀMA ŚĀSTRI AND THE GOLDEN GODDESS

The family that carried her

JourneyThrough several places, including Tiruvārūr, before Thanjavur

ZAPPY: So Kāmākṣī moved house?

PROF. BOLT: Her golden image did. The Kāñcī Goddess stayed. The Śakti Pīṭha guide tells the full journey.

The full story: Kāmākṣī, Kāñcī · The Golden Kāmākṣī

After the fall of Vijayanagara in 1565, the golden processional image of Kāmākṣī was carried away from Kāñcī for safety. A family of priests guarded it through several places, including a stay at Tiruvārūr, before it found a home at Thanjavur.

Śyāma Śāstri (1762–1827), eldest of the Carnatic Trinity, was born into that family at Tiruvārūr and became the priest of Bangāru Kāmākṣī at Thanjavur. Most of his songs are addressed to her.

At Kāñcī today, a pañcaloha image stands in the shrine of the golden Kāmākṣī. The Śakti Pīṭha guide tells this journey in full.

ONE GODDESS FOR A CITY

Kāñcī’s rule
Unique ruleKāmākṣī’s is the only Goddess shrine in old Kāñcī
Śiva templesNone has a separate shrine for the Goddess
EkāmbareśvaraShe is consort of Śiva under the mango tree
Facing herTradition says the city’s Śiva temples all face her
Mokṣa cityKāñcī is one of the seven cities of liberation
Śrīvidyā readingOne Śakti for every Śiva: the bindu at the centre of the city

ZAPPY: Why only one Goddess temple?

PROF. BOLT: Because she is the Śakti of every Śiva in Kāñcī. Like the bindu at the centre of the Śrīcakra, one point holds everything.

Kāñcī is full of temples, Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava. Yet its Goddess temple is unique: Kāmākṣī’s is the only shrine to the Goddess in the old city, and none of the city’s Śiva temples has a separate sanctum for her.

She is the consort of Ekāmbareśvara, Śiva under the single mango tree, whom she worshipped in penance. Tradition adds that the city’s Śiva temples face her shrine.

Śrīvidyā gives this a natural reading. Just as the Śrīcakra has one central point, the bindu, so Kāñcī has one Goddess, the Śakti of every Śiva in the city.

A PĪṬHA? A CAREFUL WORD

Tradition and scholarship
TraditionA Śakti Pīṭha, where Satī’s navel fell (Nābhi or Oḍyāṇa Pīṭha)
Lists differSome lists name the back, not the navel; some omit Kāñcī
ScholarsRecent research finds the Pīṭha label for Kāmākṣī in print mainly from the later 20th century
Older statusAs Kāmakoṭṭam, the shrine was famous long before
Śrīvidyā viewFor Śrīvidyā it is a pīṭha in another sense: the seat of the Śrīcakra
Our stanceWe report both and take no side

ZAPPY: Then is it a Śakti Pīṭha or not?

PROF. BOLT: For devotees, yes. For historians, the label is newer than the temple. Both can be true at once.

Popular tradition counts Kāmākṣī among the Śakti Pīṭhas, where a part of Satī’s body fell, usually the navel. Lists differ: some name another part, and some do not include Kāñcī at all.

A recent study of Kāñcī’s goddess temples from Heidelberg University notes that the identification of Kāmākṣī as a Pīṭha appears in publications mainly from the second half of the twentieth century, although the shrine’s fame as Kāmakoṭṭam is much older.

For Śrīvidyā the word pīṭha has another sense too: the seat of the Goddess in the Śrīcakra. In that sense Kāñcī is a pīṭha beyond question.

ZAPPY: When should a Śrīvidyā devotee visit?

PROF. BOLT: Any full moon, for the Navāvaraṇa pūjā. Navarātri if you love crowds.

See also: Lalitā Pañcamī and Lalitā Jayantī

On every full moon the main Kāmākṣī receives a Navāvaraṇa pūjā, according to the temple calendar published by the Kāñcī maṭha. For a Śrīvidyā devotee this is perhaps the best time to visit.

The golden chariot goes round the temple on Fridays and on new and full moons, except during Navarātri and the Brahmotsavam. The great Brahmotsavam is in the Tamil month of Māsi, February–March, with a chariot festival and a float festival on the tank.

Navarātri, Āḍi and Aippasi Pūram, Śaṅkara Jayantī and a spring festival in Vaikāsi complete the year.

WHAT KĀÑCĪ TEACHES

For the reader
Lalitā is hereNot only in Śrīpura on Meru, but in a city on earth
Gentle powerThe fierce made gentle: power held by love
GlanceKaṭākṣa: one look of grace can give speech to the mute

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: Kāmākṣī’s eyes. Her name is about them, Mūka sang of them, and devotees say her grace comes through them.

Kāñcī shows Lalitā not in a distant heaven but in a city on earth. The Goddess who sits in Śrīpura on Meru also sits in the Gāyatrī maṇḍapa, receiving flowers and kuṅkuma.

The temple’s stories are about power made gentle. The fierce form is calmed; the cursed sage is freed; the mute poet is given speech. In each case, grace comes through her glance.

Durvāsa, Śaṅkara, Mūka, Dīkṣitar and Śyāma Śāstri all belong to this one temple. Few places hold so much of Śrīvidyā’s history in so small a space.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: Lalitā Sahasranāma (ślokas 23, 118); Mūka Pañcaśatī; Wikipedia, “Kamakshi Amman Temple” (Durvāsa, Bhaṇḍa and the bila, Gāyatrī maṇḍapa, five forms, history); Kāñcī Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham pages on Kāñcī temples and the Kāmākṣī festival calendar (kamakoti.org); V. Sriram, “Some Muttuswami Dikshitar kritis on Kamakshi”; Heidelberg University Publishing studies of Kanchipuram’s goddess temples (Ute Hüsken and colleagues); Śakti Pīṭha guide on this site.

Sources differ / unverified: Who set the Śrīcakra (Durvāsa, Śaṅkara, or both) is traditional. Whether the fierce form Śaṅkara calmed was Kāmākṣī or Kālikāmbā is told both ways. The founding date (Pallava, Chola or later) is uncertain. The Śakti Pīṭha identification and the body part (navel or back) differ between lists. Ascriptions of hymns to Durvāsa and of the Mūka Pañcaśatī’s author are traditional. The Kāmakoṭi pīṭha’s account of Śaṅkara’s last years is disputed by other maṭhas.

What we leave out: This series explains names, stories, philosophy, the Śrīcakra’s geometry, deities, temples and music. It does not reproduce, assemble or decode any initiatory mantra (Bālā, Pañcadaśī, Ṣoḍaśī or others), nor any nyāsa, dīkṣā or pūjā procedure: those are received from a teacher.

Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
