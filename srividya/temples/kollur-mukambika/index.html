KOLLŪR MŪKĀMBIKĀ

The Goddess on the Śrīcakra, above the golden line

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

At Kollūr, in the forest below Kodachādri, Mūkāmbikā is worshipped in two forms: a self-born liṅga split by a golden line, and behind it an image in five metals, seated on a Śrīcakra, which Śaṅkara is said to have set up after a vision.

This guide tells the legend of the mute demon, explains the golden line and the image, follows Śaṅkara to Kodachādri, and looks at the temple’s deep bond with Kerala and with learning.

Śrī Mūkāmbikāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why would the Goddess of speech make someone mute?

PROF. BOLT: To stop a dangerous wish. Silence can be grace too.

ZAPPY: The Goddess is in a liṅga?

PROF. BOLT: In half of one, Zappy. The golden line is the meeting of Śiva and Śakti. And she sits above it on a Śrīcakra.

Mūkāmbikā of Kollūr is one of the best-loved Goddess temples of South India. It stands in a forest village below Kodachādri in the Western Ghats, beside the river Sauparṇikā.

Its sanctum holds two sacred forms. The first is a self-born liṅga divided by a golden line, one side the Goddess, the other Śiva. The second, behind it, is an image of Mūkāmbikā in five metals, seated on a Śrīcakra pedestal, which tradition says Śaṅkara set up after a vision of the Goddess.

For Śrīvidyā readers the temple has a clear meaning: a Goddess who unites the three great goddesses, enthroned on the Śrīcakra, above a liṅga that shows Śiva and Śakti as one.

ZAPPY: Why so far from any city?

PROF. BOLT: The old Goddess temples often are. Forest, hill and river were her first home.

Kollūr lies in Udupi district, inland from the Karnataka coast, surrounded by dense forest. The peak of Kodachādri, about 1,343 metres high, rises some twenty kilometres away.

Tradition names Kollūr among the seven sacred places of Tuḷunāḍu, the Tulu-speaking coast, said to have been created by Paraśurāma. The village takes its name from the sage Kola, who did penance here.

The temple was patronised by many rulers, including the Keḷadi Nāyakas, who are credited with renovating it.

New nameMūkāsura, the mute demon
BattleThe Goddess, combining the powers of the three goddesses, killed him

ZAPPY: She stole his voice?

PROF. BOLT: Before he could ask for something dangerous. Speech is power; the Goddess of speech took it back.

The temple legend tells of a demon, Kaumāsura, who did penance to win a boon of immortality. To stop him, the Goddess took away his speech, so that when the moment came he could not ask for it.

Renamed Mūkāsura, the mute demon, he turned to violence, and the Goddess, uniting the powers of the three great goddesses, killed him. She came to be called Mūkāmbikā.

At the request of the sage Kola, she agreed to stay at Kollūr and merged into the self-born liṅga, together with Śiva.

ZAPPY: Why is her side bigger?

PROF. BOLT: Because this is her temple. The liṅga says it plainly: Śakti contains Śiva, here.

The liṅga in the sanctum is svayambhū, self-born, not carved. A golden line, the svarṇa rekhā, divides it into two unequal parts.

According to the Śṛṅgeri maṭha’s account of its Kollūr branch, the smaller right side stands for Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva, and the larger left side for the Goddess, holding all her powers as Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī, the powers of action, will and knowledge.

The line is best seen at the early-morning abhiṣeka, when the liṅga is bathed and lit by reflected light. The abhiṣeka is offered to the liṅga; the image of the Goddess behind it receives decoration instead.

ZAPPY: Conch and discus? Those are Viṣṇu’s.

PROF. BOLT: They are, and they show she includes Lakṣmī. On the Śrīcakra beneath her, she is Lalitā. One image holds all three goddesses.

Behind the liṅga stands the image of Mūkāmbikā, made of pañcaloha, an alloy of five metals. She sits in padmāsana, with three eyes and four arms, holding a conch and a discus above and showing the gestures of blessing and boon below.

The image is set on a Śrīcakra pedestal. Tradition says Śaṅkara had it made according to the form he saw in his vision of the Goddess. A Śrīcakra yantra on a raised platform to the right of the sanctum is also credited to him.

The conch and discus show her as Lakṣmī; the Śrīcakra beneath her shows her as Lalitā. The temple’s theology is that she is all three goddesses in one.

ZAPPY: Can you climb Kodachādri?

PROF. BOLT: Many pilgrims trek up. The Sarvajña Pīṭha sits near the top, with views to the sea on a clear day.

Tradition says Śaṅkara meditated on Kodachādri, where a small stone shrine near the summit, the Sarvajña Pīṭha, marks his seat. There the Goddess appeared to him.

He is said to have had her image made as he saw her, and to have prescribed the temple’s rites, which the temple still follows. A seat called the Śaṅkara Siṃhāsana in the inner enclosure marks his place.

The Śṛṅgeri maṭha has a branch at Kollūr and treats the temple as closely tied to its own tradition.

THE GODDESS WHO STOPPED

A story told twice
WishŚaṅkara wanted to bring the Goddess to Kerala
ConditionShe would follow if he did not look back
The testAt Kollūr her anklets went silent; he turned to look
SoShe stayed at Kollūr
CompareŚṛṅgeri tells almost the same story of Śāradā
KeralaSome say she agreed to visit Kerala in the mornings, at Chottanikkara

ZAPPY: Didn’t we hear this at Śṛṅgeri?

PROF. BOLT: Almost word for word! Two temples, two Goddesses, one story. It tells you how close Kollūr and Śṛṅgeri feel to each other.

A popular story tells that Śaṅkara wished to take the Goddess to Kerala, and she agreed to follow him on condition that he not look back. At Kollūr her anklets fell silent, and Śaṅkara turned to see whether she was still there. So she stayed.

Readers of our Śṛṅgeri guide will recognise the story: it is told there of Śāradā, who stopped at Śṛṅgeri when Śaṅkara looked back. The two temples share the motif.

Some versions add that the Goddess agreed to be present in Kerala in the mornings, at the Chottanikkara temple near Kochi, before returning to Kollūr.

InscriptionA Kollūr record of 1481 calls her Ādi Śakti in liṅga form

ZAPPY: Three goddesses in one day?

PROF. BOLT: One Goddess, three moods. Śrīvidyā would say: the three powers, jñāna, icchā and kriyā, in Lalitā.

The temple tradition holds that Mūkāmbikā is worshipped as Sarasvatī in the morning, Lakṣmī at noon and Pārvatī or Kālī in the evening. Images of Kālī and Sarasvatī stand on either side of her.

A Kollūr inscription of 1481, as reported, describes her as Ādi Śakti in the form of the liṅga, the power of creation, preservation and dissolution.

In Śrīvidyā terms, the three goddesses are the three powers of Lalitā: icchā (will), jñāna (knowledge) and kriyā (action). Mūkāmbikā on the Śrīcakra holds all three.

ZAPPY: Why do Malayalis love Kollūr so much?

PROF. BOLT: Partly the legend: Śaṅkara, a Kerala son, wanted to take her home. Partly she’s the Goddess of learning, and Kerala loves learning.

A large share of Kollūr’s pilgrims come from Kerala, and the temple uses Malayalam as well as Kannada. The legend of Śaṅkara, born in Kerala, trying to take the Goddess home helps explain the bond.

Kollūr is a famous place for Vidyārambham, the ceremony of a child’s first letters, especially on Vijayadaśamī. Musicians also come to sing before the Goddess during Navarātri.

Kerala has its own temple named after her, the Dakṣiṇa Mūkāmbikā (Southern Mūkāmbikā) at North Paravur, also known for Vidyārambham.

ZAPPY: All these caves?

PROF. BOLT: Each with a story. The hill is where the temple’s legends happened; the village below is where the Goddess settled.

Kodachādri, the hill above Kollūr, holds many of the temple’s legends. A Gaṇapati cave is said to be where Mūkāsura did his penance. Small temples to Bhairava and Pārvatī stand on the slopes.

Citramūla is a cave where the sage Kola and Śaṅkara are said to have meditated. Higher up is the Sarvajña Pīṭha, Śaṅkara’s seat.

The hill is part of a protected forest today and a popular trek. Pilgrims who climb it see the landscape of the legends from above.

ZAPPY: When should I go?

PROF. BOLT: Dawn on any day, to see the liṅga’s golden line at the abhiṣeka. Navarātri if you’re bringing a child to start letters.

Navarātri is the great festival at Kollūr, with suvāsinī pūjā and other rites, ending with Vidyārambham on Vijayadaśamī.

The annual chariot festival falls in Phālguna, around March. Every day the temple performs its three worship services, beginning with the dawn abhiṣeka of the liṅga.

A tall pillar of lamps stands before the temple and is lit on festival nights. Fridays draw large crowds.

WHAT KOLLŪR TEACHES

For the reader

ŚrīcakraBeneath her image: Lalitā holding all

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: The golden line. The meeting of Śiva and Śakti is not an idea here; you can see it, at dawn.

Kollūr’s story begins with speech. The Goddess of learning takes away the voice that would ask for something harmful. Speech is a power, and it can be withheld as well as given.

Its liṅga shows Śiva and Śakti as one form, divided by a golden line, unequal and inseparable. Few temples make the union so visible.

Its image, seated on the Śrīcakra and holding the emblems of all three goddesses, gives Śrīvidyā’s answer: one Goddess, Lalitā, holding knowledge, wealth and power together.

Vidyārambham means “the beginning of learning”. A child, usually two to five years old, traces the first letters in rice or on the tongue with gold, guided by an elder or a priest, at a temple of the Goddess.
