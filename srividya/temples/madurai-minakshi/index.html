MADURAI MĪNĀKṢĪ

The fish-eyed queen who rules her city

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

Mīnākṣī of Madurai was born from a fire, raised as a prince, crowned queen of the Pāṇḍyas, conquered the worlds and married Śiva. Her temple puts the Goddess first.

This guide tells her story and reads it through Śrīvidyā: the kadamba forest, the fish-like eyes and the Mantriṇī of the Lalitā Sahasranāma, the hymns praising her as Śrīvidyā, and the composers who sang to her.

ZAPPY: A goddess who was a queen?

PROF. BOLT: Zappy, in Madurai she still is. For part of each year, the city counts as hers.

Mīnākṣī of Madurai is one of the best-loved goddesses of South India, and her temple one of the largest and most famous in the country. Unusually for a temple with a great Śiva shrine, the Goddess comes first: her name leads, and pilgrims visit her before her husband.

Her story is that of a girl born from a sacrificial fire who grew up as a prince, ruled the Pāṇḍya kingdom, conquered the world and married Śiva. For Śrīvidyā readers, the fire birth, the kadamba forest and her role as a ruling queen all echo Lalitā.

She is also widely praised as Śrīvidyā itself and as Rāja Mātaṅgī, the Goddess’s minister. This guide brings those threads together.

ZAPPY: Why does a kadamba forest matter for Śrīvidyā?

PROF. BOLT: Because the Lalitā Sahasranāma calls her ‘kadamba-vana-vāsinī’, dwelling in the kadamba forest. Madurai says: that forest is here.

The temple and the city grew together. The local legend says Indra, burdened with the sin of a killing, found relief in a kadamba forest when he came upon a self-born liṅga by a lake. He worshipped it with golden lotuses from the lake and built a shrine over it, still called the Indra vimāna.

Later a merchant, Dhanañjaya, spent a night in the forest shrine and saw the gods at worship. He told King Kulaśekhara Pāṇḍya, who cleared the forest and built a city with the temple at its centre.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma calls the Goddess “kadamba-vana-vāsinī”, she who dwells in the kadamba forest, in the same line as “Kāmākṣī”. Madurai’s devotees have long read that name as pointing to their city.

THE TEMPLE

Two shrines, fourteen towers
ShrinesMīnākṣī to the south, Sundareśvara to the north, with gilded vimānas
TowersFourteen gopurams; the southern one about 50 m tall
Earliest partsBuilt under Jaṭāvarman Kulaśekhara I (1190–1205)
RebuildingAfter 14th-century raids, rebuilt and greatly expanded by the Nāyakas
NāyakasViśvanātha Nāyaka began it; Tirumalai Nāyaka (1623–55) expanded it
EmblemA western gopuram is the model for the Tamil Nadu state emblem

ZAPPY: Fourteen gateways?

PROF. BOLT: And thousands of painted figures on them. It’s one of the largest temple complexes in India.

The present temple stands on the southern bank of the Vaigai. Its oldest surviving parts date from the reign of Jaṭāvarman Kulaśekhara I at the end of the 12th century. After raids in the early 14th century, it was rebuilt in stages.

Its great expansion came under the Nāyaka rulers of Madurai in the 16th and 17th centuries, above all Tirumalai Nāyaka. Fourteen gopurams rise around it, the tallest about fifty metres, covered with brightly painted figures. The Thousand-Pillar Hall and the Golden Lotus Tank are among its other sights.

Inside, the shrines of Mīnākṣī and Sundareśvara stand side by side, each under a gilded vimāna. The Goddess’s shrine is on the south.

BORN FROM THE FIRE

The story of Taḍātakai
ParentsKing Malayadhvaja Pāṇḍya and Queen Kāñcanamālā, praying for a son
The fireA three-year-old girl rose from their sacrificial fire
SignShe had three breasts; a voice said the third would vanish when she met her husband
RaisedAs a prince: trained in arms, crowned heir
NameTaḍātakai, the warrior princess
SourceThe Tiruviḷaiyāṭal Purāṇam, the “sacred sports” of Śiva at Madurai

ZAPPY: Born from a fire? Like Lalitā?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly like Lalitā, Zappy. In the Lalitopākhyāna the Goddess rises from the gods’ fire-altar. Madurai tells the same kind of birth.

The Tiruviḷaiyāṭal Purāṇam, the Tamil purāṇa of Śiva’s sacred sports at Madurai, tells how King Malayadhvaja Pāṇḍya and Queen Kāñcanamālā performed a great sacrifice for a son. Instead a girl of three rose from the fire, with three breasts.

A heavenly voice told them to raise her as a son, and said that her third breast would disappear when she met the man she was to marry. She was trained in arms and statecraft and crowned heir to the Pāṇḍya throne. Her name as a princess was Taḍātakai.

The parallel with Lalitā is striking. In the Lalitopākhyāna, Lalitā rises from the gods’ sacrificial fire to defeat Bhaṇḍa, and is crowned queen of the universe. Both are fire-born, both are sovereigns, both marry Śiva.

THE CONQUEST OF THE WORLD

Digvijaya
QueenShe inherits the throne and rules Madurai
CampaignShe marches out and conquers the kings of every direction
HeavenShe defeats the gods, even Indra
KailāsaShe attacks Śiva’s own army and defeats his hosts
The meetingShe sees Śiva; her third breast vanishes; she knows her husband
FestivalHer conquest is acted out in Madurai’s streets at Citrai

ZAPPY: She beat Śiva’s army?

PROF. BOLT: His army, yes. Then she saw him, and the battle was over. The warrior queen and the bride are one person.

When Malayadhvaja died, Taḍātakai became queen. She set out on a digvijaya, a conquest of all directions, defeating kings on earth and then the gods in heaven.

Finally she marched on Kailāsa and defeated the hosts of Śiva himself. Then Śiva appeared before her. At the sight of him her third breast vanished, and she knew him as her husband.

The episode is acted out every year at the Citrai festival, when the Goddess’s image is taken in procession through the streets as a conquering queen, before her coronation and wedding.

THE WEDDING

Mīnākṣī Tirukkalyāṇam
GroomŚiva comes to Madurai as Sundareśvara, “the beautiful lord”
BrotherViṣṇu gives his sister away, as Aḻagar
DelayIn the festival, Aḻagar arrives late and enters the Vaigai river
RuleAfter the wedding they rule Madurai together, then take divine form
JoinedTirumalai Nāyaka joined the wedding and the Aḻagar festivals in the month of Citrai
TodayCitrai draws vast crowds every April–May

ZAPPY: Why is Viṣṇu late to his own sister’s wedding?

PROF. BOLT: In the story he was delayed on the way, and arrived to find it done. In history, it’s the trace of two festivals joined into one.

Śiva came to Madurai as Sundareśvara, the beautiful lord, and married Mīnākṣī. Her brother Viṣṇu gave the bride away. After the wedding the couple ruled Madurai together, and in time took their divine forms as the deities of the temple.

In the Citrai festival, Viṣṇu as Kaḷḷaḻagar travels from his hill temple to Madurai for the wedding, but arrives too late and enters the Vaigai river instead. Hundreds of thousands of people watch his entry into the river.

Historians note that the wedding and Aḻagar’s festival were once held in different months. Tirumalai Nāyaka joined them in Citrai, which made the combined festival one of the largest in India.

THE FISH-EYED GODDESS

What her name means
Mīna + akṣīFish-eyed; Tamil Aṅgayaṟkaṇṇi, “with lovely fish eyes”
ShapeLong, bright eyes shaped like a fish
Never shutFish do not close their eyes; she never stops watching
Feeding by sightAn old belief: fish nourish their young by looking at them
SahasranāmaŚloka 6: “calan-mīnābha-locanā”, eyes like darting fish in the stream of her face’s beauty
CompareKāmākṣī: grace through the glance again

ZAPPY: A goddess named after her eyes, again?

PROF. BOLT: Kāñcī, Madurai and Kāśī: Kāmākṣī, Mīnākṣī and Viśālākṣī. All three are named for their eyes.

Mīnākṣī means fish-eyed, and her Tamil name Aṅgayaṟkaṇṇi means the same. Devotees give several readings: her eyes are long and bright like fish; fish never close their eyes, so she never stops watching over her devotees; and in an old belief, fish nourish their young simply by looking at them.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma, in its sixth śloka, describes the Goddess as having eyes like fish darting in the stream of her face’s beauty. Wikipedia’s article on Mīnākṣī notes this as the Sahasranāma’s nearest reference to her.

Kāmākṣī’s grace too was said to come through her glance. In both temples, the look of the Goddess is itself the blessing.

ZAPPY: Mīnākṣī is the Goddess’s minister?

PROF. BOLT: In Śrīvidyā terms, yes. Lalitā gives her kingdom’s rule to the Mantriṇī, and in Madurai the Goddess rules the city herself.

See also: Rājaśyāmalā, the Mantriṇī

In Śrīvidyā, the Goddess Lalitā has a chief minister, the Mantriṇī, also called Rājaśyāmalā or Rāja Mātaṅgī: green or dark in colour, holding a vīṇā and a parrot. Our deity guide to Rājaśyāmalā covers her in full.

Mīnākṣī is green-skinned in her images and holds a parrot on a lotus, and she is widely identified with Rāja Mātaṅgī; “Mantriṇī” is among her listed names. Dīkṣitar’s song “Mīnākṣi me mudam dehi” addresses her as rāja mātaṅgi.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma has a name that fits Madurai well: “mantriṇī-nyasta-rājya-dhūḥ”, she who has handed the burden of rule to the Mantriṇī. In Madurai, the Goddess as Mantriṇī rules the city.

THE QUEEN WHO RULES

NightEach night Sundareśvara is carried to her chamber
MorningHe is carried back at dawn
MeaningHere the Goddess is sovereign; the god comes to her

ZAPPY: Śiva visits her, not the other way round?

PROF. BOLT: Every night, in a palanquin. It’s one of the most charming customs of any temple.

At the Citrai festival Mīnākṣī is crowned, receives the sceptre and rules the city before her conquest and wedding. Local tradition speaks of a part of the year when Madurai is under her rule and the rest under Śiva’s, though tellings differ.

The custom of visiting the Goddess before Śiva makes the point every day. So does the night procession: each evening Sundareśvara’s image is carried in a palanquin to Mīnākṣī’s bedchamber, and carried back at dawn.

Few temples put the Goddess so clearly in charge. For Śrīvidyā, which sees Lalitā as the sovereign of the universe, Madurai is a living picture of that idea.

ŚAṄKARA AND NĪLAKAṆṬHA

ŚrīvidyāThey praise her as Śrīvidyā, dwelling in the bindu of the Śrīcakra
Nīlakaṇṭha Dīkṣita17th century, minister of Tirumalai Nāyaka
His hymnThe Ānandasāgara Stava, a hundred-odd verses to Mīnākṣī
TamilKumaraguruparar’s Mīnākṣī Piḷḷaittamiḻ, poems to the Goddess as a child
Our ruleWe give meanings, not the hymns’ bīja syllables

ZAPPY: Śaṅkara again?

PROF. BOLT: By tradition, yes. And a minister of the king wrote one of the finest hymns to her. Madurai drew poets from every corner.

The Mīnākṣī Pañcaratnam, five verses ascribed by tradition to Śaṅkara, praises Mīnākṣī as Śrīvidyā itself, seated at the left side of Śiva and dwelling in the bindu of the Śrīcakra.

Nīlakaṇṭha Dīkṣita, a seventeenth-century poet and minister of Tirumalai Nāyaka, wrote the Ānandasāgara Stava to Mīnākṣī, one of the finest Sanskrit hymns of its period.

In Tamil, Kumaraguruparar’s Mīnākṣī Piḷḷaittamiḻ celebrates the Goddess as a child, in the piḷḷaittamiḻ genre of poems to a divine infant. It is still recited in Madurai.

ZAPPY: He died singing to Mīnākṣī?

PROF. BOLT: So his family’s history says. A Śrīvidyā upāsaka, ending his life with her name.

Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar visited Madurai and composed for Mīnākṣī. His song “Mīnākṣi me mudam dehi” in the rāga Gamakakriyā is famous for another reason: according to the account of his family, he asked his disciples to sing it on Deepavali, 21 October 1835, and passed away as they sang.

His father, Rāmasvāmi Dīkṣitar, wrote a long rāgamālikā on Mīnākṣī, moving through dozens of rāgas.

Śyāma Śāstri, the third of the Trinity, composed the Navaratnamālikā, a garland of nine songs to Mīnākṣī.

ZAPPY: Which is the time to go?

PROF. BOLT: Citrai, if you love crowds and colour. A quiet evening for the night procession, if you don’t.

The great festival is Citrai, in April–May, with the Goddess’s coronation, her conquest procession, her wedding, the chariot festival and Kaḷḷaḻagar’s entry into the Vaigai.

In Āvaṇi (August–September) the temple re-enacts episodes from Śiva’s sacred sports. In Navarātri the Goddess is dressed in a different form each day. In Tai (January–February) the deities are taken on a float on the large Teppakkuḷam tank.

Every night of the year, the palanquin procession of Sundareśvara to Mīnākṣī’s chamber can be watched by visitors, a quiet alternative to the great festivals.

THREE GODDESSES, THREE EYES

Kāmākṣī, Mīnākṣī, Viśālākṣī

MaduraiMīnākṣī, fish eyes
KāśīViśālākṣī, wide eyes
PairedOften named together as the three great Goddesses of their cities

LessonThe Goddess’s grace is a look

ZAPPY: Why always the eyes?

PROF. BOLT: Because darśana is seeing and being seen. In these temples, it’s her look that blesses you.

Devotees often name three great city-goddesses together: Kāmākṣī of Kāñcī, Mīnākṣī of Madurai and Viśālākṣī of Kāśī. All three names end in akṣī, eyes: eyes of love, fish eyes and wide eyes.

For Śrīvidyā, each is a form of Lalitā. Kāñcī has her Śrīcakra; Madurai has her Mantriṇī; Kāśī has her as the Goddess of the city of liberation.

The common thread is darśana, seeing and being seen. In each temple, devotees come to meet her eyes.

WHAT MADURAI TEACHES

ZAPPY: What would you remember from Madurai?

PROF. BOLT: The third breast vanishing when she saw Śiva. Pride falls away when you meet what you were made for.

Madurai shows the Goddess as sovereign. She is born from fire, raised as a prince, crowned queen, conqueror of the worlds. Śiva comes to her, not the other way round.

The story of the third breast is the heart of the legend. Her strength is real, but at the sight of the one she was made for, the sign of her separateness disappears. The warrior and the bride are one.

For a Śrīvidyā devotee, the temple adds Mātaṅgī, the minister of wisdom, to the picture: power that rules through wisdom.
