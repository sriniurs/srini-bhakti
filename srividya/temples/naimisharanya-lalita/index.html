NAIMIṢĀRAṆYA LALITĀ DEVĪ

The Goddess of the forest where the Purāṇas were told

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

In the forest of Naimiṣa, on the Gomatī, the sages heard the Purāṇas from Sūta. Its presiding Goddess is Lalitā Devī, whose temple stands beside the sacred pool of the Cakra Tīrtha.

This guide tells the legends of the wheel and the pool, looks at the temple as a Śakti Pīṭha of the heart, and traces how the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, which holds the Lalitopākhyāna, is itself framed as a telling in this forest.

ZAPPY: A Lalitā temple in the north?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, Zappy, in the very forest where the Purāṇas were first told. The Purāṇa that holds the Lalitopākhyāna begins there too.

Naimiṣāraṇya, on the Gomatī river in Uttar Pradesh, is one of the most often named places in Sanskrit literature: the forest where the sages gathered and heard the Purāṇas from Sūta. Today it is a small pilgrim town full of shrines.

Its presiding Goddess is Lalitā Devī. Her temple, a short walk from the sacred pool called the Cakra Tīrtha, is counted among the Śakti Pīṭhas, and tradition says it marks where Satī’s heart fell.

This guide, the last of our seven temple guides, looks at the forest, the legend of the pool, the Goddess, and the link between Naimiṣa and the Purāṇa that tells Lalitā’s own story.

ZAPPY: Why does a forest matter to Lalitā?

PROF. BOLT: Because the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, which contains the Lalitopākhyāna, is framed as Sūta speaking to the sages of Naimiṣa.

The Naimiṣa forest is where, according to many Purāṇas, a great assembly of sages led by Śaunaka performed a long sacrifice. In its pauses they asked Sūta, Vyāsa’s pupil, to tell them the old stories. The Mahābhārata too is recited to Śaunaka here.

Tradition names two Sūtas: Lomaharṣaṇa, who first sat on the teller’s seat, and his son Ugraśravas. A shrine called the Sūta Gaddī marks the seat, and the Vyāsa Gaddī marks Vyāsa’s.

The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, which contains the Lalitopākhyāna and the Lalitā Sahasranāma, is likewise framed as a telling to the sages of Naimiṣa.

BRAHMĀ’S WHEEL

How Naimiṣa got its name
RequestThe sages asked Brahmā for a pure place to perform their rites
The wheelHe released a wheel and told them to settle where it stopped
NemiIts rim (nemi) wore away here, or it fell here
NameNaimiṣa: “of the rim”; or “in a moment” (nimiṣa)
PoolWhere it sank, water gushed up: the Cakra Tīrtha
Other storyViṣṇu destroyed the demon Durjaya here in a moment, by his discus

ZAPPY: Two meanings for one name?

PROF. BOLT: Nemi, the rim, or nimiṣa, the blink of an eye. Both stories are told, and both are lovely.

A popular legend says the sages asked Brahmā where they could perform their rites in peace. He released a wheel and told them to settle where it stopped. At Naimiṣa its rim, the nemi, wore away or fell, and the forest took its name from it.

Where the wheel sank into the earth, water gushed up and formed a pool, the Cakra Tīrtha, which pilgrims still bathe in. A temple of Cakra Nārāyaṇa stands beside it.

Another explanation links the name to nimiṣa, the blink of an eye: Viṣṇu is said to have destroyed the demon Durjaya and his army here in a moment with his discus.

LALITĀ STILLS THE WATERS

Her role in the legend
FloodThe water from the wheel’s pit would not stop rising
PrayerThe sages turned to Brahmā; Brahmā prayed to Lalitā
GraceLalitā stopped the flow, and the pool settled
PresidingShe stayed as the Goddess of the forest
GentleHere her name is read in its simplest sense: graceful, gentle
StatusA local legend, told by pilgrims and guides

ZAPPY: She stopped a flood?

PROF. BOLT: At Brahmā’s request. The Goddess who holds the worlds can hold back water, too.

In the local telling, the water rising from the wheel’s pit would not stop, and threatened to flood the forest. The sages turned to Brahmā, and Brahmā prayed to Lalitā, who stilled the waters. The pool settled into its present form.

Lalitā then stayed in the forest as its presiding Goddess. Here her name is often explained in its simplest sense: lalitā, the graceful, the gentle.

This is a local legend, told by pilgrims and guides rather than in the major Purāṇas, and we present it as such.

Heart of the Goddess
TraditionCounted among the 51 Śakti Pīṭhas by the district and by pilgrims
Body partSatī’s heart, by popular tradition
Devī Bhāgavata“Vārāṇasyāṃ Viśālākṣī, Naimiṣe Liṅgadhāriṇī, Prayāge Lalitā Devī…”
Liṅgadhāriṇī“She who holds the liṅga”: the name the list gives at Naimiṣa

ZAPPY: If the list says Lalitā is at Prayāga, why is she here?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess is Liṅgadhāriṇī here in the old list, and Lalitā is her name today. Both Naimiṣa and Prayāga honour Lalitā; lists and local names move over time.

The Sitapur district website describes the Lalitā Devī temple as one of the fifty-one Śakti Pīṭhas, and popular tradition says it marks where Satī’s heart fell.

The Devī Bhāgavata’s list of 108 seats of the Goddess names the Goddess at Naimiṣa as Liṅgadhāriṇī, “she who holds the liṅga”, in the same verse that places Viśālākṣī at Vārāṇasī and Lalitā Devī at Prayāga.

Lists of Śakti Pīṭhas differ, and several places claim the heart. We report the tradition of each place as its own.

THE TEMPLE

ZAPPY: Is it a big temple?

PROF. BOLT: Not by South Indian standards. It’s a busy, much-loved shrine in a town full of small holy places.

The temple stands within walking distance of the Cakra Tīrtha. Its entrance is flanked by elephant statues, and its inner sanctum holds an old image of the Goddess, richly dressed and decorated.

The temple is open every day from morning to evening, and is especially crowded during the spring and autumn Navarātris and on full moons.

Naimiṣāraṇya has many other shrines close by, including the Bhūteśvara Mahādeva temple, Cakra Nārāyaṇa, Hanumān Gaṛhī with its large image of Hanumān, and the Dadhīci Kuṇḍ at nearby Miśrikh.

LALITĀ AS THE HEART

A Śrīvidyā reading
HeartHṛdaya: in Śrīvidyā, the seat where the Goddess is met within
SahasranāmaShe is “dahara-ākāśa-rūpiṇī”, the space in the heart (śloka 122)
ŚrīcakraIts centre, the bindu, as the heart of the cosmos
PīṭhaThe heart falling here makes Naimiṣa a natural Lalitā seat
PurāṇasWords heard in the heart, from Sūta’s mouth
LabelA devotional reading, not a temple claim

ZAPPY: Why does the heart matter so much?

PROF. BOLT: In Śrīvidyā, outer worship leads inward. The heart is where the Goddess is finally found. A heart-pīṭha says that out loud.

In Śrīvidyā, outer worship leads inward, and the heart is where the Goddess is finally met. The Lalitā Sahasranāma calls her “dahara-ākāśa-rūpiṇī”, the one whose form is the small space within the heart.

The bindu at the centre of the Śrīcakra is often read as the heart of the cosmos. A pīṭha where Satī’s heart is said to have fallen fits that idea naturally.

This is our devotional reading, not a claim made by the temple. We offer it as a way for Śrīvidyā readers to see the place.

FROM NAIMIṢA TO ŚRĪPURA

How the story reached us

Inner storyHayagrīva tells Agastya the Lalitopākhyāna
SahasranāmaHayagrīva also gives Agastya the Thousand Names
ChainLalitā → Hayagrīva → Agastya → … → Sūta → Naimiṣa’s sages
Our guidesLalitopākhyāna 1–6, Hayagrīva, and Agastya and Lopāmudrā
SoThe Lalitā story is told, in the Purāṇa’s outer frame, in this forest

ZAPPY: Did Lalitā’s story happen here?

PROF. BOLT: No, it happened in Śrīpura. But the Purāṇa that tells it is set as a telling here, among the sages of Naimiṣa.

The Lalitopākhyāna is told by Hayagrīva to the sage Agastya, as our Lalitopākhyāna guides explain. It forms part of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, which is itself told by Sūta to the sages of Naimiṣa.

So the chain of tellings runs from Hayagrīva to Agastya, and on through the tradition to Sūta in the Naimiṣa forest. The story of Lalitā’s battle in Śrīpura reaches us, in the Purāṇa’s outer frame, through this place.

Our guides to Hayagrīva and to Agastya and Lopāmudrā cover the inner tellers. This temple stands where, in the Purāṇa’s outer frame, the telling took place.

ZAPPY: What order should I follow?

PROF. BOLT: Pilgrims usually bathe at the Cakra Tīrtha first, then visit Lalitā Devī. Ask locally for the parikramā season.

Pilgrims usually begin by bathing in the Cakra Tīrtha and then visit Lalitā Devī. The Sūta and Vyāsa Gaddīs, Hanumān Gaṛhī and the other shrines follow.

At Miśrikh nearby is the Dadhīci Kuṇḍ, linked with the sage Dadhīci, who gave his bones to make Indra’s thunderbolt.

Naimiṣāraṇya also has a longer pilgrim circuit, a parikramā of its many sacred sites, walked in its season. Local priests can advise on dates.

ZAPPY: When should a Śrīvidyā devotee go?

PROF. BOLT: Lalitā Jayantī, on the Māgha full moon. It’s her day, at her northern seat.

See: Lalitā Pañcamī and Lalitā Jayantī

The spring and autumn Navarātris are the busiest times at the temple. Full moons bring pilgrims each month, and Somavatī Amāvasyā, a new moon falling on a Monday, is a great bathing day at the Cakra Tīrtha.

Lalitā Jayantī, the full moon of Māgha, is the Goddess’s own day. Our festivals guide covers it in detail.

Worship continues every day from morning to evening, so pilgrims can visit at any season.

ZAPPY: So Śrīvidyā isn’t only South Indian?

PROF. BOLT: Its living temples are mostly in the south today, but Lalitā is worshipped all over India.

Most of Śrīvidyā’s great living temples are in South India, as the first six guides in this section show. But Lalitā’s name is kept in the north too, at Naimiṣa and at Prayāga, and Lalitā Jayantī is kept more widely in North India.

Tripurasundarī is also worshipped at Udaipur in Tripura, a famous seat of the Goddess in the north-east.

For the devotee, north and south are one: the same Lalitā, under different names and in different temples.

WHAT NAIMIṢA TEACHES

For the reader

HeartWhere the Goddess is met, inside
LineageStories pass from mouth to ear, teacher to student
SimplicityA small temple, a pool, a forest
VisitSit by the Cakra Tīrtha and read a story from the Purāṇas

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: That every Purāṇa you love was, in its own frame, first told here, to people sitting quietly under trees.

Naimiṣa is a place of listening. Its sages sat for years hearing the old stories; its temple stands where, in legend, the Goddess calmed the overflowing waters.

For a Śrīvidyā reader, the heart-pīṭha and the Purāṇa’s frame together make Naimiṣa a quiet, fitting end to a journey through Lalitā’s temples.

The lesson may be the simplest of all: sit still, listen, and let the story reach the heart.
