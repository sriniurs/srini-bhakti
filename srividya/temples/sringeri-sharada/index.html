ŚṚṄGERI ŚĀRADĀ

Knowledge enthroned on the Śrīcakra

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

At Śṛṅgeri, by the Tuṅgā, the Goddess is Śāradā, Sarasvatī as the giver of knowledge. Temple tradition says her first image was set over a Śrīcakra carved by Śaṅkara himself.

This guide tells how Śaṅkara chose the place and how Sarasvatī came to stay, explains the Śrīcakra and Śāradā’s identity with Lalitā, and visits the Vidyāśaṅkara temple and the worship of the maṭha.

Śrī Śāradāmbāyai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why would Sarasvatī sit on the Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: Because knowledge, at its height, is the Goddess herself.

ZAPPY: Sarasvatī in a Śrīvidyā series?

PROF. BOLT: At Śṛṅgeri, Zappy, Śāradā sits on a Śrīcakra. Sarasvatī and Lalitā are one Goddess here.

Śṛṅgeri, in the forested hills of the Western Ghats, is the seat of the southern maṭha of the Śaṅkara tradition and home of the Goddess Śāradā, Sarasvatī as the giver of knowledge. Our Foundations guide on Śaṅkara and Śrīvidyā introduced it; this guide looks at the temple itself.

Its Śrīvidyā link is direct. Temple tradition says Śaṅkara carved a Śrīcakra on a rock by the river and set Śāradā’s image above it, so that the Goddess of learning sits enthroned on the diagram of Lalitā.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma is recited at the temple daily, and during Navarātri Śāradā appears as Rājarājeśvarī among her nine forms. For the maṭha, Śāradā and Lalitā are one Goddess.

THE COBRA AND THE FROG

The sightOn the Tuṅgā’s bank, a cobra spread its hood to shade a frog in labour
MeaningNatural enemies at peace: a holy place
DecisionŚaṅkara chose it for his first maṭha, by tradition
ShrineKappe Śaṅkara (“frog Śaṅkara”) marks the spot on the river steps
ṚṣyaśṛṅgaThe town’s name comes from Ṛṣyaśṛṅga-giri, the hill of the Rāmāyaṇa sage
First headSureśvara, identified by tradition with Maṇḍana Miśra

ZAPPY: A snake protecting a frog?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the sign Śaṅkara read. Where even enemies don’t harm each other, knowledge can grow in peace.

The best-known Śṛṅgeri story says that Śaṅkara, walking by the Tuṅgā, saw a cobra spreading its hood to shade a frog in labour from the midday sun. Seeing natural enemies at peace, he judged the place holy and chose it for his first maṭha.

A small shrine on the river steps, Kappe Śaṅkara, “the frog Śaṅkara”, marks the spot.

The town’s name comes from Ṛṣyaśṛṅga-giri, the hill of the sage Ṛṣyaśṛṅga, who in the Rāmāyaṇa brought rain to a drought-stricken kingdom. Tradition names Sureśvara, identified with Maṇḍana Miśra, as the first head of the maṭha.

ZAPPY: Like Orpheus?

PROF. BOLT: A little! He looked back, and she stopped. But here it’s a blessing: she stayed at Śṛṅgeri for good.

The Śaṅkara biographies tell of a great debate between Śaṅkara and the Mīmāṃsā scholar Maṇḍana Miśra, judged by Maṇḍana’s learned wife, Ubhaya Bhāratī. When Śaṅkara won, Maṇḍana became his disciple.

Ubhaya Bhāratī is held to be Sarasvatī herself on earth; one account says a curse of Durvāsa had brought her down. Śaṅkara asked her to come with him; she agreed to follow on condition that he not look back.

At Śṛṅgeri he looked back, and she stayed there. The story explains why the Goddess of knowledge has her seat in this forest town.

ZAPPY: So the Goddess of learning sits on the Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. At Śṛṅgeri, knowledge itself is the Goddess of the Śrīcakra.

According to the maṭha’s own account, the first shrine was a simple one, with a sandalwood image of Śāradā set over a Śrīcakra that Śaṅkara had carved on a rock. The original sandalwood image, damaged with age, is kept today in the Vidyāśaṅkara temple.

In the fourteenth century, a Kerala-style temple of timber and tile was built, and a seated golden image replaced the standing sandalwood one.

The arrangement says something about the place. The Goddess of speech and learning is enthroned on the Śrīcakra, the diagram of Lalitā. Knowledge, at Śṛṅgeri, is the Goddess of Śrīvidyā.

At ŚṛṅgeriThe Lalitā Sahasranāma is recited daily, the temple guide says
NavarātriŚāradā is dressed as Rājarājeśvarī among her forms
Maṭha viewŚāradā and Lalitā are the same Goddess

ZAPPY: Is Sarasvatī Lalitā’s attendant or Lalitā herself?

PROF. BOLT: Both, in the Sahasranāma: she fans the Goddess, and she is one of the Goddess’s names. Śṛṅgeri worships the second.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma links the two goddesses in three ways. In śloka 42 Lalitā is “Śāradārādhyā”, worshipped by Śāradā, or worshipped in autumn. In śloka 123 she is fanned by Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī on her left and right. And in śloka 137 “Sarasvatī” is one of her own names.

The temple guide notes that the Lalitā Sahasranāma is recited daily at Śṛṅgeri. In Navarātri, Śāradā’s festival image takes many forms, one of them Rājarājeśvarī, Lalitā as queen of kings.

For the maṭha there is no contradiction. Sarasvatī as attendant and Sarasvatī as the Goddess herself are two ways of seeing the same truth.

ZAPPY: How old is the temple really?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition goes back to Śaṅkara. Inscriptions begin in the 14th century. The present building is from 1916.

The maṭha traces its temple to Śaṅkara in the eighth century. Historians note that records of the maṭhas begin only in the fourteenth century, so the earlier history is traditional.

In the fourteenth century, Bhāratī Kṛṣṇa Tīrtha and Vidyāraṇya, with support from the first Vijayanagara rulers, built a Kerala-style temple and installed the golden image.

The present granite temple was raised under Saccidānanda Śivābhinava Nṛsiṃha Bhāratī and consecrated by Candraśekhara Bhāratī in May 1916. A golden chariot for the Goddess was dedicated in 1999.

VIDYĀRAṆYA AND VIJAYANAGARA

A sage and an empire
WhoVidyāraṇya, scholar and later head of the maṭha (from about 1375)
EmpireTradition credits him with guiding Harihara and Bukka, founders of Vijayanagara
1346The brothers granted tax-free land around Śṛṅgeri to the maṭha
BooksAdvaita works including the Pañcadaśī
Not a mantraHis Pañcadaśī is an Advaita treatise in fifteen chapters, not the Śrīvidyā mantra of the same name
DurbarThe Navarātri durbar is traced to this royal link

ZAPPY: Pañcadaśī? Isn’t that a secret mantra?

PROF. BOLT: Same word, different thing. Vidyāraṇya’s Pañcadaśī is a book of fifteen chapters on Vedānta, read openly by everyone.

Vidyāraṇya is one of the great figures of South Indian history: a scholar of Vedānta and, by tradition, the guide of Harihara and Bukka, who founded the Vijayanagara empire in 1336. He became head of the Śṛṅgeri maṭha around 1375.

Vijayanagara inscriptions record a grant of tax-free land around Śṛṅgeri to the maṭha in 1346. The royal link is why the maṭha dates its Navarātri durbar to the founding of Vijayanagara.

Among Vidyāraṇya’s works is the Pañcadaśī, a famous Advaita textbook in fifteen chapters. It shares its name with the fifteen-syllabled Śrīvidyā mantra, but it is a different thing entirely: a public book of philosophy.

THE VIDYĀŚAṄKARA TEMPLE

Stone and stars

StyleHoysaḷa and Vijayanagara features, on a carved plinth
Zodiac pillarsTwelve pillars, each carved with a sign of the zodiac
SunlightThe sun’s rays are said to fall on each pillar in its month
TreasureThe original sandalwood Śāradā is kept here
FestivalA chariot festival in Kārttika

ZAPPY: A temple that tells the month?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the tradition about the zodiac pillars. Astronomers have studied them; the design is remarkable either way.

The Vidyāśaṅkara temple stands beside the Śāradā temple. Begun in 1338 under the first Vijayanagara rulers, at Vidyāraṇya’s request, it was built over the samādhi of the earlier teacher Vidyātīrtha, also called Vidyāśaṅkara.

It combines Hoysaḷa and Vijayanagara features on a richly carved plinth. In its hall stand twelve pillars, each carved with a sign of the zodiac; tradition says the morning sun falls on each pillar in its own month. Astronomers have studied the design.

The original sandalwood image of Śāradā, installed by Śaṅkara according to tradition, is kept in this temple.

THE JAGADGURU’S WORSHIP

NightlyThe Jagadguru worships it every night

ZAPPY: Can visitors watch?

PROF. BOLT: The night worship of Candramaulīśvara can be attended at the maṭha. It’s calm and beautiful.

The heads of the maṭha, the Jagadgurus, perform daily worship in the maṭha. Each night they worship the Candramaulīśvara liṅga, a crystal liṅga said in tradition to have been given to Śaṅkara by Śiva.

The maṭha’s worship includes Śrīvidyā rites belonging to its own lineage. As elsewhere in this series, we name the tradition but do not describe its procedures.

The maṭha counts thirty-six heads from Sureśvara to the present senior Jagadguru, Bhāratī Tīrtha, whose successor Vidhuśekhara Bhāratī now shares the duties.

ZAPPY: Rājarājeśvarī at Śṛṅgeri?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, one of her nine forms. Lalitā appears in plain sight during Navarātri.

See: Navarātri and Mysuru Dasarā

Śarannavarātri is Śṛṅgeri’s greatest festival. It begins with a great bathing of the Goddess on the new moon before Navarātri, and each day her festival image is dressed in a new form.

The forms include the Seven Mothers, Rājarājeśvarī, Vīṇā Śāradā and Jagat Prasūtikā, the mother holding the world as a baby. Each night the Jagadguru holds a durbar in royal dress.

Our festivals guide, Navarātri and Mysuru Dasarā, covers Śṛṅgeri’s Navarātri alongside Mysuru’s.

ZAPPY: A Gaṇapati drawn with turmeric?

PROF. BOLT: On a pillar, by a Jagadguru. It was later carved where he drew it.

Around the Śāradā temple stand shrines to Toraṇa Gaṇapati and to Ādi Śaṅkara. The Stambha Gaṇapati was drawn on a pillar with a piece of turmeric by the twenty-fourth Jagadguru in the seventeenth century, and later carved where he drew it.

On a nearby hill stands the old Malahānikareśvara temple of Śiva. Tradition says Śaṅkara placed guardian deities in the four directions around the town.

On the river steps, pilgrims feed the large fish of the Tuṅgā, who gather at the bank, unafraid.

ZAPPY: When should I go?

PROF. BOLT: Navarātri for the alaṅkāras; any quiet day for the river and the night pūjā.

Navarātri in September–October is the great festival. Śaṅkara Jayantī falls in April–May.

The Vidyātīrtha chariot festival is held for seven days in the bright half of Kārttika, and on Kārttika Pūrṇimā the Lakṣa Dīpotsava lights a hundred thousand lamps.

On Mahāśivarātri the Jagadguru performs special worship. Every day, the Lalitā Sahasranāma is recited and the night worship held at the maṭha.

VisitSit by the Tuṅgā and recite a few names of the Sahasranāma

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: The cobra and the frog. Wisdom needs safety, and gives it.

Śṛṅgeri begins with a picture of peace: the cobra sheltering the frog. Its lesson is that knowledge needs a place where enemies do not harm each other, and gives that peace in turn.

Its Goddess is knowledge itself, enthroned on the Śrīcakra. Here Advaita philosophy and Śrīvidyā worship live in one maṭha, as our Foundations guide described.

And the story of Ubhaya Bhāratī suggests how a sacred place is fixed: not by plan, but by a single moment of turning back.

Śāradā is a name of Sarasvatī often explained as “she of autumn” (śarad), the season of clear skies, or as the Goddess worshipped in the autumn Navarātri. Kashmir was once called Śāradā-deśa, the land of Śāradā.
