TIRUVĀNAIKKĀVAL AKHILĀṆḌEŚVARĪ

The Goddess who wears the Śrīcakra

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

At Tiruvānaikkāval, beside Śrīraṅgam, Śiva is the liṅga of water and the Goddess is Akhilāṇḍeśvarī, Queen of all the worlds. Tradition says she was once fierce, until Śaṅkara gave her earrings carved with the Śrīcakra.

This guide tells the temple’s stories, explains the earrings and the Gaṇapati who faces her, describes the noon worship in which the priest becomes the Goddess, and looks at the Śrīvidyā worship the temple records for her.

Śrī Akhilāṇḍeśvaryai namaḥ,

ZAPPY: Why would a goddess need calming?

PROF. BOLT: Because great power, turned toward the world, must become grace. The Śrīcakra does that.

ZAPPY: A Goddess calmed by earrings?

PROF. BOLT: By Śrīcakra earrings, Zappy. The whole Śrīvidyā world, worn on her ears.

Tiruvānaikkāval is one of the five great Śiva temples of the elements, the temple of water. Its Śiva is Jambukeśvara, a liṅga under a jambu tree, with a spring that keeps the sanctum floor wet.

Its Goddess, Akhilāṇḍeśvarī, is the reason this temple appears in a Śrīvidyā series. Tradition says she was once fierce, and that Śaṅkara calmed her by giving her earrings carved with the Śrīcakra and by placing Gaṇapati opposite her shrine.

The temple’s own listing names the Goddess’s worship as Śrīvidyā Vaidika pūjā. And every day at noon her priest, dressed as the Goddess, carries her worship to Śiva.

THE PLACE

Elephant-guarded grove
NameTiru-āṉai-kkā: the sacred grove of the elephant

TreeThe white nāval (jambu) tree, under which the liṅga stands

BuilderTradition: the early Chola king Kocceṅkaṇāṉ
SizeFive enclosures, among the largest temples in Tamil Nadu

ZAPPY: Why an elephant in the name?

PROF. BOLT: Because of the old story of an elephant and a spider who both worshipped here. You’ll meet them next.

Tiruvānaikkāval stands on the island between the Kāverī and the Koḷḷiḍam, a short walk from the great Raṅganātha temple of Śrīraṅgam. Its Tamil name means the sacred grove of the elephant.

The liṅga stands under a white nāval (jambu) tree. A spring rises in the sanctum, so the liṅga is said always to stand in water: this is the appu liṅga, the liṅga of water, among the five elemental liṅgas.

Tradition credits the first temple to the early Chola king Kocceṅkaṇāṉ. The present complex, with five enclosures and tall gopurams, grew over many centuries.

THE ELEPHANT AND THE SPIDER

ZAPPY: They both loved Śiva and still fought?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the point of the story. Devotion can be blind. Śiva accepted both.

The Pañcabhūta series on this site covers the water liṅga in full.

The best-known legend of the temple tells of an elephant and a spider who both worshipped the liṅga. The elephant bathed it with river water and flowers; the spider spun a web over it to shield it from falling leaves and the sun.

The elephant, thinking the web was dirt, tore it away each day. The angry spider crept into the elephant’s trunk and stung it; both died. Śiva granted liberation to the elephant, and the spider was reborn as the Chola king Kocceṅkaṇāṉ.

Remembering his former life, the king built the sanctum with a door too narrow for an elephant. He is counted among the sixty-three Nāyaṉmār, the saints of Tamil Śaivism.

THE GODDESS’S PENANCE

Why she is a student here
CausePārvatī mocked Śiva’s austerity; he sent her to earth to do penance
The liṅgaIn the jambu forest she shaped a liṅga from Kāverī water
TeachingŚiva appeared and taught her Śiva-jñāna
FacingHe faces west, she faces east: teacher and student
Upadeśa sthalaA “place of teaching”, so there is no wedding festival here
NameAs Queen of all the worlds, she is Akhilāṇḍeśvarī

ZAPPY: No wedding for them here?

PROF. BOLT: No. Here they are guru and disciple, not bride and groom. That’s rare among Śiva temples.

Another legend explains the Goddess. Pārvatī once laughed at Śiva’s penance, and he sent her to earth to do penance herself. In the jambu forest she shaped a liṅga from the water of the Kāverī and worshipped it.

Śiva appeared and taught her the knowledge of Śiva. In the temple he faces west and she faces east, as teacher and student face each other. Temples where Śiva taught in this way are called upadeśa sthalas.

Because they are guru and disciple here, not bride and groom, the temple holds no wedding festival for them, unlike most Śiva temples.

THE NOON WORSHIP

The priest as the Goddess
WhenEvery day at the noon worship (ucci kāla pūjā)
WhoThe Goddess’s priest, in a sari and her crown
ProcessionWith music, and sometimes the temple elephant
WhatHe worships Jambukeśvara and the temple cow
MeaningThe Goddess herself comes daily to worship Śiva
For visitorsOne of the most remarkable rites in any Tamil temple

ZAPPY: A man dressed as the Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: For one rite a day. He isn’t pretending; he’s carrying her worship to Śiva, as she did in the legend.

At the noon worship each day, the priest of the Goddess’s shrine puts on a sari and the Goddess’s crown. He walks in procession, with music and sometimes the temple elephant, to the sanctum of Jambukeśvara, and there performs the worship of Śiva and of the temple cow.

The meaning is that Akhilāṇḍeśvarī herself comes every day to worship Śiva, as she did in her penance. The priest is not acting a part; he is carrying her worship.

It is one of the most remarkable living rituals in South India, and visitors who time their visit for noon can see it.

ZAPPY: Vārāhī? Isn’t she Lalitā’s general?

PROF. BOLT: She is. Our deity guide to Vārāhī explains her. Here the fierce power is gathered into the Śrīcakra and worn as ornament.

See also: Vārāhī, the commander

Temple tradition says that Akhilāṇḍeśvarī was once an ugra, a fierce and frightening Goddess, and that devotees prayed to her from outside the shrine. Some accounts say her fierce form was that of Vārāhī, Lalitā’s boar-faced commander.

Śaṅkara is said to have visited the temple and calmed her, not by suppressing her power but by turning it. He gave her earrings carved with the Śrīcakra and placed a smiling Gaṇapati opposite her.

The story parallels Kāñcī’s, where the Goddess is calmed by a Śrīcakra set before her. In both, the Śrīcakra is the means by which fierce power becomes grace.

THE ŚRĪCAKRA EARRINGS

The tāṭaṅka
WhatA pair of heavy earrings, each carved with a Śrīcakra

SahasranāmaŚloka 8: her two earrings are the sun and the moon

ZAPPY: Why earrings, of all things?

PROF. BOLT: Because in the Sahasranāma her earrings are the sun and moon. Put the Śrīcakra there, and the cosmos is her ornament.

The tāṭaṅkas are a pair of heavy earrings, each bearing a Śrīcakra. They are the most distinctive feature of the Goddess’s image, and devotees look for them at once.

They are cleaned and reconsecrated from time to time by the Śaṅkarācāryas of the Kāñcī Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham, who take them away and return them after a week of rites. The most recent such reconsecration was reported in 2024.

In the Lalitā Sahasranāma, the Goddess’s two earrings are the sun and the moon. At Tiruvānaikkāval the earrings carry the Śrīcakra, the diagram of the whole universe, as if to show the same truth in another way.

ZAPPY: Did it work?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess is worshipped today as gentle and gracious. Devotees say Gaṇapati’s smile did its share.

See also: Mahāgaṇapati

Opposite the Goddess’s shrine stands Prasanna Gaṇapati, the cheerful Gaṇeśa. Tradition says Śaṅkara installed him there, on the idea that no mother can stay angry when she sees her child’s face.

Devotees also say that the Goddess and Gaṇapati, placed as they are, together trace the shape of the syllable Om.

In Śrīvidyā worship, Mahāgaṇapati is honoured before the Goddess. Our deity guide to Mahāgaṇapati explains his place in the tradition.

ZAPPY: So the Goddess here is worshipped by Śrīvidyā rules?

PROF. BOLT: That’s what the temple’s own listing says. It’s one reason this temple belongs in our series.

The temple guide published by Dinamalar lists the temple’s worship as Śaivāgama, and Śrīvidyā Vaidika pūjā. The first refers to Śiva’s worship by the Śaiva Āgamas; the second to the Goddess’s, along Śrīvidyā lines with Vedic recitation.

The Kāñcī Śaṅkara Maṭha has a branch beside the temple on its north street, and the Kāñcī ācāryas’ care for the earrings ties the shrine to that tradition.

As elsewhere in this series, we describe the tradition but do not give its procedures, which belong to initiated priests.

ZAPPY: ‘All the eggs’?

PROF. BOLT: Aṇḍa means a cosmic egg, a universe. She is queen of all of them, every universe there is.

The Lalitā Sahasranāma describes the Goddess, in its eighth śloka, as one whose pair of earrings are the sun and the moon. At Tiruvānaikkāval, the earrings are the temple’s great treasure.

Her name, Akhilāṇḍeśvarī, means queen of all the aṇḍas, the cosmic eggs or universes. The Sahasranāma similarly praises the Goddess as mother of countless worlds.

The water liṅga adds another link: the Goddess made it with her own hands from the river, so the Śiva she worships is, in a sense, her own creation.

ZAPPY: Is that famous song really Dīkṣitar’s?

PROF. BOLT: Most singers say so. A few scholars point out it never mentions the temple, unusual for him. We note both.

The Tēvāram poets, the Tamil Śaiva saints, sang of Tiruvānaikkāval, which is one of the 275 temples praised in their hymns.

A famous Tamil legend tells how the poet Kāḷamēkam, once a temple cook, received the Goddess’s grace here and became a great poet, known for his quick and witty verse.

The Carnatic song “Akhilāṇḍeśvari rakṣa mām” in Dvijāvanti is sung everywhere and usually credited to Dīkṣitar. Some scholars doubt this, noting it has none of the local details he usually includes. Śyāma Śāstri also sang of the Goddess with Jambūpati, her consort.

ZAPPY: When to go?

PROF. BOLT: Any day near noon, to see the priest carry the Goddess’s worship to Śiva.

The great annual festival falls in the Tamil month of Pañguni, March–April. Fridays in Āḍi, July–August, are special days for the Goddess, as in most Tamil temples.

Navarātri is celebrated for Akhilāṇḍeśvarī, and a float festival takes place in Tai, January–February.

The noon worship, with the priest as the Goddess, happens every day, and is the best reason to visit at midday.

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: The earrings. The Śrīcakra is so precious that the Goddess wears it close to her face.

Tiruvānaikkāval’s central story is about turning power, not destroying it. The Goddess’s fierceness is not taken away; it is gathered into the Śrīcakra and worn as ornament, and softened by the sight of her child.

The temple also shows the Goddess as a student, receiving Śiva’s teaching. In Śrīvidyā the Goddess is often the teacher; here she shows how to learn.

And the elephant and the spider show that devotion comes in many forms, some clumsy, some fierce, and that the Lord accepts them all.
