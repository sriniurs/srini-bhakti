TIRUVĀRŪR KAMALĀMBĀ

The yoginī Goddess and the songs of the Śrīcakra

ŚRĪVIDYĀ · A REFERENCE GUIDE

FOREWORD

At Tiruvārūr, inside the great Tyāgarāja temple, the Goddess Kamalāmbā sits in yogic pose in her own shrine. For her, Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar composed the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, eleven songs that walk through the nine enclosures of the Śrīcakra.

This guide covers the town of lotuses, the temple and its legends, Kamalāmbā and her story, the songs and their rāgas, and the remarkable fact that all three great Carnatic composers were born here.

Śrī Kamalāmbikāyai namaḥ,

ZAPPY: Why is this temple in a Śrīvidyā series?

PROF. BOLT: Because of one Goddess and one set of songs, Zappy: Kamalāmbā and the Navāvaraṇa kṛtis.

Tiruvārūr, in the Kāverī delta, is home to one of the greatest temple complexes of Tamil Nadu, dedicated to Śiva as Tyāgarāja. Inside it, in a shrine of her own, sits the Goddess Kamalāmbā.

She is unusual in two ways. Her image sits cross-legged in a yogic pose, which a study of the temple describes as unique among Hindu images. And she is the Goddess for whom Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar composed the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, the most famous Śrīvidyā music ever written.

Tiruvārūr is also the birthplace of all three of the Carnatic Trinity: Śyāma Śāstri, Tyāgarāja and Dīkṣitar. This guide covers the town, the temple, Kamalāmbā and her songs.

TIRUVĀRŪR

The town of the lotus
KamalālayaIts old name: “abode of lotuses”, hence Kamalāmbā and the Kamalālayam tank

TēvāramSung by the Śaiva saints; a pāḍal peṟṟa sthalam
SayingBirth at Tiruvārūr gives liberation, a well-known verse says

ZAPPY: Born here, and you’re liberated?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the old saying: birth at Tiruvārūr, sight of Chidambaram, thought of Aruṇācala, death at Kāśī. Each gives liberation.

Tiruvārūr’s old Sanskrit name, Kamalālaya-kṣetra, means the sacred place that is an abode of lotuses. From it come both the Goddess’s name, Kamalāmbā, and the temple tank’s, Kamalālayam.

The town was one of the traditional capitals of the Cholas and is sung in the Tēvāram, the hymns of the Tamil Śaiva saints. Tamil legend makes it the seat of Manu Nīti Cōḻaṉ, the king famous for his justice.

A well-known verse says that birth at Tiruvārūr, the sight of Chidambaram, the thought of Aruṇācala and death at Kāśī each give liberation. Tiruvārūr is also called the mūlādhāra kṣetra, the root centre among holy places.

THE TYĀGARĀJA TEMPLE

A city within a city
SizeSeveral hectares, with nine gopurams and dozens of shrines
Two ŚivasVanmīkanātha in an anthill, and Tyāgarāja as Somāskanda
SomāskandaŚiva seated with Umā, Skanda between them
Ajapā naṭanaTyāgarāja’s dance is said to follow the rhythm of breath
InscriptionsChola records from the 11th century, under Rājendra I
TankKamalālayam, about as large as the temple itself

ZAPPY: A dance of breath?

PROF. BOLT: Ajapā means ‘without recitation’: the mantra the breath says by itself. Tyāgarāja dances to it.

The temple complex is one of the largest in India, with nine gopurams and many shrines. It has two principal forms of Śiva: Vanmīkanātha, a liṅga in an anthill, and Tyāgarāja, a processional image of Śiva as Somāskanda, seated with Umā and with the child Skanda between them.

Tyāgarāja’s dance is called ajapā naṭana. Ajapā is the mantra the breath recites on its own, without effort; the dance is said to follow its rhythm.

Chola inscriptions on the shrine walls go back to the eleventh century, and the temple influenced Rājarāja Chola’s great temple at Thanjavur. The Kamalālayam tank to its west is about as large as the temple itself.

MUCUKUNDA AND THE SEVEN IMAGES

How Tyāgarāja came

IndraKept the image in heaven
The boonKing Mucukunda Chola helped Indra and asked for it
The testIndra made six copies; the king chose the true one
SevenThe seven images became the seven Viṭaṅka shrines of the delta
NameTyāgarāja: “king of renunciation”, or of giving

ZAPPY: Why make six fakes?

PROF. BOLT: Indra didn’t want to give it up. Mucukunda’s devotion saw through the trick.

The legend of the Tyāgarāja image says that Viṣṇu worshipped Śiva as Somāskanda, resting on his chest. The image passed to Indra’s heaven.

King Mucukunda Chola helped Indra in battle and asked for the image as his reward. Unwilling to give it up, Indra had six copies made, but the king picked the true one.

The seven images, the true one and the six copies, became the seven Viṭaṅka shrines of the Kāverī delta, with Tiruvārūr first among them. Tyāgarāja is usually explained as king of renunciation or of giving.

KAMALĀMBĀ

The Goddess in yogic pose
ShrineHer own shrine in the third enclosure, facing north
PoseSeated with legs crossed in yoga, described as unique among Hindu images
HandsA flower in the right hand, the left resting on her hip
CrownA crescent moon on her head
StoryShe did penance to marry Tyāgarāja; tradition says her wish was not fulfilled
ConsortNīlotpalāmbā is worshipped as Tyāgarāja’s consort, in another shrine

ZAPPY: A goddess whose wish wasn’t granted?

PROF. BOLT: She stays in penance, as a yoginī, forever turned toward Kailāsa. That’s why she sits in yoga, not as a bride.

Kamalāmbā’s shrine stands in the third enclosure of the temple, facing north. She sits cross-legged in a yogic posture, holding a flower in her right hand, with her left resting on her hip, and wears a crescent moon in her crown.

A study of her image describes this posture as unique: no other Hindu temple image of the Goddess is known to sit this way.

Temple tradition says she did penance to marry Tyāgarāja and that her wish was never fulfilled. Nīlotpalāmbā, in another shrine, is worshipped as his consort. Kamalāmbā remains in penance, a yoginī, facing north toward Kailāsa.

A YOGINĪ AND A PĪṬHA

Kamalāmbā in Śākta tradition
YoginīShe is worshipped as the yoginī, the tantric centre of the temple
PīṭhaCounted by some among the Śakti Pīṭhas, sometimes the 64
ŚrīcakraThe temple’s enclosures are sometimes read as the layers of the Śrīcakra
NorthFacing north, toward Kailāsa, the direction of release
ContrastHer yogic stillness against the wedded Goddess within
CautionThe pīṭha and Śrīcakra readings are devotional, not ancient records

ZAPPY: Is the temple built as a Śrīcakra?

PROF. BOLT: Some devotees read it that way. It’s a devotional reading, and a lovely one. We label it as such.

In local tradition Kamalāmbā is the yoginī, the centre of tantric power in the temple, and her shrine is counted by some among the Śakti Pīṭhas, sometimes listed among the sixty-four.

Devotees sometimes read the temple’s enclosures as the layers of the Śrīcakra, with Kamalāmbā near the centre. This is a devotional reading rather than a recorded design, and we present it as such.

Her northward gaze, toward Kailāsa, is often explained as the direction of release from rebirth, matching her posture of penance.

DĪKṢITAR’S TOWN

Born1775 or 1776, at Tiruvārūr
FatherRāmasvāmi Dīkṣitar, musician and Śrīvidyā devotee
InitiationInto Śrīvidyā; he signed every song “Guruguha”
Half his lifeSpent at Tiruvārūr, devoted to Kamalāmbā and Tyāgarāja
Song setsNavāvaraṇa for Kamalāmbā; vibhakti sets for Tyāgarāja and Nīlotpalāmbā
Ganapati“Śrī Mahāgaṇapati ravatu mām” for the Gaṇapati by the tank

ZAPPY: He wrote whole sets for one temple?

PROF. BOLT: Three sets for the deities here alone. Tiruvārūr was the centre of his life and music.

Muttusvāmi Dīkṣitar was born at Tiruvārūr in 1775 or 1776, the son of Rāmasvāmi Dīkṣitar, himself a musician and a devotee of the Goddess. He was initiated into Śrīvidyā and signed all his songs with the name Guruguha.

He spent about half his life in Tiruvārūr, deeply devoted to Kamalāmbā and the Tyāgarāja deities. Here he composed the Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis, and sets of songs in the eight grammatical cases for Tyāgarāja and for Nīlotpalāmbā.

His song “Śrī Mahāgaṇapati ravatu mām” is addressed to a Gaṇapati shrine on the bank of the Kamalālayam tank. Dīkṣitar will have his own guide in our Teachers section.

THE NAVĀVARAṆA KṚTIS

Eleven songs on the Śrīcakra
ShapeA dhyāna song, nine āvaraṇa songs and a maṅgaḷa song
GrammarThe eight āvaraṇa songs use the name Kamalāmbā in eight cases; the ninth uses them all
ContentsEach song names the enclosure’s goddesses, yoginīs, siddhis and mudrās
LineageDīkṣitar calls her “worshipped by Durvāsa”, the mark of the Kādi line
UseSung in groups during Navarātri; studied by Śrīvidyā devotees
ComingA verse-by-verse guide in this series, from a supplied text

ZAPPY: Eight cases of one word?

PROF. BOLT: Kamalāmbā, Kamalāmbām, Kamalāmbayā and so on. It’s grammar used as worship: the Goddess in every relation.

The Kamalāmbā Navāvaraṇa kṛtis are eleven songs. The first is a dhyāna song, a meditation on the Goddess; then come nine songs, one for each enclosure of the Śrīcakra; and the last is a maṅgaḷa song of blessing.

The nine āvaraṇa songs use the name Kamalāmbā in all eight grammatical cases of Sanskrit, one per song, with the ninth using all eight together. Each names the enclosure’s presiding goddess, yoginī, attendant powers, siddhi and mudrā, as named in the Śrīvidyā texts.

Dīkṣitar calls the Goddess “worshipped by Durvāsa”, which marks his own lineage, the Kādi line. The songs are public music, sung in concerts and in groups during Navarātri. A verse-by-verse guide to them is planned for this series once a text is supplied.

THE ELEVEN, ONE BY ONE

Rāgas of the set
DhyānaKamalāmbike (Toḍi)
Āvaraṇas 1–3Kamalāmbā saṃrakṣatu (Ānandabhairavi); Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re (Kalyāṇi); Śrī Kamalāmbikayā (Śaṅkarābharaṇam)
Āvaraṇas 4–6Kamalāmbikāyai (Kāmbhoji); Śrī Kamalāmbikāyāḥ (Bhairavi); Kamalāmbikāyāḥ tava (Punnāgavarāḷi)
Āvaraṇas 7–9Śrī Kamalāmbikāyām (Sahānā); Śrī Kamalāmbike (Ghaṇṭā); Śrī Kamalāmbā jayati (Āhiri)
MaṅgaḷaŚrī Kamalāmbike śive (Śrī)
NoteRāgas as in the usual published order; talas vary between editions

ZAPPY: Why end in rāga Śrī?

PROF. BOLT: Śrī is the most auspicious rāga to close with, and it is also the Goddess’s own name.

The usual published order of the eleven songs, with their rāgas, is: Kamalāmbike in Toḍi; Kamalāmbā saṃrakṣatu in Ānandabhairavi; Kamalāmbāṃ bhaja re in Kalyāṇi; Śrī Kamalāmbikayā in Śaṅkarābharaṇam; Kamalāmbikāyai in Kāmbhoji; and Śrī Kamalāmbikāyāḥ in Bhairavi.

Then Kamalāmbikāyāḥ tava in Punnāgavarāḷi; Śrī Kamalāmbikāyām in Sahānā; Śrī Kamalāmbike in Ghaṇṭā; Śrī Kamalāmbā jayati in Āhiri; and the closing Śrī Kamalāmbike śive in Śrī.

The tālas are given differently in different editions, so we list only the rāgas here. Musicians admire the set for its range of rāgas, its rhythmic variety and its use of rare forms.

THREE COMPOSERS, ONE TOWN

The Trinity’s birthplace

TyāgarājaBorn 1767; named after the temple’s deity
DīkṣitarBorn 1775/76
HallA memorial hall with their sculptures stands near the temple
FestivalA Trinity festival is held in Tiruvārūr
WonderThree founders of Carnatic music within thirteen years, in one town

ZAPPY: All three born here?

PROF. BOLT: All three. The golden Kāmākṣī was even staying at Tiruvārūr when Śyāma Śāstri was born.

All three composers of the Carnatic Trinity were born at Tiruvārūr within a few years of each other: Śyāma Śāstri in 1762, Tyāgarāja in 1767 and Dīkṣitar in 1775 or 1776.

Śyāma Śāstri was born there because his family, the priests of the golden Kāmākṣī carried away from Kāñcī, were then staying at Tiruvārūr with the image. Tyāgarāja was named after the temple’s deity.

A memorial hall with sculptures of the three stands near the temple, and a music festival in their honour is held in the town.

THE GREAT CHARIOT

Āḻittēr
SizeAbout 29 m tall when decorated, around 360 tonnes
RecordThe largest temple chariot in India
WhenThe chariot festival in Pañguni–Citrai (March–April)
Pulled byThousands of devotees on long ropes
RouteThe four great streets around the temple
Saying“Tiruvārūr tēr aḻaku”: the beauty of Tiruvārūr’s chariot is proverbial

ZAPPY: Why so huge?

PROF. BOLT: To carry the Lord through his whole town. When it moves, the whole town moves with it.

The temple’s great chariot, the Āḻittēr, is the largest temple chariot in India, about 29 metres tall when fully decorated and weighing around 360 tonnes.

It is drawn through the four great streets around the temple in the annual chariot festival, in March–April, by thousands of devotees pulling long ropes.

The beauty of the Tiruvārūr chariot is a Tamil proverb. Its route marks out the sacred town, with the temple at its centre.

ZAPPY: Best time for a Śrīvidyā devotee?

PROF. BOLT: Navarātri, to hear the Navāvaraṇa kṛtis sung where they were born.

The great annual festival with the chariot falls in Pañguni–Citrai, March–April. Ārudrā darśana in Mārgaḻi celebrates Śiva’s dance, and a float festival is held on the Kamalālayam tank.

For the Goddess, Navarātri and the Fridays of Āḍi are special days. In Navarātri, the Navāvaraṇa kṛtis are sung in their home temple.

A festival in honour of the Trinity brings musicians to the town where all three were born.

WHAT TIRUVĀRŪR TEACHES

For the reader

UnfulfilledA wish held in penance becomes a power of its own
MusicŚrīvidyā carried in song, for anyone to hear
GrammarEvery relation to the Goddess is worship
RootMūlādhāra kṣetra: the root of the sacred map
VisitSit before Kamalāmbā and listen to one āvaraṇa song

ZAPPY: What’s the one thing to remember?

PROF. BOLT: That Śrīvidyā’s most famous songs were written for a Goddess sitting in yoga, in her own small shrine, in a giant temple.

Kamalāmbā sits in yoga, not as a bride. Her story is of a wish held in penance, and the stillness that comes from it. For Śrīvidyā she is a picture of the Goddess turned inward.

Her songs show that Śrīvidyā can be carried in music for anyone to hear. The initiated worship of the Śrīcakra stays with the guru; Dīkṣitar’s songs about it are sung in concert halls everywhere.

The grammar of the songs has its own lesson: the Goddess can be addressed, praised, served, given to, approached and rested in. Every relation to her is worship.

Vibhakti, the grammatical case, is used devotionally in Sanskrit: by putting a deity’s name in every case, a poet shows every way one can relate to the deity. Dīkṣitar used this device in several song sets.
