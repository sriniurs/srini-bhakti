WHY SO MANY GODS?

One reality, many faces

The Veda’s answer. The Ṛgveda (1.164.46) says: “Truth is one; the wise call it by many names.” Most Hindu traditions hold that there is one ultimate reality, called Brahman, Bhagavān or simply God, and that the gods are its forms, powers or servants.

Each tradition has its supreme. Vaiṣṇavas see Viṣṇu as supreme, Śaivas Śiva, Śāktas the Devī; Smārtas worship five deities as forms of one Brahman. Many households honour several, with one as their own (iṣṭa devatā). Traditions differ on how the others relate to the supreme.

Why forms help. The infinite is hard to love; a form with a face, a story and a festival is not. The many gods are, for many devotees, many doorways to one house.

Read more. Who Is Viṣṇu, Who Is Śiva, Durgā and Pārvatī, and Brahmā (which explains Brahmā versus Brahman).

ZAPPY: So Hindus believe in one God or many?

PROF. BOLT: Both answers are heard, Zappy, and both are partly right. Ask which tradition, and you’ll get a precise answer.

One truth, many names.

IS IT IDOL WORSHIP?

What a mūrti is

A mūrti, not an idol. The image is called a mūrti, “embodiment”. Temple images are consecrated by rites (prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā) inviting the deity to be present; devotees treat the image as a living presence, not as a lump of stone.

A doorway. Most teachers explain that God is everywhere, and the image is where the devotee’s attention can meet him, as a photograph focuses love for a person. Some traditions worship without images, through the liṅga, a lamp, a book or the name alone.

Critics within. Image worship has had Hindu critics too, from some Upaniṣadic sages to reform movements like the Ārya Samāj. Traditions differ.

Read more. A Simple Home Pūjā and How to Read a Hindu Temple.

ZAPPY: Why bathe and feed an image?

PROF. BOLT: To treat God as an honoured guest, Zappy. The care changes the worshipper as much as anything.

An image as a doorway.

DO I NEED A GURU?

For prayer, no; for depth, usually

For daily prayer, no. Anyone may pray, light a lamp, recite open hymns and visit temples. Most Hindus learn their first prayers from parents and grandparents.

For depth, a teacher helps. The tradition values a guru for study and practice: scripture is traditionally learned from a teacher, and advanced meditation is risky to learn from books.

Initiation. Some mantras are given only through dīkṣā, initiation, in a lineage. This library never prints such mantras; it describes them. Everything we print is meant for open recitation.

Choosing well. Teachers in every tradition warn: test a teacher’s conduct and humility before trusting them. Read about the guru in The Guru and Datta Jayantī.

ZAPPY: Can a book be my teacher?

PROF. BOLT: A good start, Zappy. But a book can’t see when you’ve misunderstood. A teacher can.

Pray freely; study with a teacher.

CAN ANYONE CHANT THESE HYMNS?

Open hymns and restricted mantras

Stotras are for everyone. Hymns of praise, like the Sahasranāmas, aṣṭakams and the Hanumān Cālīsā, are meant for all devotees, with no initiation needed. Correct pronunciation is respected but devotion matters most.

Some recitation is restricted by tradition. Orthodox Vedic schools teach Vedic chanting through initiation and a teacher; some bīja mantras are given only in dīkṣā. Views on who may learn Vedic chanting differ today, and many teachers now teach openly. Traditions differ.

Meaning helps. This library gives every verse with a plain-English meaning, so that chanting is with understanding.

Start with. Gaṇeśa’s daily ślokas, Śāntākāram, or the Hanumān Cālīsā.

ZAPPY: What if I pronounce it wrong?

PROF. BOLT: Keep learning, Zappy, and keep chanting. A child’s wrong words still reach the mother.

Chant with meaning and love.

WHAT ARE KARMA AND REBIRTH?

Actions and their fruits

Karma. Literally “action”. Every intentional act leaves an effect that returns to the doer, in this life or later. It is not fate: present choices shape the future.

Rebirth. Most Hindu traditions hold that the self (ātman) takes body after body (saṃsāra) according to its karma, until it is freed (mokṣa).

Freedom. How liberation comes, through knowledge, devotion, disciplined action or grace, is where the schools differ most. The Bhagavad Gītā discusses all of these.

Read more. The Gītā’s chapter 2 and chapter 3; the Garuḍa Purāṇa’s account of what happens after death.

ZAPPY: So if something bad happens, I deserved it?

PROF. BOLT: Teachers warn against that, Zappy. Karma explains, it doesn’t blame; and the right response to suffering is compassion.

Action now shapes what comes.

WHICH BOOK FIRST?

A reading order for scripture

The Bhagavad Gītā. Seven hundred verses, every major teaching in brief, set in a story. Read it with a commentary or a guide; start with our Gītā overview.

Then the stories. The Rāmāyaṇa and the Mahābhārata (of which the Gītā is part) are how most Hindus first learn dharma. Our Hanumān stories cover much of the Rāmāyaṇa.

For the Devī. The Devī Māhātmya, seven hundred verses on the Goddess, read especially in Navarātri.

Later. The Upaniṣads, the Bhāgavata Purāṇa and the great commentaries reward slow study with a teacher.

ZAPPY: Can I read the Gītā alone?

PROF. BOLT: You can start alone, Zappy. Read slowly, and talk about it with someone who has read it longer.

Start with the Gītā.

WHY DO FESTIVAL DATES MOVE?

Lunar months and tithis

Lunar months. Most Hindu festivals follow the moon: a month has a bright half and a dark half, each of fifteen lunar days called tithis. A festival falls on a tithi, so its solar date moves each year.

Solar festivals. A few follow the sun: Makara Saṅkrānti stays near 14 January because it marks the sun entering Capricorn.

Keeping in step. An extra month is added about every three years to keep lunar months aligned with the seasons. Regions also differ: the South mostly counts months from new moon to new moon, the North from full moon to full moon.

Read more. Our Pañcāṅga guide explains tithi, nakṣatra and the rest, with a table of every festival.

ZAPPY: So Dīpāvali isn’t on a fixed date?

PROF. BOLT: It’s fixed on the moon, Zappy, not on your school calendar.

Fixed by the moon.

WHY FAST?

Discipline, health, devotion

What fasting is. An upavāsa ranges from skipping grains to a full fast without water. Most fasts allow fruit, milk or one simple meal.

Why. Self-control, a lighter body for prayer, and an offering of effort. The word upavāsa means “sitting near” (God), not starving.

When. Ekādaśī, Pradoṣa, Saṅkaṣṭī, Navarātri and many vrata days. Children, the elderly, the pregnant and the sick are traditionally exempt.

Read more. Why Do We Fast?

ZAPPY: Is fasting compulsory?

PROF. BOLT: No, Zappy. It’s a choice, and every tradition says health comes first.

Sit near God.

MUST I BE VEGETARIAN?

What traditions say

Prized, not universal. Vegetarianism is widely valued, especially among Vaiṣṇavas, many Śaivas and brāhmaṇa communities, because of ahiṃsā, non-harm. Many other Hindu communities eat meat or fish.

On sacred days. Many who eat meat avoid it on festival days, in the month of Śrāvaṇa, on Mondays or Saturdays, or during a vow like Ayyappa’s.

Offered food. Food offered to deities in most temples is vegetarian; some goddess and village shrines have other customs. Traditions differ.

The principle. The Gītā (17.8–10) speaks of food that is fresh, wholesome and moderate; many teachers apply that before anything else.

ZAPPY: So what should I do?

PROF. BOLT: Follow your family’s tradition thoughtfully, Zappy, and eat with gratitude. Ahiṃsā is a direction to walk in.

Eat with care and gratitude.

WHAT ARE THE MARKS ON THE LETTERS?

IAST in one page

Why marks. Sanskrit has more sounds than English letters. IAST (International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration) uses dots and bars so each sound has one spelling. That is why this library writes Kṛṣṇa, not Krishna.

Three quick rules. A bar lengthens a vowel (ā as in father); a dot under a consonant makes it retroflex, tongue curled back (ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ṣ); ś and ṣ both sound like “sh”.

Why not the Devanagari script? So that anyone, in any region, can read the same text; the site keeps to IAST throughout.

Read more. How to Read IAST, a full pronunciation guide.

PROF. BOLT: Five minutes of it, Zappy, and every verse on the site opens up.

WHERE DO I START?

Three simple first steps

Light a lamp. A small lamp, morning or evening, in front of a picture of the deity you feel drawn to. That is a complete act of worship. See A Simple Home Pūjā.

Learn one verse. One short prayer, understood: perhaps Śāntākāram or a Gaṇeśa śloka.

Read one story. A guide a week from this library: the order we suggest is in Start Here: The First Ten Pages.

Keep a festival. Pick the next festival in the calendar and keep it simply. The festivals collection explains each one.

ZAPPY: Just a lamp?

PROF. BOLT: A lamp lit with attention, Zappy. Everything else grows from that.

A lamp, a verse, a story.
