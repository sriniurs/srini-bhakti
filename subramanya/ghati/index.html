Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya

The Twin Images on the Hill · explained and illustrated

Cobra, Narasiṃha and the mirror

HOW THIS WORKS: The name, the god’s penance as a serpent and Narasiṃha’s guard, the image and the mirror, the forest of nāga stones, the history, and the chariot and cattle fair.

WHERE WE ARE

An hour north of Bengaluru, in the dry hills of Doḍḍaballāpura, stands a temple with one of the strangest images in Karnataka. Facing east is Subrahmaṇya, as a seven-hooded cobra. Facing west, on the back, is Lakṣmī Narasiṃha. You see the cobra directly, and Narasiṃha in a mirror.

The story is the theme of Kukke told another way: the son of Śiva took the form of a serpent, and Viṣṇu himself, as Narasiṃha, guarded him from Garuḍa. This last part visits Ghāṭi: the name, the legends, the image and the mirror, the forest of nāga stones, the history, the chariot festival and its famous cattle fair a month after Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī.

THE NAME

Pot, hill or demon?

Ghaṭa sarpa. A popular explanation: the cobra’s spread hood looks like a pot (ghaṭa); the god lived here as a “pot-hooded serpent”.

Ghāṭ. Another: “ghāṭ”, the hill country where the temple stands.

Ghaṭikāsura. A third: the demon Ghaṭikāsura, said to have been killed here.

Full name. Śrī Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya Svāmi Kṣetra; tradition counts it among seven svayaṃvyakta (self-revealed) shrines of Subrahmaṇya.

ZAPPY: Which one is right?

PROF. BOLT: Traditions differ, Zappy. All three tell you something about the place: a serpent, a hill and a victory.

A serpent, a hill, a victory.

THE SERPENT’S PENANCE

Why the god became a cobra

The story

Ghāṭi temple tradition; tellings differ.

Tradition says Subrahmaṇya came to these hills and did penance in the form of a serpent.
Why? In one telling, it was before his war with Tāraka; in another, to atone after the killing in that war.
A local story adds a demon, Ghaṭikāsura, whom the god destroyed here.
While he lived here as a serpent, he learned that the nāgas around him lived in fear of Garuḍa.
He turned to Viṣṇu for help. That is the next page.

ZAPPY: The war-god did penance as a snake?

PROF. BOLT: Even heroes need quiet, Zappy. The hills of Ghāṭi gave him that.

The warrior becomes a serpent.

NARASIṂHA GUARDS THE NĀGAS

Viṣṇu stands behind

The story

Ghāṭi temple tradition.

Subrahmaṇya asked Viṣṇu to keep his mount Garuḍa from harming the serpents.
Viṣṇu came with Lakṣmī as Lakṣmī Narasiṃha and stood guard over him.
Garuḍa could not touch the serpents here; Narasiṃha gave them abhaya, freedom from fear.
Sages (one telling names Mārkaṇḍeya) prayed that both forms stay here together. So the image shows the cobra facing east and Narasiṃha facing west.
The same theme as Kukke: there the son protects the serpent from Garuḍa; at Ghāṭi Viṣṇu himself holds his own eagle back.

ZAPPY: Viṣṇu stopped his own eagle?

PROF. BOLT: For his nephew, Zappy. Family first.

Viṣṇu holds back his own eagle.

THE IMAGE AND THE MIRROR

How you see both gods

East face. Subrahmaṇya as a seven-hooded cobra: the main darśan.

West face. Lakṣmī Narasiṃha, on the back of the same image.

The mirror. A large mirror in the sanctum shows Narasiṃha to devotees standing before Subrahmaṇya, so both are seen at once without walking round.

Svayambhū. Both are held to have risen from the earth, not carved by hands. Most say one stone; some descriptions speak of two images back to back.

ZAPPY: Why a mirror?

PROF. BOLT: Because the two gods face opposite ways, Zappy. The mirror lets you see both at once.

Two gods, one glance.

THE FOREST OF NĀGA STONES

Vows for children

Nāga stones. Thousands of stone serpent images stand near the temple, set up by devotees over generations.

Harake. A vow (harake in Kannaḍa): couples pray here for children and return to fulfil it, very often by installing a nāga stone (nāga pratiṣṭhā).

Ketu. The temple is also a centre of worship for Ketu, the shadow planet linked with serpents.

Sarpa doṣa. As at Kukke, devotees come for relief from troubles believed to follow harm to snakes.

ZAPPY: Every stone is a vow?

PROF. BOLT: Every one, Zappy. And many are a child’s story.

A forest of answered prayers.

IN THE VERSE

Nava Nāga Stotra

The nine great serpents
anantaṃ vāsukiṃ śeṣaṃ padmanābhaṃ ca kambalamśaṅkhapālaṃ dhārtarāṣṭraṃ takṣakaṃ kāliyaṃ tathāetāni nava nāmāni nāgānāṃ ca mahātmanāmsāyaṅkāle paṭhen nityaṃ prātaḥkāle viśeṣataḥtasya viṣabhayaṃ nāsti sarvatra vijayī bhavet
Ananta, Vāsuki, Śeṣa, Padmanābha and Kambala, Śaṅkhapāla, Dhārtarāṣṭra, Takṣaka and Kāliya: these are the nine names of the great serpents. Whoever recites them every evening, and especially in the morning, has no fear of poison and is victorious everywhere.
WordMeaning
anantamAnanta, the endless
vāsukimVāsuki
śeṣamŚeṣa
takṣakamTakṣaka
viṣa-bhayamfear of poison
vijayīvictorious

The serpents’ roll-call. At a shrine where the god himself is a seven-hooded cobra and thousands of nāga stones stand, this verse of the nine great serpents is recited by many pilgrims. Vāsuki of Kukke and Śeṣa of Viṣṇu’s couch are both in it.

Text as on shlokam.org and sanskritdocuments.org (Navanāganāmastotram), as on our Nāga Pañcamī page.
Nine names against fear.

HISTORY

The Ghorpaḍes of Sandūr

Recorded history. More than six hundred years, by temple records.

The Ghorpaḍes. The temple was first developed by the Ghorpaḍes, rulers of the small state of Sandūr, who governed parts of Baḷḷāri, over 300 km away: kings built for gods wherever their faith took them.

Style and priests. A Dravidian-style temple with a gopura; tradition says one family has served as priests for generations.

Today. A major pilgrim centre of the Bengaluru Rural region, busiest on Sundays, Ṣaṣṭhīs and the chariot festival.

ZAPPY: Sandūr is far from Bengaluru, isn’t it?

PROF. BOLT: Over 300 km, Zappy. Devotion travels.

Kings who built far from home.

THE CHARIOT AND THE CATTLE FAIR

Puṣya Ṣaṣṭhī at Ghāṭi

Brahmarathotsava. The great chariot festival falls on Puṣya Śuddha Ṣaṣṭhī, one lunar month after Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī, and the image is drawn round the temple by devotees.

The cattle fair. Around the festival is held one of the best-known cattle fairs of South India, with farmers from Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and beyond buying and selling bullocks under the god’s blessing.

Other days. Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī itself is kept with special worship; Narasiṃha Jayantī is the other great festival, for the god on the west face; Kārtika Pūrṇimā draws crowds too.

The old way. In rural Karnataka the god’s festival was always also the farmers’ market: the jātre brings together faith, trade and family.

ZAPPY: A cattle fair at a temple?

PROF. BOLT: The old way, Zappy. The god’s festival was the farmers’ market; bullocks were bought under his blessing.

Faith and the farmers’ market.

KUKKE AND GHĀṬI

Two serpent shrines side by side

🌳 Setting

Kukke: rainforest of the west coast. Ghāṭi: dry hills near Bengaluru.

🐍 Form

Kukke: Subrahmaṇya above Vāsuki and Śeṣa. Ghāṭi: Subrahmaṇya himself as a seven-hooded cobra.

🛡️ Protector

Kukke: Subrahmaṇya protects Vāsuki from Garuḍa. Ghāṭi: Narasiṃha protects the nāgas.

🚩 Chariot

Kukke: Mārgaśīrṣa Ṣaṣṭhī. Ghāṭi: Puṣya Ṣaṣṭhī, a month later. Shared: nāga vows, sarpa doṣa, and the god of Karnataka’s serpents.

One god, two shrines.
