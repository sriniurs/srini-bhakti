Kukke Subrahmaṇya

The Serpent’s Refuge · explained and illustrated

The serpent’s refuge in the forest

HOW THIS WORKS: The legends of Vāsuki and the wedding, a walk through the sanctum, Ādi Subrahmaṇya and the Kukke liṅga, a verse of the Bhujaṅgam, the nāga rites, and Campā Ṣaṣṭhī’s chariot.

WHERE WE ARE

Deep in the forests of the Western Ghāṭs, where the Kumāradhārā river comes down from Kumāra Parvata, Śiva’s son is worshipped not as a warrior with a spear but as the lord of serpents. Here, the story goes, the great serpent Vāsuki hid in a cave from Garuḍa, and Subrahmaṇya promised to stay with him forever.

So at Kukke the god and the serpent are worshipped together, and families from all over India come for the rites of the nāgas. This part tells the stories, walks through the shrine and its neighbours, explains the rites honestly, gives a verse of the Bhujaṅgam, and describes the festival that makes Kukke the heart of Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī.

ZAPPY: A god worshipped as a snake?

PROF. BOLT: As the snake’s protector, and in the snake, Zappy. At Kukke the two cannot be separated.

ITS NAMES

Kukke, Subrahmaṇya, Subbappa

🛕 Kukke Subrahmaṇya

The temple’s common name; “Kukke” is tied to the old Kukke liṅga worshipped here. The village around it is simply called Subrahmaṇya.

🐍 Ahipeśvara

“Lord of serpents”: an old name of the Kukke liṅga.

🍚 Annadāta Subbappa

“Subbappa the giver of food”: the temple is known for feeding every pilgrim.

🙏 Subbappa, Subbarāya

Affectionate Kannaḍa and Tuḷu names for the god.

ZAPPY: Why is he called the giver of food?

PROF. BOLT: Because nobody leaves Kukke hungry, Zappy. The temple feeds thousands every day.

Many names, one refuge.

VĀSUKI AND GARUḌA

The serpent who hid in a cave

The story

Temple tradition, with the Skanda Purāṇa (Sahyādri Khaṇḍa) cited by the temple; tellings differ.

Garuḍa, the eagle who carries Viṣṇu, was the old enemy of the serpents and hunted them to eat. (Why, is told in our Nāga Pañcamī article.)
Vāsuki, king of serpents and a great devotee of Śiva, hid in a cave called Biladvāra near the Kumāradhārā, deep in prayer.
In one telling Garuḍa found him and attacked, until the sage Kaśyapa, father of both, asked him to spare Śiva’s devotee.
Vāsuki prayed to Śiva for safety. Śiva sent him to his son: “Subrahmaṇya will protect you.”
Subrahmaṇya came, blessed Vāsuki and promised to stay here with him forever. So worship of Vāsuki here is worship of Subrahmaṇya.

ZAPPY: Isn’t the peacock also a snake-eater?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and still it is the snake who gets refuge, Zappy. The god protects even his mount’s prey.

Refuge for the hunted.

THE WEDDING AND THE RIVER

Kumāra Parvata and the Kumāradhārā

The story

Kukke temple tradition.

In this tradition, after killing Tāraka and Śūrapadma, Skanda came with Gaṇeśa to the peak now called Kumāra Parvata.
Indra gave him his daughter Devasenā on Mārgaśīrṣa Śuddha Ṣaṣṭhī, with the gods present.
Waters of all the sacred rivers, brought for the wedding, flowed down as the Kumāradhārā, “the stream of Kumāra”.
Another telling says he washed his vel in this river after the war.
So pilgrims bathe in the Kumāradhārā before entering the temple, and the wedding day is the temple’s great festival. The full war is in Part 2.

ZAPPY: A river made of wedding water?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the story, Zappy. Bathe in it and you’ve been to the wedding.

A river from a wedding.

THE SANCTUM

God above, serpents below

Three tiers. As commonly described: at the top Subrahmaṇya, seated, lord of the shrine; in the middle Vāsuki, king of serpents, whom he protects; at the base Śeṣa (Ādiśeṣa), the serpent Viṣṇu rests on.

The Garuḍa pillar. A silver-covered pillar stands between the sanctum and the entrance. Tradition says it shields devotees from the fiery breath of Vāsuki: Garuḍa cannot touch Vāsuki, and Vāsuki’s breath cannot touch you.

Darśan. Devotees enter from the back (east) gate in many accounts, after the river bath, and walk round to the front. Customs and queue arrangements are set by the temple.

The worship. Daily rites follow Madhvācārya’s Tantrasāra Saṅgraha, the ritual manual of the Mādhva tradition.

ZAPPY: Garuḍa guards the people from the serpent?

PROF. BOLT: Neatly balanced, Zappy. Even the eagle has a job here.

God above, serpents below.

ĀDI SUBRAHMAṆYA AND BILADVĀRA

The anthill and the cave

Ādi Subrahmaṇya. A short walk from the main temple is a separate shrine where an anthill (valmīka) in the sanctum is worshipped every day. “Ādi” means first: it is held to be the original seat of the serpent god.

Biladvāra. “The door of the hole”: the cave where Vāsuki is said to have hidden and prayed.

Kāśikaṭṭe Gaṇapati. An old Gaṇeśa shrine nearby, said in tradition to have been set up by Nārada.

Darpaṇa tīrtha. A stream behind the temple, flowing into the Kumāradhārā.

ZAPPY: Why an anthill?

PROF. BOLT: It’s a cobra’s home, Zappy. At Ādi Subrahmaṇya the home itself is the shrine.

The first seat.

THE KUKKE LIṄGA AND ŚAṄKARA

Father and son in one house

The Kukke liṅga. Before the present image, tradition says, worship centred on the Kukke liṅga, called Ahipeśvara, “lord of serpents”: the son’s name was first written on the father’s stone.

Śaṅkara. Ādi Śaṅkara’s traditional biographies record his visit here, and the Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam is often linked with this shrine (next page).

Two lineages. A Śṛṅgeri maṭha and the Candramaulīśvara shrine stand in the precincts; the Subrahmaṇya Maṭha of the Mādhva tradition is beside the temple. Both traditions have long roots here.

One family. Śiva, Subrahmaṇya, Vāsuki, Gaṇeśa: Kukke is a whole Śaiva household gathered in one forest.

ZAPPY: A Śiva liṅga in Subrahmaṇya’s temple?

PROF. BOLT: Father and son share the house, Zappy.

Father and son share the house.

IN THE VERSE

Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, verse 5

Troubles vanish like waves
yathābdhes taraṅgā layaṃ yānti tuṅgāstathaivāpadaḥ sannidhau sevatāṃ meitīvormipaṅktīr nṛṇāṃ darśayantaṃsadā bhāvaye hṛtsaroje guhaṃ tam
“Just as high waves of the ocean sink to rest, so do the troubles of those who serve me in my presence”: as if to show this to people through the rows of waves, he stands; I always meditate on that Guha in the lotus of my heart.

The serpent metre. The Bhujaṅgam is written in the bhujaṅga-prayāta metre, “the serpent’s glide”: a fitting hymn for Karnataka’s serpent shrine, where devotees come precisely to have their troubles subside. Families often recite it during the rites at Kukke.

Text as on shlokam.org (Subramanya Bhujangam), verse 5.

THE NĀGA RITES

Why families come to Kukke

Sarpa saṃskāra. A two-day rite, like last rites for a serpent, done by those who believe they harmed a snake, in this life or another.

Āśleṣā bali. A rite on the day of the Āśleṣā star, sacred to the serpents.

Nāga pratiṣṭhā. Installing a stone serpent image, often after a vow. Families seek children, health, marriage, and relief from sarpa doṣa, the trouble believed to follow harm to serpents.

A frank note. These rites rest on belief; their rules, timings and fees are set by the temple and should be checked with it directly. What is certain is how old the trust in them is along this coast.

ZAPPY: Do these rites really work?

PROF. BOLT: That’s for the believer, Zappy. What’s certain is how many generations have trusted them.

A coast’s old trust.

CAMPĀ ṢAṢṬHĪ AT KUKKE

The great chariot

When. Mārgaśīrṣa Śuddha Ṣaṣṭhī, the day of the wedding: Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī, also called Campā Ṣaṣṭhī.

The festival. A run of days with processions, small chariots and lit nights, ending in the Brahmaratha, the great chariot, pulled by devotees through the car street.

Other days. Kiru Ṣaṣṭhī, the “small Ṣaṣṭhī”; Nāgara Pañcamī; Nāgārādhane.

Feeding. The busiest days of the year, and pilgrims are fed by the temple, true to the name Annadāta Subbappa.

ZAPPY: How many people pull it?

PROF. BOLT: Thousands, Zappy. Everyone who can get a hand on the rope.

A whole town pulls together.

HISTORY

Priests, maṭhas and the State

Paraśurāma kṣetra. Kukke is counted among the seven holy places of the coast said to be set up by Paraśurāma.

Style. The temple is built in the Karnataka coastal style: sloping tiled roofs, wood and granite.

Stewards and priests. The Koojugodu Kattemane family long served as chief administrators, with close ties to Śṛṅgeri; the priesthood changed hands between Brahmin communities in the nineteenth century, and the Śrī Subrahmaṇya Sabhā was founded in 1908.

Today. The temple is run by the Karnataka Endowment Department and is among the state’s best-known pilgrim centres.

ZAPPY: Why so many groups in one temple’s story?

PROF. BOLT: Big temples gather many hands, Zappy. The god stays the same; the stewards change.

Many hands, one shrine.

AROUND KUKKE

The sacred landscape

Kumāra Parvata. The peak above Kukke, sacred as the place of the wedding; a well-known forest trek.

Kumāradhārā. The river of the wedding waters, where pilgrims bathe.

Garuḍa folklore. Locals say Garuḍa never flies over the temple or the peak, out of respect for Vāsuki’s refuge. (Folklore; we have not verified it.)

Serpent country. The coast of Tuḷunāḍu is full of nāga groves (nāgabana), honoured by every family. Next: Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya, the serpent shrine near Bengaluru.

ZAPPY: Eagles really avoid it?

PROF. BOLT: So the locals say, Zappy. Watch the sky next time and tell me.

Forest, river and serpent groves.
