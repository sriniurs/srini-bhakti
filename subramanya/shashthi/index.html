PART 2 OF 4 · THE VEL · THE PEACOCK AND THE ROOSTER

The Ṣaṣṭhī Story

Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī and Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī · explained and illustrated

The spear and the peacock

HOW THIS WORKS: Which Ṣaṣṭhī is which, why the sixth, the whole war from Tāraka to the mango tree, the wedding at Kumāra Parvata, two verses of Śaṅkara’s Bhujaṅgam, and how Karnataka keeps it.

WHERE WE ARE

Skanda was born for a war. On the sixth day of the bright fortnight, the tradition says, he finished it: the demon king was struck down, and from his body came the peacock Skanda rides and the rooster on his flag. That sixth day, Ṣaṣṭhī, became his.

Tamil Nadu keeps Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī in Aippasi with six days of fasting and the great Sūrasaṃhāram on the beach at Tiruccendūr. A month later Karnataka and the Telugu lands keep Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī in Mārgaśīrṣa, remembering his wedding, with chariots at Kukke and milk at the cobra’s anthill. This part tells the war in full, explains the two Ṣaṣṭhīs, gives two verses from Śaṅkara’s Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, and shows how the day is kept.

ZAPPY: Two Ṣaṣṭhīs for one god?

PROF. BOLT: Two regions, two months, one victory, Zappy. Tamil Nadu remembers the war; Karnataka remembers the wedding and the serpent.

WHICH ṢAṢṬHĪ IS WHICH?

Three names, three months

Monthly. Every bright Ṣaṣṭhī is a small fast for Skanda, all year round.

Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī (Kanda Ṣaṣṭi). In Aippasi (October–November) Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka and Malaysia keep six days: the war on the sixth, the wedding on the seventh.

Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī. In Mārgaśīrṣa (November–December), Karnataka and the Telugu states keep the next Ṣaṣṭhī: almanacs call it the Ṣaṣṭhī after Sūrasaṃhāram. The same day in Maharashtra is Campā Ṣaṣṭhī, ending the six-day festival of Khaṇḍobā at Jejuri, a form of Śiva. Why “Campā”? Often said to be for the campaka flower; explanations differ.

Puṣya Ṣaṣṭhī. The month after, Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya holds its chariot festival.

ZAPPY: So Kukke Ṣaṣṭhī and Jejuri’s Campā Ṣaṣṭhī fall on one day?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, Zappy. Śiva’s son at Kukke, Śiva himself as Khaṇḍobā at Jejuri: one family, one day.

One god, three Ṣaṣṭhīs.

WHY THE SIXTH DAY?

Sixes everywhere

Six mothers. The child born of Śiva’s fire was nursed by the six Kṛttikās, the Pleiades; he grew six faces, one for each mother: Ṣaṇmukha, Kārttikeya. His birth is told in Part 1.

Six syllables, six abodes. His name Śaravaṇabhava, “born in the reed-pond”, has six syllables; Tamil Nadu’s six great shrines are the Āṟupaṭai Vīṭu.

Six days of war. In the Tamil telling the battle with Śūrapadma lasts six days and ends on Ṣaṣṭhī.

Six enemies. A common teaching: the vel destroys the six inner foes (ṣaḍripu): desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride and envy. It is a teaching built on the story, very popular in Ṣaṣṭhī sermons; read it as a meaning, not a fact.

ZAPPY: Is the six-enemies idea in the Purāṇa?

PROF. BOLT: It’s a teaching built on the story, Zappy. Read it as a meaning, not a fact.

Six faces, six days.

THE GENERAL OF THE GODS

A boy takes command

The story

Skanda Purāṇa and Śiva Purāṇa; the Tamil Kanda Purāṇam for the southern telling.

The child of the reeds grew in six days into a young warrior.
The gods made him Devasenāpati, commander of their army. Each gave a gift: Pārvatī the spear, the vel; others weapons, a banner, a mount.
With the gods behind him, Kumāra marched against the demons.
In the Tamil telling, Śūrapadma ruled with his brothers Siṃhamukha, lion-faced, and Tāraka, elephant-faced.
Skanda first sent his commander Vīrabāhu to offer peace. Śūrapadma refused.

ZAPPY: He offered peace first?

PROF. BOLT: Always, Zappy. Even the general of the gods sends a messenger before the spear.

Peace offered before war.

TĀRAKA AND THE KRAUÑCA HILL

The first victory

The story

Purāṇic and Tamil tellings; details vary.

Tāraka fought with every trick of illusion and hid behind the mountain Krauñca, which his ally guarded.
Skanda hurled his vel. It pierced the Krauñca mountain right through and killed Tāraka.
Old texts remember Skanda as Krauñcabhettā, “the piercer of Krauñca”. In some tellings Krauñca is a demon ally of Tāraka.
Brahmā’s boon held: only Śiva’s son could kill Tāraka, and he did.
The Tamil Kanda Purāṇam goes on: Siṃhamukha falls next, and then the king, Śūrapadma.

ZAPPY: The spear went through a mountain?

PROF. BOLT: The vel is knowledge, many teachers say, Zappy: nothing hides from it.

The spear that pierced the mountain.

ŚŪRAPADMA AND THE MANGO TREE

Pride split in two

The story

The Tamil Kanda Purāṇam’s climax.

The war raged for six days. One by one, Śūrapadma’s sons and brothers fell.
On the last day the demon king took one form after another to escape. At last he became a huge mango tree standing in the sea.
Skanda hurled his vel and split the tree in two.
Even then he did not destroy him: one half became the peacock he rides, the other the rooster on his banner.
The enemy is not killed but turned into a servant. Many read it as the ego, split and tamed, carrying the god. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: He spared the worst demon?

PROF. BOLT: He spared what was left after the pride was gone, Zappy. That’s the Ṣaṣṭhī lesson.

The enemy becomes the vehicle.

SŪRASAṂHĀRAM

The war re-enacted on the beach

Where. Tiruccendūr, Murugan’s seashore abode, where he camped for the war, on the evening of Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī, the sixth day of the Aippasi festival.

What. Murugan’s festival image meets effigies of the demon on the beach; the demon keeps changing heads (elephant, lion, his own) and each is “cut off”; finally the tree, and the peacock and rooster.

Next day. Tirukkalyāṇam, Murugan’s wedding with Devasenā.

The fast. Many devotees fast for all six days, some on one meal, some on milk and fruit; others keep it on Ṣaṣṭhī alone. The same rite is kept in Murugan temples across Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka, Malaysia and Singapore.

ZAPPY: The demon changes heads?

PROF. BOLT: Pride keeps changing masks, Zappy. The vel keeps finding it.

The war on the beach.

THE WEDDING AT KUMĀRA PARVATA

Karnataka’s Ṣaṣṭhī

The story

The Kukke Subrahmaṇya temple tradition.

After the victory, in the Karnataka tradition, Skanda came with his brother Gaṇeśa to Kumāra Parvata in the Western Ghāṭs.
There Indra gave him his daughter Devasenā in marriage.
The wedding took place on Mārgaśīrṣa Śuddha Ṣaṣṭhī: Karnataka’s Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī.
Holy waters brought from every river for the wedding flowed together as the Kumāradhārā, the river at Kukke.
Nearby, the serpent king Vāsuki was praying in a cave to escape Garuḍa. Skanda stayed to protect him: that story is Part 3.

ZAPPY: So Karnataka celebrates the wedding?

PROF. BOLT: The wedding and the serpent, Zappy. That’s why milk goes to the anthills on this day.

A wedding in the Ghāṭs.

IN THE VERSE

Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, verse 3

The son of Mahādeva on his peacock
mayūrādhirūḍhaṃ mahāvākyagūḍhaṃmanohāridehaṃ mahaccittagehammahīdevadevaṃ mahāvedabhāvaṃmahādevabālaṃ bhaje lokapālam
I worship the protector of the world, the child of Mahādeva: mounted on the peacock, hidden in the great sayings of the Veda, of captivating form, dwelling in great minds, god of the brahmins, the very meaning of the Veda.
WordMeaning
mayūra-adhirūḍhammounted on the peacock
mahāvākya-gūḍhamhidden in the great Vedic sayings
manohāri-dehamof captivating form
mahat-citta-gehamdwelling in great minds
mahādeva-bālamchild of Mahādeva
loka-pālamprotector of the world

Śaṅkara at Tiruccendūr. Tradition says Ādi Śaṅkara, ill and in pain, came to Tiruccendūr and, seeing Murugan, burst into this hymn in the gliding bhujaṅga (serpent) metre. This verse paints the god of the day: the boy on the peacock that was once a demon. The Bhujaṅgam is widely sung on Ṣaṣṭhī.

Text as on shlokam.org (Subramanya Bhujangam), verse 3; the full hymn is on sanskritdocuments.org.
The child of Śiva on his peacock.

HOW KARNATAKA KEEPS IT

At home and at the anthill

Fast. Many fast till the pūjā, or keep one meal.

The anthill. Milk, turmeric, flowers and a lamp are offered at the cobra’s anthill (huttu) or at nāga stones under a peepal tree: Subrahmaṇya in Karnataka is the protector of serpents (read our Nāga Pañcamī article).

Feeding a boy. In many homes a young bachelor boy (brahmacārī) is fed and honoured as the boy-god Kumāra, and given new clothes.

Temples. Chariot festivals at Kukke Subrahmaṇya and Subrahmaṇya shrines across the state; Mysuru’s Subrahmaṇya temples, such as the one at Bannimaṇṭap, keep the day. It is also a day to fulfil vows made for children, health and freedom from “sarpa doṣa”.

ZAPPY: Why feed a boy?

PROF. BOLT: Kumāra means the boy, Zappy. Feed a boy, feed the god.

Milk at the anthill, food for Kumāra.

ṢAṢṬHĪ AT KUKKE

The Brahmaratha and the Ghāṭs

Campā Ṣaṣṭhī. At Kukke Subrahmaṇya in the Western Ghāṭs, the Ṣaṣṭhī of Mārgaśīrṣa is the great annual festival, with the Brahmaratha, the temple’s great chariot, pulled through the street by thousands.

The days before. Processions, smaller chariots and illuminated nights lead up to it; pilgrims bathe in the Kumāradhārā before darśan.

Vows. Kukke is Karnataka’s great shrine for serpent vows and rites, and many fulfil them at Ṣaṣṭhī.

The full story. Vāsuki, Garuḍa and the cave, the temple and its rites: Part 3, Kukke Subrahmaṇya.

ZAPPY: How big is the chariot?

PROF. BOLT: Big enough that it takes a whole town to move it, Zappy. That’s the point.

A whole town pulls the chariot.

IN THE VERSE

Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, verse 2

I know nothing; the six-faced one shines in me
na jānāmi śabdaṃ na jānāmi cārthaṃna jānāmi padyaṃ na jānāmi gadyamcid ekā ṣaḍāsyā hṛdi dyotate memukhān niḥsarante giraś cāpi citram
I know no words, I know no meanings, I know no verse, I know no prose. Yet a single consciousness with six faces shines in my heart, and wondrous words pour out of my mouth.
WordMeaning

arthammeaning

Humility. The great Śaṅkara says he knows nothing of poetry: the six-faced god speaks through him. It is the perfect verse for a day about pride defeated.

Text as given at mantra.tips and sanskritdocuments.org (Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, verse 2), set in IAST.
Pride gone, the god speaks.

WHAT ṢAṢṬHĪ TEACHES

The vel and the peacock

The boy-god. The strongest of the gods is a boy: courage and clarity, not size, win the war. (Interpretation.)

The vel. His spear pierces mountains and illusions; many read it as discrimination, the sharp knowledge that sees through pride. (Interpretation.)

Mercy. The demon is split, not destroyed: what was pride becomes the vehicle and the flag. The enemy within is tamed and put to work.

Protector. In Karnataka he is above all the protector of serpents and of children: the festival’s anthills and boy-feasts make the god practical.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Ṣaṣṭhī?

PROF. BOLT: Say his name six times, Zappy, once for each face, and feed a hungry child.

Pride tamed, not destroyed.
