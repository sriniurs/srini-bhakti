Who Is Skanda?

The Six-Faced Son of Śiva · explained and illustrated

Born in the reeds

HOW THIS WORKS: His names, his birth in the reeds, his signs, the boy who taught Śiva and lost a race for a fruit, his wives and six abodes, his long history, and why Karnataka sees him as a serpent.

WHERE WE ARE

He is the youngest of the gods and the general of their army. The north knows him as Kārttikeya or Skanda, Tamil Nadu as Murugan, and Karnataka as Subrahmaṇya, very often worshipped in the form of a serpent.

He was born for one job: to kill the demon Tāraka, whom no one but a son of Śiva could defeat. He was born in a bed of reeds, nursed by six star-mothers, given six faces, a spear by his mother and a peacock to ride. This first part meets the god himself: his names, his birth, his signs, the stories of the boy who taught his father and lost a race for a fruit, his two wives, his six abodes, his long history, and why Karnataka sees him in the cobra. Part 2 tells the Ṣaṣṭhī war; Parts 3 and 4 visit Kukke and Ghāṭi.

ZAPPY: Why so many names for one god?

PROF. BOLT: Because almost every region of India loved him in its own way, Zappy. Each name keeps one story.

HIS NAMES

One god, many stories

⚡ Skanda, Kumāra

Skanda, “the one who leaps” or “attacks”: the spurt of Śiva’s energy. Kumāra, “the youth”: he never grows old.

✨ Kārttikeya, Ṣaṇmukha

“Son of the Kṛttikās”, the six star-mothers; Ṣaṇmukha, “six-faced” (Āṟumukham in Tamil); Śaravaṇabhava, “born in the reeds”.

🕳️ Guha, Subrahmaṇya

Guha, “the hidden one”, who lives in the cave of the heart. Subrahmaṇya is often read as “dear to the brāhmaṇas” or “of Brahman”; its meaning is debated.

🌺 Murugan, Subbarāya

Tamil Murugan, “the beautiful”, god of the hills; Vēlan, the spear-bearer. In Karnataka homes, Subbarāya, Subbappa.

ZAPPY: Which name should I use?

PROF. BOLT: Any of them, Zappy. In Karnataka, “Subbarāya” or “Subrahmaṇya Svāmi” is what grandmother says.

Many names, one boy-god.

A DEMON ONLY A CHILD COULD KILL

Why he was born

The story

Śiva Purāṇa and Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava, “the birth of the prince”.

The demon Tāraka won a boon from Brahmā: he could be killed only by a son of Śiva. Śiva, mourning Satī, was lost in meditation and had no wife. Tāraka felt safe.
He drove the gods from heaven. Indra and the gods went to Brahmā, who told them: only Śiva’s son can save you.
The gods sent Kāma, god of love, to wake Śiva. Śiva opened his third eye and burned Kāma to ash (read our Holi article).
Pārvatī won Śiva by her own fierce penance, and they married. This is the story of Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava.
From Śiva came a blaze of energy so fierce that no one could hold it. Where it went next is the story of the birth.

ZAPPY: The whole universe waited for one child?

PROF. BOLT: A very special one, Zappy. The gods needed a hero only Śiva and Pārvatī could give.

The world waits for a child.

BORN IN THE REEDS

Fire, river, lake and six stars

The story

Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata, the Purāṇas, and the Tamil Kanda Purāṇam; tellings differ.

The fire-god Agni took Śiva’s blazing seed, but even he could not bear its heat.
He gave it to the river Gaṅgā, who carried it to a lake of reeds, Śaravaṇa, in the Himālaya.
There it became six shining boys, each on a lotus. In the Tamil Kanda Purāṇam, six sparks from Śiva’s six faces go straight to the lake.
The six Kṛttikās, the stars of the Pleiades, came down and nursed them: so he is Kārttikeya.
Pārvatī came and embraced all six at once, and they became one child with six faces and twelve arms. (Our Navarātri Skandamātā night tells the mother’s side.)

ZAPPY: Why six?

PROF. BOLT: Six mothers, six faces, six days of war, the sixth lunar day, Zappy. With Skanda, everything comes in sixes.

Six mothers, one child.

THE VEL, THE PEACOCK, THE ROOSTER

His three signs

The vel. A spear with a leaf-shaped blade, given by his mother Pārvatī. Tamil devotees read its long shaft and broad tip as knowledge: deep, wide and sharp. The cry “Vetrivel Muruganukku arōharā”, victory to Murugan of the conquering spear, rings at every festival.

The peacock. His mount. In the Tamil telling it is half of the defeated demon Śūrapadma; many read it as pride tamed (read Part 2).

The rooster. On his banner, the other half of Śūrapadma, crowing at dawn.

The snake. Many images show a snake under the peacock’s feet: the peacock eats snakes. Yet in Karnataka the god himself is worshipped as a serpent. That puzzle is the heart of Parts 3 and 4.

ZAPPY: His peacock eats snakes, and he is a snake?

PROF. BOLT: Karnataka holds both, Zappy. Parts 3 and 4 explain how.

Spear, peacock, rooster.

THE TEACHER OF HIS FATHER

Svāminātha

The story

A Tamil temple tradition of Svāmimalai; versions differ.

The boy Skanda asked Brahmā, the creator, what Om (the Praṇava) means. Brahmā could not explain it.
Skanda locked Brahmā up and took over creation himself.
Śiva came to free Brahmā and asked his son: “Then you tell me what Om means.”
Śiva sat as a disciple and the boy whispered the meaning in his ear. So he is Svāminātha, “teacher of the Lord”, at Svāmimalai near Kumbakōṇam.
The point the tradition draws: wisdom does not depend on age.

ZAPPY: A child taught Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: And Śiva was happy to learn, Zappy. That’s the other half of the lesson.

Wisdom does not wait for age.

THE FRUIT OF WISDOM

Round the world, or round your parents?

The story

A widely told South Indian story.

The sage Nārada brought a single golden fruit (a mango, in most tellings) to Śiva and Pārvatī. Who should have it?
“Whoever goes round the world first,” said Śiva. Skanda leapt on his peacock and flew off.
Gaṇeśa simply walked round his parents: “You are my world.” He won the fruit.
Skanda came back, felt cheated, gave up his finery and went to live alone on the hill of Paḻani, as a young renunciant with a staff.
Pārvatī followed: “Paḻam nī”, “you are the fruit.” Folk etymology hears “Paḻani” in it.

ZAPPY: So who was right?

PROF. BOLT: Both, Zappy. Gaṇeśa shows love for parents; Skanda shows that the seeker must leave home. The fruit was the lesson.

You are the fruit.

IN THE VERSE

Subrahmaṇya Bhujaṅgam, verse 4

The god by the sea who carries us across
yadā sannidhānaṃ gatā mānavā mebhavāmbhodhipāraṃ gatās te tadaivaiti vyañjayan sindhutīre ya āstetam īḍe pavitraṃ parāśaktiputram
“When people come into my presence, at that very moment they have crossed the ocean of worldly life”: as if to show this, he stands on the shore of the sea. I praise him, the pure son of the supreme Śakti.

Tiruccendūr. Śaṅkara composed the Bhujaṅgam at Murugan’s seashore temple, and this verse makes the shore itself a sermon: the god stands at the edge of the ocean to show he carries devotees across the ocean of births.

The god on the shore.

DEVASENĀ AND VAḶḶI

Two wives, two worlds

Devasenā. Daughter of Indra (in the Skanda Purāṇa; of Dakṣa in the Mahābhārata). Her name means “the army of the gods”: the general marries his army. In Tamil, Devayānai. Karnataka’s tradition places the wedding near Kukke (Part 3).

Vaḷḷi. Daughter of a hill-tribe chief, who guarded millet fields with a sling. Murugan wooed her disguised as a hunter and an old man, with Gaṇeśa’s help as a wild elephant.

The pair. Many read them as two powers: Devasenā, action (kriyā); Vaḷḷi, desire or love (icchā); the vel, knowledge (jñāna). (Interpretation.)

The bachelor. In many northern traditions Kārttikeya stays a lifelong bachelor, and women traditionally kept out of some of his shrines.

ZAPPY: The general married an army?

PROF. BOLT: Devasenā means exactly that, Zappy. The Purāṇas love a good pun.

Royal and tribal, both his own.

THE SIX ABODES

Āṟupaṭai Vīṭu of Tamil Nadu

💍 Tirupparaṅkuṉṟam and Tiruttaṇi

Near Madurai, his wedding with Devasenā; near Chennai, peace after the war and his marriage to Vaḷḷi.

🌊 Tiruccendūr

On the seashore: victory over Śūrapadma and the great Sūrasaṃhāram of Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī.

⛰️ Paḻani and Svāmimalai

The hill of the fruit, the renunciant boy with a staff; near Kumbakōṇam, the teacher of Om.

🌳 Paḻamudircōlai

Near Madurai, in the forest: Vaḷḷi and Devasenā together. The “six camps” come from Nakkīrar’s Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai, a Tamil pilgrims’ guide-poem well over a thousand years old.

ZAPPY: Six again?

PROF. BOLT: Of course, Zappy. Many read the six as six chapters of his life: wisdom, war, renunciation, teaching, peace, union.

Six homes, six chapters.

HOW OLD IS HE?

From the Vedas to Kuṣāṇa coins

Vedic hints. A bright boy called Kumāra appears in the Ṛgveda; the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka names Ṣaṇmukha; the Chāndogya Upaniṣad calls the sage Sanatkumāra by the name Skanda.

Grammarians and epics. Pāṇini, Patañjali and Kauṭilya’s Arthaśāstra know Skanda; the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata tell his birth, with Agni and Gaṅgā.

Coins. Kuṣāṇa and Yaudheya coins of the first and second centuries CE show him with spear and peacock; six-faced images are firmly established after about the sixth century.

The South and the Purāṇa. The Tamil Sangam poems make Murugan the god of the hills; the Skanda Purāṇa, the largest Purāṇa, carries his name; Kālidāsa’s Kumārasambhava (about the fifth century) tells his birth.

ZAPPY: He’s on old coins?

PROF. BOLT: Over 1,800 years old, Zappy, spear and peacock and all.

Two thousand years of the boy-god.

SUBRAHMAṆYA IN KARNATAKA

The god who is a serpent

Serpent form. Along the west coast and across Karnataka, Subrahmaṇya is worshipped as a nāga, a cobra, often seven-hooded.

Anthills and stones. The anthill (huttu), the cobra’s home, is worshipped as his shrine; stone serpent images (nāgara kallu) stand under trees, installed after vows, often for children.

Why? An old serpent worship of the coast and the Malnāḍ joined the cult of Skanda. Scholars and traditions differ on how; many point to the Ṣaṣṭhī and Pañcamī days that both share.

Great shrines and days. Kukke Subrahmaṇya (Part 3) and Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya (Part 4); Nāgara Pañcamī and Subrahmaṇya Ṣaṣṭhī.

ZAPPY: So in Karnataka, the snake is the god?

PROF. BOLT: The god in the snake, Zappy. You’ll see it clearly at Kukke.

The war-god as a cobra.
