TEMPLES OF ANDHRA PRADESH · OVERVIEW · ARCHITECTURE

Temples of the Telugu Country

How Andhra temples are built · explained and illustrated

Hills, rivers, the sea and the gods

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt read the temples of Andhra: the hill shrines, the four great building traditions, the songs and festivals, and the parts every temple in this series shares.

WHERE WE ARE

Andhra Pradesh stretches from the Eastern Ghats to the Bay of Bengal, cut by two great rivers, the Godāvarī and the Kṛṣṇā. Its temples sit on hilltops, on riverbanks and by the sea.

Over fifteen centuries many dynasties built here, and each left its style: the Eastern Chalukyas, the Kākatīyas, the Eastern Gaṅgas of Kaliṅga and the Vijayanagara emperors. Some temples even show the curved northern tower of Odisha.

This overview introduces that variety once, so that each temple in the series can tell its own story. The Telugu tradition is shared with Telangana, and we mention its great temples too.

ZAPPY: Is an Andhra temple different from a Tamil one?

PROF. BOLT: They share a lot: sanctum, halls, gopurams, Āgama worship. But the hills, the history and the songs give Andhra temples their own character.

ZAPPY: Why so many hill temples?

PROF. BOLT: The Eastern Ghats rise across the state. Hills are natural homes for gods: high, apart and hard to reach, so the journey is part of the worship.

THREE LANDSCAPES

Hills, rivers and the coast

The story

The hills. The Eastern Ghats carry the great hill shrines: Tirumala on the Seven Hills, Srisailam in the Nallamala forest, Ahobilam, Simhachalam above Visakhapatnam, Annavaram on Ratnagiri.
The rivers. The Kṛṣṇā and Godāvarī deltas are dense with temples: Kanaka Durgā above the Kṛṣṇā at Vijayawada, the Pañcārāma Śiva temples of the Godāvarī delta, Draksharamam among them.
The coast. In the north-east, near Odisha, temples such as Srikurmam, Arasavalli and Simhachalam show links with Kaliṅga architecture.
The south-west. In the Rāyalaseema region, Vijayanagara left fine temples: Lepakshi, Tadipatri, and the gopurams of the Tirupati area.
Reading the map. The series follows this map: hill, river, coast and plateau each give a different kind of pilgrimage.

ZAPPY: Is Mantralayam a temple?

PROF. BOLT: It is the shrine of a saint, Rāghavendra, by the Tuṅgabhadrā. It is one of the most visited holy places in the state, so it belongs in this series.

Hills, rivers, coast, plateau: four kinds of pilgrimage.

THE PAÑCĀRĀMAS

Eastern Chalukyas and Śiva in the Godāvarī delta

The dynasty. The Eastern Chalukyas of Vengī ruled the Kṛṣṇā–Godāvarī region from the 7th to the 11th centuries. They and their successors built many Śiva temples in the delta.

Pañcārāma. Five Śiva temples form the Pañcārāma Kṣetras: Amaravati, Draksharamam, Samalkot, Palakollu and Bhimavaram. The legend says the liṅga of the demon Tārakāsura broke into five pieces that fell at these places.

Two storeys. Draksharamam and Samalkot share an unusual feature: a two-storeyed sanctum, with the tall liṅga rising through both floors.

Draksharamam. Draksharamam is also a Śakti Pīṭha of Māṇikyāmbā; its guide is on this site and is Part 5 of this series.

Read it. Look for plain early walls, inscriptions in Telugu and Sanskrit, and later Vijayanagara or Nāyaka additions around them.

ZAPPY: Five pieces of one liṅga?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and pilgrims try to visit all five, often in one trip on a Monday in the month of Kārttika.

THE KĀKATĪYAS

Star plans and floating bricks

Kākatīyas. From Warangal in present-day Telangana, the Kākatīyas ruled much of the Telugu country in the 12th–13th centuries.

Ramappa. Their Rudreśvara (Ramappa) temple at Palampet, begun in 1213, is a UNESCO World Heritage site, famous for its floating bricks, sandbox foundation and black stone bracket figures (How to Read Part 6).

Thousand Pillar temple. At Hanamkonda the Thousand Pillar temple has three shrines round a pillared hall on a star-shaped plan.

Toraṇas. The great carved stone gateways of Warangal Fort became a symbol of the region, on the emblem of Telangana.

In Andhra too. Kākatīya influence spread into Andhra, seen in temples and inscriptions along the Kṛṣṇā.

ZAPPY: Why are these in an Andhra series?

PROF. BOLT: Because the Telugu country is one cultural region, now in two states. Knowing the Kākatīyas helps you read many Andhra temples.

KALIṄGA TOUCHES IN THE NORTH-EAST

Curved towers near Odisha

Neighbours. The north-eastern districts border Odisha, and for centuries were ruled by the Eastern Gaṅgas of Kaliṅga.

Simhachalam. The Simhachalam temple near Visakhapatnam was rebuilt in the 13th century under the Eastern Gaṅga king Narasiṃhadeva I, the builder of Konārak, and shows Kaliṅga features (Part 6).

Srikurmam and Arasavalli. Near Srikakulam, the Kūrma temple of Viṣṇu as the tortoise and the Sūrya temple at Arasavalli are famous old shrines of this region.

How to tell. Look for a curved tower (rekha deul) and a hall with a stepped pyramid roof (pīḍha deul), as in Odisha (How to Read Part 4).

A meeting point. So Andhra is where the northern and southern temple traditions meet, side by side, as at Pattadakal in Karnataka.

ZAPPY: Northern towers in the South?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Building styles follow rulers and craftsmen, not modern state borders.

VIJAYANAGARA

Gopurams, halls and painted ceilings

An empire. From its capital at Hampi, the Vijayanagara empire (14th–16th centuries) ruled the whole south, including Andhra. Its greatest emperor, Kṛṣṇadevarāya, was a Telugu poet as well as a king.

Gopurams. Vijayanagara rulers added tall gateway towers, called rāyagopurams, to temples across Andhra, including at Tirumala, Srisailam and Srikalahasti.

Kalyāṇa maṇḍapams. They built pillared wedding halls with rearing horses and yāḷis on the pillars, for the divine wedding festivals.

Lepakshi. At Lepakshi, two brothers serving the empire built a temple famous for its painted ceilings, huge Nandi and hanging pillar (Part 10).

Tirumala’s patron. Kṛṣṇadevarāya visited Tirumala several times and gave rich gifts; copper statues of him and his queens stand in the temple.

ZAPPY: Did the empire’s fall end temple building?

PROF. BOLT: No. Later Nāyakas, local chiefs, temple boards and devotees went on building and renewing, as they still do.

THE HILL TEMPLE

How a hill shrine works

Two ways up. Most hill temples have a stepped footpath for pilgrims on foot and a ghat road for buses and cars.

Walking as worship. Many pilgrims climb on foot to fulfil vows, stopping at shrines and shelters on the way. At Tirumala, thousands climb the Alipiri path every day.

Small towns. At the top, a small town grows around the temple: lodges, kitchens, choultries, shops, offices and queue complexes.

At the foot. Many hill temples have a related temple at the foot of the hill, like Tiruchanur for Tirumala, visited before or after.

Tonsure. Offering one’s hair is a common vow at Andhra hill temples, at large tonsure halls called kalyāṇakaṭṭa.

ZAPPY: Why offer hair?

PROF. BOLT: It is a gift of pride and beauty, given to the god in thanks or in hope. At Tirumala it is one of the most common vows.

HOW WORSHIP IS DONE

Āgamas, sevās and prasādam

Āgamas. Viṣṇu temples follow the Vaikhānasa or Pāñcarātra Āgamas; Tirumala follows Vaikhānasa. Śiva temples follow the Śaiva Āgamas.

Sevās. Andhra temples offer many sevās, rituals that devotees can sponsor: the Suprabhātam awakening, the Kalyāṇotsavam wedding, abhiṣekam, the Ūñjal swing, the night Ekānta sevā.

Kalyāṇam. The divine wedding, Kalyāṇotsavam, is especially loved in Andhra temples: devotee couples sponsor it and watch it performed.

Prasādam. Each temple has its famous prasādam: Tirumala’s laddu, Annavaram’s wheat prasādam, Simhachalam’s sandal paste.

Administration. The Andhra Pradesh Endowments Department supervises most temples; Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams (TTD) runs Tirumala and many other temples.

ZAPPY: Do I need a ticket?

PROF. BOLT: For free darśana, no; for special queues and sevās, yes, often booked online in advance. Rules change, so check official sites.

SONGS AND DANCE

Annamācārya, Tyāgarāja and Kūcipūḍi

Annamācārya. Tāḷḷapāka Annamācārya (15th century) sang thousands of Telugu songs, kīrtanas, to Veṅkaṭeśvara of Tirumala. They were engraved on copper plates by his family and are sung widely today.

Tyāgarāja and others. Tyāgarāja (1767–1847), though he lived in the Tamil country, composed in Telugu; Telugu is the main language of Carnatic music. Bhadrācala Rāmadāsu sang of Rāma at Bhadrachalam.

In the temple. Songs are part of worship: the Suprabhātam at dawn, the kīrtanas of Annamācārya at sevās, bhajans of pilgrims on the hill paths.

Kūcipūḍi. The classical dance Kūcipūḍi takes its name from a village in the Kṛṣṇā district. It grew from devotional dance-drama.

Read the sound. When you visit, listen as much as you look: the songs tell you which god is here and how people love him.

ZAPPY: Thousands of songs?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition says around thirty-two thousand. Many survive on the copper plates found at Tirumala.

THE FESTIVAL YEAR

Brahmotsavam, Dasara, Śivarātri and more

Brahmotsavam. The great annual festival of Viṣṇu temples, best known at Tirumala, with a different vehicle each day and the Garuḍa sevā.

Dasara. The Navarātri festival of the goddess, especially grand at Vijayawada’s Kanaka Durgā temple, with a new alaṅkāram each day.

Mahāśivarātri. The great night of Śiva at Srisailam, Srikalahasti and the Pañcārāma temples.

Kārttika. The month of Kārttika (October–November) brings lamps, Monday worship of Śiva and many Satyanārāyaṇa vratas at Annavaram.

Local festivals. Simhachalam’s Chandanotsavam, Mantralayam’s Ārādhanā and many village jātaras fill the year.

ZAPPY: Which is the biggest?

PROF. BOLT: By numbers, Tirumala’s Brahmotsavam and Vijayawada’s Dasara. But every village has its own “biggest” festival.

OLD AND NEW TOGETHER

Renewal and rebuilding

Living temples. Andhra temples are constantly renewed: towers repainted, halls added, queue complexes built for growing crowds.

Srikalahasti. In 2010 the old Vijayanagara rājagopuram of Srikalahasti collapsed. It was rebuilt in the following years, a reminder that temples need care (guide on this site).

Saṃprokṣaṇam. Consecration after renovation, called saṃprokṣaṇam or kumbhābhiṣekam, is held at intervals.

Protection. Some temples, like Lepakshi, are also protected monuments of the Archaeological Survey of India.

Read the layers. As in How to Read Part 11, look for the old core, later halls and gopurams, and recent buildings.

ZAPPY: Is new building a problem?

PROF. BOLT: Not in itself. Temples must serve today’s pilgrims. The care lies in keeping the old evidence safe while doing so.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical and respectful
Plan. For Tirumala and other busy temples, book darśana and rooms online in advance through the official sites.
Dress. Many temples, including Tirumala, require traditional dress for some darśanas: dhoti or kurta-pyjama for men, sari or churidar for women.
Footwear and phones. Leave footwear outside; at some temples phones and cameras must be deposited.
Queues. Expect long queues at weekends and festivals. Carry water and patience.
Prasādam. Buy prasādam only at official counters, and keep the temples clean.

ZAPPY: Any tip for a first visit?

PROF. BOLT: Start with one hill temple and one river temple. You will feel the difference between them.
