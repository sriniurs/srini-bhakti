TEMPLES OF ANDHRA PRADESH · PART 10 OF 10 · VIJAYANAGARA

Lepakshi Vīrabhadra

Paintings, a hanging pillar and a great Nandi · explained and illustrated

A temple on a tortoise rock

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt explore Lepakshi: a giant Nandi, a seven-hooded Nāga-liṅga, a pillar that hangs above the floor, an unfinished hall and some of the finest painted ceilings in India.

WHERE WE ARE

In the dry country of the Sri Sathya Sai district, near the Karnataka border, a long low rock rises from the plain. On it stands the temple of Vīrabhadra at Lepakshi.

It was built in the 16th century, at the height of the Vijayanagara empire, by two brothers, Virūpaṇṇa and Vīraṇṇa, who served the empire as governors.

Lepakshi is a protected monument of the Archaeological Survey of India and a living temple. It is the last stop in our Andhra series, and the most artistic.

ZAPPY: Who is Vīrabhadra?

PROF. BOLT: A fierce form of Śiva, born from his anger to destroy Dakṣa’s sacrifice after Satī’s death. He is a favourite god in the Telugu and Kannada lands.

ZAPPY: Why “tortoise rock”?

PROF. BOLT: The rock is shaped like a tortoise’s shell; it is called Kūrma śaila, linking it with Viṣṇu’s tortoise avatāra.

THE BROTHERS WHO BUILT IT

Virūpaṇṇa and Vīraṇṇa

The story

Governors. In the 1530s, under the Vijayanagara emperor Acyutadevarāya, the brothers Virūpaṇṇa and Vīraṇṇa were governors of the Penukonda region.
The temple. They built the Vīrabhadra temple on the rock at Lepakshi, with halls, shrines, sculpture and paintings of extraordinary quality.
The treasury. A well-known legend says Virūpaṇṇa used state funds for the temple while the king was away. Accused of misuse, he punished himself by blinding his own eyes.
The marks. Guides show two reddish marks on a wall near the unfinished hall, said to be where his eyes struck.
Unfinished. The legend explains why the wedding hall was never finished. Historians treat it as a story; the hall’s incompleteness is real.

ZAPPY: Did that really happen?

PROF. BOLT: It is a legend told for centuries. We cannot prove it; but it shows how people remember the cost of building such a temple.

Lepakshi: built by two brothers, remembered with a sad legend.

THE GREAT NANDI

One of India’s largest

Where. About a few hundred metres from the temple, on the road into the village, lies the Nandi, Śiva’s bull, carved from a single block of granite.

Size. It is about 4.5 m high and 8 m long, one of the largest monolithic Nandis in India.

Details. It wears carved necklaces, bells and chains, and its head is raised and turned, as if alert.

Facing. It faces the temple and, tradition says, the Nāga-liṅga within the enclosure.

Photograph. It stands in a garden, open to view; one of the most photographed sculptures in Andhra.

ZAPPY: Why is it so far from the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Nobody knows for certain. It faces the temple from the entrance, so devotees pass it first as they arrive.

THE SEVEN-HOODED NĀGA-LIṄGA

A serpent shelters Śiva

Carved from the rock. Behind the main shrine, in the open enclosure, a huge liṅga is carved from the rock itself, sheltered by the coils and seven hoods of a serpent.

A story. A popular story says sculptors carved it in the time it took their mother to cook a meal; amazed, she cast an evil eye on it, and a crack appeared. It is a local tale.

Among the largest. It is one of the largest Nāga-liṅgas in India.

Worship. It is worshipped, with offerings, as part of the temple.

Read it. The serpent protects Śiva, as Ādiśeṣa protects Viṣṇu: a sign of shelter and power.

ZAPPY: Is the crack real?

PROF. BOLT: There is a visible crack in the rock. The story explains it in a homely way.

THE HANGING PILLAR

A pillar that does not touch the floor

The puzzle. In the main hall, one of the many pillars does not rest fully on the floor. Visitors pass a thin cloth or paper under one corner of its base.

A tradition. The story says a British engineer in the colonial period tried to find how the roof was supported and shifted the pillar, which then lost its full contact with the floor.

Engineering. The roof beams and the other pillars carry the load. The hanging pillar shows how carefully the structure was balanced.

Belief. Some devotees believe passing a cloth under the pillar brings good fortune.

Care. Please follow the guides’ and ASI’s rules; the pillar and its base are delicate.

ZAPPY: So the pillar holds nothing?

PROF. BOLT: It shares less load than the others. The hall stands because the whole frame works together, a lesson in stone engineering.

THE HALLS AND THEIR PILLARS

Dancers, musicians and gods

The nāṭya maṇḍapa. The front hall, the dance hall, has pillars carved with life-size figures: dancers, drummers, musicians and gods.

Śiva dancing. Many panels show Śiva dancing, Brahmā playing the drum, and celestial musicians: the temple as a stage for divine music.

Textile patterns. The carved clothing is so detailed that textile designers have studied it; “Lepakshi” designs inspired a handicrafts brand in Andhra.

Many shrines. Beyond the halls are shrines of Vīrabhadra, Śiva as Pāpanāśeśvara, Viṣṇu as Raghunātha, and Durgā.

Read it. Walk slowly round each pillar; every face has something new.

ZAPPY: Why so much dance?

PROF. BOLT: Vijayanagara temples loved music and dance. The hall itself celebrates them in stone.

THE PAINTED CEILINGS

Vijayanagara colour

Among the finest. The ceilings of the halls carry some of the best surviving Vijayanagara paintings anywhere (How to Read Part 9).

A giant Vīrabhadra. Over the inner hall, a huge painting of Vīrabhadra fills the ceiling; it is among the largest single figures in Indian mural painting.

Stories. Long strips show scenes from the epics and Purāṇas, Śiva’s stories, and the temple’s patrons in worship.

Style. Figures in profile with large eyes, sharp black outlines, flat earth colours and richly patterned clothes.

Look carefully. The light is dim; give your eyes time, and never use flash.

ZAPPY: Are the paintings original?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, from the 16th century, though faded and damaged in places. Conservation work continues.

THE UNFINISHED WEDDING HALL

Pillars without a roof

The kalyāṇa maṇḍapa. Behind the main temple stands the wedding hall, meant for the divine wedding of Śiva and Pārvatī.

Unfinished. It was never completed: tall carved pillars stand open to the sky, with no roof.

Wedding guests. The pillars are carved with the gods and sages who came to the wedding, as if waiting for the ceremony.

The legend. Tradition links its incompleteness with Virūpaṇṇa’s fall (page “The brothers who built it”).

A beautiful ruin. Many visitors find this open hall the most moving part of Lepakshi.

ZAPPY: Will it ever be finished?

PROF. BOLT: It is a protected monument and will be conserved as it is. Its incompleteness is now part of its story.

JAṬĀYU AND THE NAME

“Rise, bird!”

The Rāmāyaṇa. In the Rāmāyaṇa, the vulture king Jaṭāyu fought Rāvaṇa as he carried Sītā away, and fell wounded.

At Lepakshi. Local tradition says Jaṭāyu fell here. Rāma, finding him, said in Telugu “Lē pakṣi”, “rise, bird!”, and the place took the name.

Sītā’s footprint. In the temple enclosure, a large footprint in the rock is shown as Sītā’s, always kept wet by a spring, tradition says.

Jaṭāyu park. A modern sculpture of Jaṭāyu stands on a rock near the village.

Two layers. So Lepakshi has two stories: the epic and the empire. Visitors meet both.

ZAPPY: Is the name really from “lē pakṣi”?

PROF. BOLT: That is the popular explanation. Place names often gather stories; this one is loved.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Lepakshi is about 15 km from Hindupur and about 120 km from Bengaluru, an easy day trip by road.
Timings. Open from morning to evening; worship continues in the shrines.
Guides. Local guides explain the hanging pillar, paintings and legends; agree a fee first.
Photography. Allowed in the halls without flash; follow ASI rules near the paintings.
Combine. With Penukonda fort, Puttaparthi or a return via Bengaluru.

ZAPPY: Best time to go?

PROF. BOLT: Winter mornings: cool stone, good light for the sculptures, fewer crowds.
