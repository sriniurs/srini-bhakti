TEMPLES OF ANDHRA PRADESH · PART 9 OF 10 · THE TUṄGABHADRĀ

Mantralayam Rāghavendra

The saint’s brindāvana · explained and illustrated

Where Rāyaru lives on

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt visit Mantralayam on the Tuṅgabhadrā, where the saint Rāghavendra Tīrtha entered his brindāvana in 1671 and is still believed to bless devotees who come to him.

WHERE WE ARE

On the bank of the Tuṅgabhadrā river, in the Kurnool district near the Karnataka border, lies the village of Mantralayam.

Here is the brindāvana of Śrī Rāghavendra Tīrtha (1595–1671), a saint and scholar of the Mādhva tradition, whom devotees lovingly call Rāyaru. They believe he is still present in his brindāvana, blessing those who pray to him.

It is not a temple of a god in the usual sense, but the shrine of a saint, and one of the most visited holy places of south India.

ZAPPY: What is a brindāvana?

PROF. BOLT: A shrine built over the place where a saint’s body rests, usually topped with a tulasī (holy basil) plant. Devotees worship it as the saint’s living presence.

ZAPPY: Rāyaru?

PROF. BOLT: “The revered one” in Kannada and Telugu: an affectionate name for Rāghavendra used by devotees.

THE LIFE OF RĀGHAVENDRA

From Veṅkaṭanātha to Rāyaru

The story

Born in 1595. He was born Veṅkaṭanātha in Bhuvanagiri in the Tamil country, into a family of scholars and musicians. He was a fine vīṇā player.
Student. He studied under Sudhīndra Tīrtha of the Śrī Maṭha at Kumbakonam, and became a brilliant scholar of Mādhva philosophy.
Renunciation. In 1621 he took sannyāsa, receiving the name Rāghavendra Tīrtha, and became head of the maṭha.
Travels. For fifty years he travelled through the south, teaching, writing and, devotees believe, healing and helping people in trouble.
The brindāvana. In 1671, at Mantralayam, he entered a brindāvana alive, telling his disciples he would remain there for centuries, blessing devotees.

ZAPPY: He entered it alive?

PROF. BOLT: That is the tradition: he sat in meditation and the brindāvana was closed over him. Devotees believe he is still present.

Rāghavendra: scholar, saint, still present at Mantralayam.

THE SCHOLAR

Books that are still read

Dvaita. Rāghavendra belonged to the Dvaita school of Vedānta founded by Madhvācārya (13th century), which teaches that God and the individual soul are eternally distinct, and that liberation comes by God’s grace through devotion.

Parimala. His most famous work is the Parimala, a commentary on Jayatīrtha’s Nyāya Sudhā; it earned him the title Parimalācārya.

Many works. He wrote dozens of works: commentaries on the Upaniṣads, the Brahma Sūtras and the Gītā, and short devotional pieces.

Music. A gifted vīṇā player, he is honoured by musicians, and devotional songs in Kannada and Telugu praise him.

Read him today. His works are still studied in Mādhva maṭhas and schools. Mantralayam has a Sanskrit school too.

ZAPPY: Is he only for Mādhvas?

PROF. BOLT: His devotees come from every community and region. People pray to Rāyaru for health, work, family, and peace.

THE BRINDĀVANA

How the shrine works

The shrine. The brindāvana stands in the central shrine of the maṭha, covered in silver, with the tulasī plant on top.

Daily worship. Priests perform pūjā through the day, with abhiṣekam, alaṅkāram and offerings, as for a deity.

Pradakṣiṇā. Devotees walk round the brindāvana many times; some make vows to complete a set number of circuits.

Hastodaka. The midday offering of food to the saint, after the worship of God, is important; devotees then receive the prasādam.

Other brindāvanas. The maṭha also has brindāvanas of other saints of the lineage, and a shrine of Mūla Rāma, the ancient image worshipped by the maṭha.

ZAPPY: Why tulasī?

PROF. BOLT: Tulasī is sacred to Viṣṇu. In the Mādhva tradition, saints’ brindāvanas are topped with it as a sign of their devotion.

THE ĀRĀDHANĀ

Three days in August

When. The Ārādhanā marks the day Rāghavendra entered the brindāvana. It is held over three days in Śrāvaṇa (usually August): pūrva, madhya and uttara ārādhanā.

Celebrations. The maṭha holds special worship, processions with the saint’s image on chariots and vehicles, music and discourses.

Everywhere. Ārādhanā is celebrated at Rāghavendra maṭhas and temples across India and abroad, but Mantralayam is the centre.

Music. Many musicians offer concerts in his honour, recalling his own love of the vīṇā.

Thursdays. Every Thursday (Guruvāra), considered the guru’s day, is busy at Mantralayam.

ZAPPY: Why Thursday?

PROF. BOLT: Thursday is Guruvāra, the day of the guru, and Rāyaru is the guru for millions.

A FAMOUS VERSE

“Pūjyāya Rāghavendrāya”

The verse. Devotees recite a short verse, beginning “Pūjyāya Rāghavendrāya satyadharmaratāya ca”, attributed to Appaṇṇācārya, a disciple of Rāghavendra.

Its meaning. Our rendering: “To the worthy Rāghavendra, devoted to truth and dharma; to the one who grants wishes to those who worship him, like the wish-fulfilling tree and the wish-fulfilling cow: our salutations.”

The story. Tradition says Appaṇṇācārya was hurrying to Mantralayam when he heard Rāghavendra had entered the brindāvana, and composed the praise on the way.

Recited daily. The verse is recited daily by devotees in homes and maṭhas across the south.

A small text. Like many beloved prayers, it is short enough for anyone to learn.

ZAPPY: Is the whole verse longer?

PROF. BOLT: The full stotra, the Śrī Rāghavendra Stotram, is longer; the first verse is the one most people know.

THE MUNRO STORY

A British officer and the saint

The setting. Around 1800 the British administration was reviewing grants of land, including the village of Mantralayam held by the maṭha.

The officer. Sir Thomas Munro, then Collector of Bellary, came to inquire. Tradition says he removed his hat and shoes, entered, and spoke with Rāghavendra, whom only he could see.

The decision. The land grant was confirmed. The incident is recorded in tradition and mentioned in an old Madras district gazetteer.

How to read it. For devotees it is a sign of the saint’s living presence; for historians, a striking story preserved in records and memory.

Remembered. The story is retold in the maṭha’s literature and is part of Mantralayam’s identity.

ZAPPY: Is the Munro story true?

PROF. BOLT: It is the maṭha’s tradition and appears in old records. We give it as tradition; its meaning for devotees is what matters most.

AROUND MANTRALAYAM

Mañcālamma, Pañcamukhi and the river

Mañcālamma. The village goddess Mañcālamma has a shrine beside the maṭha. Tradition says Rāghavendra himself asked devotees to visit her first.

The river bath. Pilgrims bathe in the Tuṅgabhadrā before visiting the brindāvana, at ghats near the maṭha.

Pañcamukhi Āñjaneya. A short distance away is a shrine of Hanumān with five faces, where Rāghavendra is said to have meditated.

Bichali. Across the river, Bichali is linked with Rāghavendra’s disciple Appaṇṇācārya and has a small shrine.

Village life. Mantralayam is still a village, with lodges, maṭha kitchens and small shops around the shrine.

ZAPPY: Why visit the village goddess first?

PROF. BOLT: The village belonged to her. It is a sign of humility and of respect for local traditions.

THE MAṬHA TODAY

Food, learning and care

Annadāna. The maṭha serves free meals to pilgrims every day, a tradition of feeding all who come.

Learning. It runs a Sanskrit school and supports scholars of Mādhva philosophy.

Lineage. The maṭha is led by a line of pontiffs in Rāghavendra’s succession, who travel, teach and lead worship.

Services. Rooms, cloakrooms and queue systems serve the crowds; booking is available through official channels.

A living tradition. So Mantralayam is both a shrine and an active institution of learning and service.

ZAPPY: Is food given to everyone?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, as a rule, pilgrims of any background can eat at the free meal. Follow the hall’s customs.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Mantralayam Road railway station is about 15 km away; Kurnool and Raichur are the nearest larger towns; Bengaluru and Hyderabad are long road journeys away.
Timings. The shrine opens early and stays open till night, with pūjā times throughout the day.
Dress. Traditional dress is expected inside: men in dhoti, often without shirt in the inner shrine; women in sari or traditional dress.
Order. River bath, Mañcālamma, then the brindāvana; then Pañcamukhi if time allows.
Busy times. Thursdays, weekends and the Ārādhanā are very crowded.

ZAPPY: Can I stay overnight?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, the maṭha and private lodges offer rooms. Staying lets you attend the early-morning worship.
