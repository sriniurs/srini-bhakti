TEMPLES OF ANDHRA PRADESH · PART 6 OF 10 · THE LION HILL

Simhachalam Varāha-Narasiṃha

The god under sandal paste · explained and illustrated

Hidden in sandal, revealed once a year

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt climb the Lion Hill near Visakhapatnam to meet a god who is hidden in sandal paste all year and shown in his true form on just one day, and read a temple built in the style of Kaliṅga.

WHERE WE ARE

Above the port city of Visakhapatnam rises a forested hill called Siṃhācalam, the “Lion Hill”. On it stands the temple of Varāha-Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha.

The god here combines two avatāras of Viṣṇu: Varāha, the boar, and Narasiṃha, the man-lion. For almost the whole year he is covered in a thick layer of sandal paste and looks like a liṅga-shaped mound.

On one day a year, at the Chandanotsavam, the paste is removed and devotees see the god in his true form, the nijarūpa.

ZAPPY: Why cover a god in sandal?

PROF. BOLT: Narasiṃha is a fierce, fiery god. Sandal cools. Devotees say the paste calms his heat for the good of the world.

ZAPPY: Varāha and Narasiṃha together?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, a rare combined form: a boar’s face, a lion’s tail and a human body, as tradition describes the true form.

PRAHLĀDA AND THE HILL

The temple’s story

The story

The demon king. Hiraṇyakaśipu, brother of the demon Hiraṇyākṣa whom Varāha had killed, hated Viṣṇu. His son Prahlāda was Viṣṇu’s devoted worshipper.
Thrown from the hill. The temple’s story says the king had Prahlāda thrown from this hill into the sea, with the hill on top of him. Viṣṇu came and saved him.
Two avatāras. Prahlāda asked to see the god as both Varāha, who had killed his uncle, and Narasiṃha, who would kill his father. Viṣṇu appeared in a combined form.
Lost and found. Prahlāda built the first temple. Ages later it was buried in an anthill, and King Purūravas found the image, guided by a heavenly voice.
Covered in sandal. The voice told him to cover the god in sandal paste and uncover him only once a year. That is still done today.

ZAPPY: So the paste is a promise?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, kept for centuries: covered all year, uncovered for one day.

Prahlāda’s god: Varāha and Narasiṃha in one.

THE SANDAL PASTE

Twelve months of cool

Layers. The god is covered with sandal paste in layers, applied on set days through the year, so that he looks like a large smooth mound.

Grinding. The paste is ground by hand from sandalwood, in large quantities, by temple servants and volunteers.

Four applications. The paste is applied on four main occasions in the year, each adding to the covering, starting from the evening of Chandanotsavam.

Prasādam. Small portions of the sandal paste are given to devotees as prasādam, valued as blessed and cooling.

What you see. On ordinary days you see the covered god, decorated with flowers and jewels over the sandal.

ZAPPY: Does the god look like a liṅga?

PROF. BOLT: When covered, the outline is rounded, so visitors sometimes say so. But he is Narasiṃha, worshipped in the Vaiṣṇava way.

CHANDANOTSAVAM

The day of the true form

When. Chandanotsavam falls on Akṣaya Tṛtīyā, the third day of the bright fortnight of Vaiśākha (April–May).

Removing the paste. In the early hours, priests carefully remove the year’s sandal, revealing the image.

Nijarūpa darśanam. For about twelve hours, devotees see the god in his true form, the nijarūpa. Hundreds of thousands come; queues stretch down the hill.

Covered again. In the evening a fresh layer of sandal is applied, about three maunds in the tradition, and the god is hidden for another year.

A great event. It is the biggest day in the temple’s year and one of the great festivals of north Andhra.

ZAPPY: Can I photograph the true form?

PROF. BOLT: No. Photography of the deity is not allowed. The darśana is to be seen and remembered.

KALIṄGA IN STONE

An Odisha-style temple in Andhra

Eastern Gaṅgas. The present temple was rebuilt in the 13th century under the Eastern Gaṅga king Narasiṃhadeva I, the builder of the Sun temple at Konārak in Odisha.

Kaliṅga features. So it shows Kaliṅga features: a curved tower over the sanctum, a hall with a stepped pyramid roof, and walls with fine carving.

Black stone. The temple is built of dark stone, and its walls and pillars carry carved scenes and figures.

Inscriptions. The temple has hundreds of inscriptions in Telugu, Odia and Sanskrit, recording gifts from kings, queens and devotees, including Kṛṣṇadevarāya of Vijayanagara.

Read it. Compare with Konārak (How to Read Part 10) and with the southern temples of this series. Simhachalam is where the two traditions meet.

ZAPPY: Kṛṣṇadevarāya came here?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, after his campaigns in the east in the early 16th century, he visited and made gifts, recorded in inscriptions.

THE KAPPA STAMBHAM

The pillar of embrace

A special pillar. In the temple hall stands the kappa stambham, a pillar that devotees embrace while praying.

For children. It is especially believed to grant children to couples who pray for them, and to bring health.

Santāna Gopāla. Tradition links the pillar with Santāna Gopāla, the giver of children, said to be present in it.

Tribute. Devotees offer at the pillar and may tie threads; priests guide the ritual.

A personal moment. In a busy temple, this is one of the few places where devotees touch the sacred directly.

ZAPPY: Why “kappa”?

PROF. BOLT: It means tribute or offering. The pillar is where offerings and prayers were made.

THE HILL AND ITS WATERS

Springs, steps and the giri pradakṣiṇa

Steps. A stepped path of about a thousand steps climbs from the foot of the hill; most pilgrims now come by the ghat road.

Gaṅgadhāra. A perennial spring on the hill, the Gaṅgadhāra, falls into a pool; pilgrims bathe or sprinkle its water before darśana.

Forest. The hill is covered in forest, with fruit orchards that the temple has long owned.

Giri pradakṣiṇa. Once a year, on Āṣāḍha Pūrṇimā, hundreds of thousands walk round the whole hill, a giri pradakṣiṇa of about 32 km.

Read the climb. As at other hill temples, the path itself is part of the worship.

ZAPPY: Thirty-two kilometres on foot?

PROF. BOLT: In one night and day, yes. People walk in groups, singing, resting at stalls set up along the route.

FESTIVALS AND WORSHIP

Through the year

Kalyāṇotsavam. The annual divine wedding is held in Caitra, soon after Ugādi, with a chariot festival. A daily Kalyāṇam is also offered for devotees.

Narasiṃha Jayantī. The day of Narasiṃha’s appearance, in Vaiśākha, is celebrated with special worship.

Daily worship. The temple follows the Pāñcarātra Āgama, with pūjās from early morning to night.

Vehicles. Festival images go out on vehicles and in the chariot round the temple on festival days.

Kārttika and Dhanurmāsa. Lamps in Kārttika and Tiruppāvai recitation in the month of Dhanus (December–January), as in other Vaiṣṇava temples.

ZAPPY: Is this a Divya Deśam?

PROF. BOLT: No; it is not among the 108 sung by the Āḻvārs. But it is one of the most important Narasiṃha temples in India.

THE MAN-LION

Why Narasiṃha is loved

The boon. Hiraṇyakaśipu had a boon: he could not be killed by man or beast, inside or outside, by day or night, on earth or in the sky, by any weapon.

The answer. Viṣṇu came out of a pillar as Narasiṃha, neither man nor beast, at twilight, on a threshold, held the king on his lap, and killed him with his claws.

Protector. Narasiṃha is worshipped as the fierce protector of devotees. His temples are found across Andhra, from Ahobilam to Mangalagiri to Yadagirigutta.

Lakṣmī calms him. He is often shown with Lakṣmī on his lap, as Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha: the fierce god made gentle by love.

At Simhachalam. Here he is cooled by sandal, joined with Varāha, and worshipped as a gentle, giving god.

ZAPPY: Why so many Narasiṃha temples in Andhra?

PROF. BOLT: He is a favourite god of the Telugu country. Part 4, Ahobilam, has nine of them.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Simhachalam is about 16 km from Visakhapatnam city, which has an airport and a major railway station. Buses and ghat road reach the top.
Timings. The temple opens early and closes in the afternoon for a few hours. Check timings, especially for Chandanotsavam.
Darśana. Free and paid queues; on Chandanotsavam expect very long waits and special arrangements.
Dress. Modest or traditional dress; footwear outside.
Combine. Visakhapatnam’s beaches, the Kailasagiri hill and Buddhist sites such as Thotlakonda nearby.

ZAPPY: Best time to go?

PROF. BOLT: For quiet darśana, any weekday morning. For the true form, Chandanotsavam, if you can face the crowds.
