TEMPLES OF ANDHRA PRADESH · PART 1 OF 10 · THE SEVEN HILLS

Tirumala Veṅkaṭeśvara

The Lord of the Seven Hills · explained and illustrated

Śrīnivāsa on Veṅkaṭādri

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt climb the Seven Hills to meet Veṅkaṭeśvara, hear the story of his marriage and his loan, see the golden vimāna, taste the laddu and learn how to plan a visit.

WHERE WE ARE

On the Seven Hills above Tirupati stands one of the most visited holy places on earth: the temple of Śrī Veṅkaṭeśvara, also called Śrīnivāsa, Bālājī and, in the Tamil and Telugu songs, the Lord of the Seven Hills.

Tens of thousands of pilgrims come every day, and many more on festival days. They come on foot and by road, offer their hair, wait in long queues for a glimpse of the god, and carry home his laddu.

Tirumala is also a Divya Deśam sung by the Āḻvārs, and the god of Annamācārya’s thousands of songs.

With the blessings of the Lord,

ZAPPY: Why do so many people go?

PROF. BOLT: Many believe he is the god of the Kali age, who answers prayers here and now. Every pilgrim has a reason.

ZAPPY: Seven hills?

PROF. BOLT: They are named in tradition as Śeṣādri, Nīlādri, Garuḍādri, Añjanādri, Vṛṣabhādri, Nārāyaṇādri and Veṅkaṭādri, said to form the body of the serpent Ādiśeṣa.

THE STORY OF ŚRĪNIVĀSA

Why Viṣṇu came to the hill

The story

The kick. The Purāṇic story tells that the sage Bhṛgu, testing the gods, kicked Viṣṇu on the chest, where Lakṣmī lives. Viṣṇu calmly honoured the sage; Lakṣmī, offended, left Vaikuṇṭha.
On earth. Viṣṇu came to earth searching for her and lived on the hill as Śrīnivāsa, cared for, the story says, by Vakuḷā Devī, a devotee.
Padmāvatī. He fell in love with Padmāvatī, daughter of the local king Ākāśa Rāja, and they were married in a grand wedding.
The loan. To pay for the wedding, Śrīnivāsa borrowed a fortune from Kubera, the god of wealth, promising to repay it by the end of the Kali age.
The offerings. So devotees say they help him repay the loan through their offerings in the temple’s hundi. Lakṣmī came to live on his chest, and Padmāvatī has her temple at Tiruchanur below.

ZAPPY: The god is in debt?

PROF. BOLT: It is a loving, playful story. It makes the great god close and human: he needs his devotees as much as they need him.

Śrīnivāsa, Padmāvatī and the loan from Kubera.

THE GOLDEN VIMĀNA

Ānanda Nilayam

Ānanda Nilayam. The vimāna over the sanctum is called Ānanda Nilayam, “the abode of bliss”. It is covered in gilded copper and shines across the hill.

Vimāna Veṅkaṭeśvara. On one side of the tower is a small image of the god, the Vimāna Veṅkaṭeśvara. Devotees who cannot get close darśana look for him.

Kings and gold. Many rulers gilded or re-gilded the vimāna over the centuries, recorded in the temple’s inscriptions.

Inside. The sanctum is small and dark, lit by lamps. The god stands about two metres tall, with his eyes covered by a white mark of camphor and sandal, the nāmam.

Read the plan. Around the sanctum are enclosures with the vimāna circuit, smaller shrines, the kitchen, the hundi and the halls.

ZAPPY: Why are his eyes covered?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees say his gaze is too powerful. The covering is lightened only on certain days.

THE GOD IN THE SANCTUM

How Veṅkaṭeśvara stands

Standing. Veṅkaṭeśvara stands upright on a lotus pedestal, about two metres tall, a dark stone image adorned with gold, jewels and flowers.

Four arms. His upper hands hold the discus and the conch. His lower right hand points down to his feet, the varada gesture; his lower left rests on his thigh.

Pointing to the feet. The meaning devotees give: “Surrender at my feet, and the ocean of saṃsāra becomes only knee-deep.”

Lakṣmī on his chest. Lakṣmī is believed to reside on his chest, and a small image of her is there.

The nāmam. His forehead and eyes are covered by the white nāmam, the Śrī Vaiṣṇava mark.

ZAPPY: Is the image Viṣṇu or Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: Over the centuries some have debated it, but in worship and tradition he is Viṣṇu, the Lord of the Śrī Vaiṣṇavas and of countless devotees.

THE PATHS UP THE HILL

Alipiri and Srivari Mettu

Alipiri. The main footpath starts at Alipiri at the foot of the hills in Tirupati, through a gateway with a great Garuḍa. It is about 3,500 steps and takes several hours.

Srivari Mettu. A shorter, steeper path, Srivari Mettu, starts near Srinivasa Mangapuram; tradition says Śrīnivāsa himself walked it after his wedding.

Shelters. Both paths are roofed in many places, with water, toilets and shrines on the way. TTD often gives special darśana tokens to walkers.

By road. Two ghat roads, one up and one down, carry buses and cars.

Varāha first. Tradition says pilgrims should first visit the Bhū Varāha temple near the tank, since the hill is Varāha’s land and he allowed Śrīnivāsa to live there.

ZAPPY: Is the climb hard?

PROF. BOLT: It is long but steady. Start early, carry water, and walk at your own pace. Grandparents and children do it every day.

THE GIFT OF HAIR

Kalyāṇakaṭṭa

A common vow. Offering one’s hair to the god is one of Tirumala’s best-known vows. Men, women and children have their heads shaved at the Kalyāṇakaṭṭa halls.

The meaning. The hair is an offering of pride and beauty, given in thanks for a prayer answered or as a promise.

Free and hygienic. TTD provides the service free, with fresh blades for each pilgrim.

Then the tank. Pilgrims often bathe in the Svāmi Puṣkariṇī, the temple tank, after tonsure and before darśana.

A legend. One story says a devotee, Nīlā Devī, gave her own hair to cover a bald patch on the god’s head, and he promised that all hair offered would be received by her.

ZAPPY: What happens to all that hair?

PROF. BOLT: It is auctioned, and the money goes to the temple’s welfare work. Even the hair is put to use.

A DAY OF SEVĀS

From Suprabhātam to Ekānta Sevā

Suprabhātam. The day begins before dawn with the Suprabhātam, the awakening hymn Kausalyā supraja Rāma, sung at the sanctum door. Recordings of it play in millions of homes.

Tomāla and Archana. The god is decorated with garlands (Tomāla sevā) and worshipped with his names (Archana).

Kalyāṇotsavam. Every day the festival images of the god and his consorts are married in the Kalyāṇotsavam, sponsored by devotees.

Evening. The Ūñjal (swing) and the Sahasra Dīpālaṅkāra, a thousand lamps, are lovely evening sevās.

Ekānta Sevā. At night, in the Ekānta Sevā, the god is put to rest with a lullaby, traditionally sung by descendants of Annamācārya.

ZAPPY: Can anyone attend sevās?

PROF. BOLT: Most need advance booking through TTD, and places are limited. Sarva darśana, the general queue, is open to all.

THE TIRUPATI LADDU

Prasādam with a GI tag

The laddu. The Tirupati laddu is a sweet ball made of gram flour, sugar, ghee, cardamom, cashews and raisins. Every pilgrim wants one.

Since long ago. Sweets have been offered to the god for centuries; the laddu has been the main prasādam since the 20th century.

The kitchen. It is made in the temple kitchen, the Potu, by teams of cooks, in huge quantities every day.

GI tag. The Tirupati laddu received a Geographical Indication tag in 2009, so the name can be used only for the temple’s laddu.

Other prasādams. Pilgrims also receive free food at the vast TTD annaprasādam halls, which serve meals to huge numbers every day.

ZAPPY: How many are made?

PROF. BOLT: Hundreds of thousands a day, by reports. The exact number changes with the crowd.

ANNAMĀCĀRYA

The poet of the Seven Hills

Who. Tāḷḷapāka Annamācārya (15th century), born at Tāḷḷapāka in present-day Annamayya district, devoted his life to Veṅkaṭeśvara.

Songs. Tradition says he composed about thirty-two thousand songs, kīrtanas, in Telugu and Sanskrit. They range from deep philosophy to tender love songs addressed to the god.

Copper plates. His family had his songs engraved on copper plates and kept in the temple. Thousands were found in the 20th century and published.

Still sung. Songs such as “Brahmam okkaṭe” and “Adivo alladivo” are sung in concerts and homes. TTD runs a project to sing and teach them.

In worship. His descendants still sing at the god’s bedtime ritual, and his songs are part of many sevās.

ZAPPY: Are all thirty-two thousand found?

PROF. BOLT: No, only a part, though still many thousands. Each one is a small window into his love for the god.

THE BRAHMOTSAVAM

Nine days of processions

When. The annual Brahmotsavam is held in September–October. In some years a second, Navarātri Brahmotsavam is also held.

Vehicles. For nine days the festival images, Malayappa Svāmī with Śrīdevī and Bhūdevī, go round the four streets on a different vāhana each morning and evening.

Garuḍa Sevā. The night of the Garuḍa vāhana is the most crowded day of the year.

Rathotsavam. Near the end, the god rides the great chariot, and on the last day the Cakra Snānam, the sacred bath of the discus, is held in the tank.

Other festivals. Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, when the Vaikuṇṭha Dvāram, the special gateway round the sanctum, is opened; Rathasaptamī, when the god rides seven vehicles in one day.

ZAPPY: Is it too crowded for a first visit?

PROF. BOLT: It is the most crowded time. For a calm first visit choose an ordinary weekday; come back for the Brahmotsavam later.

AROUND TIRUMALA

Tiruchanur, Govindarāja and more

Padmāvatī. At Tiruchanur, a few kilometres from Tirupati, is the temple of Goddess Padmāvatī. Tradition says a visit to Tirumala is complete only after visiting her.

Govindarāja. In Tirupati town, the Govindarāja temple, with its tall gopuram, honours Viṣṇu reclining; it was consecrated, tradition says, by Rāmānuja.

Kapila Tīrtham. At the foot of the hills, a Śiva temple with a waterfall.

Srinivasa Mangapuram. A temple of Śrīnivāsa, where tradition says he stayed after his wedding.

Srikalahasti. About 40 km away, the great Śiva temple of the wind element, Part 3 of this series (guide on this site).

ZAPPY: How many days for all of this?

PROF. BOLT: Two or three days for Tirumala and Tirupati’s temples; add one for Srikalahasti.

HOW TO VISIT

Planning Tirumala
Book early. Book special-entry darśana, sevās and accommodation on the official TTD website. Free darśana (sarva darśanam) needs no booking but can take many hours.
Dress code. Traditional dress is required for many darśanas: dhoti, or kurta-pyjama, for men; sari or churidar with dupatta for women.
Order. Varāha temple, then Veṅkaṭeśvara, then Padmāvatī at Tiruchanur: the traditional order.
Rules. Phones, cameras and footwear are deposited before the queue. Follow TTD instructions and staff.
Getting there. Tirupati has an airport and major railway station; buses run up the hill all day.

ZAPPY: Any advice for the queue?

PROF. BOLT: Eat before, carry nothing unnecessary, and treat the waiting as part of the pilgrimage. The darśana is brief, but you will remember it.
