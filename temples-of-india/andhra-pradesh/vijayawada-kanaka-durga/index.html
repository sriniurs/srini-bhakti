TEMPLES OF ANDHRA PRADESH · PART 7 OF 10 · INDRAKĪLĀDRI

Vijayawada Kanaka Durgā

The goddess above the Kṛṣṇā · explained and illustrated

The golden goddess on the hill

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt climb Indrakīlādri to meet Kanaka Durgā, the goddess who defeated Mahiṣāsura, hear where Arjuna won his weapon, and see the city celebrate Dasara with a new form of the goddess every day.

WHERE WE ARE

Vijayawada, on the Kṛṣṇā river, is one of Andhra’s busiest cities. Rising over it is a small hill, Indrakīlādri, and on it the temple of Kanaka Durgā, the “golden Durgā”.

She is the city’s guardian goddess. Millions climb her hill each year, above all during Dasara, the Navarātri festival, when she appears in a different form each day.

The hill also has a great story from the Mahābhārata: it is where Arjuna did penance and received a divine weapon from Śiva.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why is she golden?

PROF. BOLT: Kanaka means gold. The name speaks of her radiance and her gift of prosperity to the city.

ZAPPY: Indrakīlādri?

PROF. BOLT: “Indra’s hill”. Tradition says Indra and the gods worshipped the goddess here; the hill keeps the name.

THE GODDESS ON THE HILL

The temple’s story

The story

Mahiṣāsura. The demon Mahiṣāsura, who could take the form of a buffalo, conquered the gods and drove them from heaven.
The goddess. The gods’ combined power took shape as Durgā, armed with all their weapons. She fought and killed Mahiṣāsura (see the Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts series on this site).
On Indrakīlādri. The temple’s story says the sage Indrakīla prayed to the goddess to live on him; he became the hill, and she came to stay as Kanaka Durgā.
Self-manifest. Her image is believed to be svayambhū, self-manifest, appearing on the hill rather than made by human hands.
The city’s mother. For the people of Vijayawada, she is the mother who protects the city from floods, disease and enemies.

ZAPPY: Is this the same story as in the Devī Māhātmya?

PROF. BOLT: The same goddess and the same victory. Each temple adds its own local story of how she came to stay.

Kanaka Durgā: Mahiṣāsura’s slayer, Vijayawada’s mother.

ARJUNA AND THE HUNTER

Where the Pāśupata was given

Penance. In the Mahābhārata, Arjuna did fierce penance to win divine weapons for the coming war. Local tradition places it on Indrakīlādri.

The boar. A demon in the form of a boar charged at him. Arjuna shot it; at the same moment a hunter, a kirāta, shot it too.

The quarrel. They fought over who had killed it. Arjuna could not defeat the hunter, and finally recognised him as Śiva.

The gift. Pleased with his courage and devotion, Śiva gave Arjuna the Pāśupata weapon. The story is told in Bhāravi’s Sanskrit poem Kirātārjunīya.

Vijaya. Some say the city’s name, Vijayawada, the “place of victory”, remembers Arjuna, also called Vijaya. Others link it to Durgā’s victory.

ZAPPY: Is there a shrine to this story?

PROF. BOLT: Yes: the Malleśvara temple on the hill is linked with Śiva’s appearance, and the story is painted and carved in the temple.

THE TEMPLE ON THE HILL

Durgā and Malleśvara

Climbing. Pilgrims reach the temple by stone steps, many climbing them as a vow and applying turmeric to each step, or by the ghat road and lifts.

The goddess. In the sanctum, Kanaka Durgā is shown with eight arms holding weapons, standing on Mahiṣāsura, richly dressed and adorned.

Malleśvara. Near her shrine is the temple of Śiva as Malleśvara, the hill’s god, worshipped together with the goddess.

Śrī Cakra. Temple tradition says Ādi Śaṅkara installed a Śrī Cakra here, and the goddess is worshipped in the Śākta way.

Views. From the top you see the Kṛṣṇā, the Prakasam barrage and the whole city: the goddess watching over her people.

ZAPPY: Turmeric on the steps?

PROF. BOLT: A vow especially of women: they climb slowly, applying turmeric and kumkum to each step as an offering to the goddess.

DASARA ON INDRAKĪLĀDRI

Ten days, ten forms

Navarātri. Dasara is the temple’s greatest festival, held for the nine nights of Navarātri and the tenth day, Vijayadaśamī, in Āśvayuja (September–October).

Alaṅkārams. Each day the goddess is dressed as a different form: forms such as Bālā Tripurasundarī, Gāyatrī, Annapūrṇā, Lalitā, Mahālakṣmī, Sarasvatī, Durgā, Mahiṣāsuramardinī and Rājarājeśvarī. The order is set by the temple each year.

Sarasvatī day. On the day of the Mūla star, the goddess appears as Sarasvatī, and huge crowds of students and families come.

Crowds. Millions of devotees visit over the festival; the city organises special queues, transport and services.

Read the forms. Each form is one aspect of the one Goddess: food, learning, wealth, beauty, power. Together they show her whole nature.

ZAPPY: Why does she change form each day?

PROF. BOLT: To show that the Goddess is all of these at once. Devotees come on the day of the form they need most.

THE GODDESS ON THE RIVER

Teppotsavam on the Kṛṣṇā

Vijayadaśamī. On Vijayadaśamī, the goddess and Malleśvara are taken down to the Kṛṣṇā and placed on a swan-shaped float, the haṃsa vāhanam.

On the river. The float is taken out on the river at night, lit with lamps and fireworks, while crowds watch from the banks and ghats.

Teppotsavam. This float festival, the Teppotsavam, closes Dasara.

River safety. Depending on the river’s level and safety, the float may be held in a limited way; the temple announces the arrangements.

River and hill. The festival joins the goddess on the hill with the river below: Vijayawada’s two sacred landmarks.

ZAPPY: A goddess on a swan?

PROF. BOLT: The swan, haṃsa, is a vehicle of grace and purity. Here it carries the goddess on her city’s river.

BHAVĀNĪ DĪKṢĀ

A vow in red

The vow. Many devotees take Bhavānī Dīkṣā: a period of discipline, often forty days, wearing red clothes, eating simple food and keeping strict habits.

Like Ayyappa. It is similar to the Ayyappa and Hanumān dīkṣās: a time of devotion and self-control.

Completion. At the end, around the month of Mārgaśira (November–December), devotees come to the temple to complete the vow, offering at the goddess’s feet.

Crowds in red. During the completion days, the hill and the city fill with devotees in red, chanting her names.

Read the colour. Red is the goddess’s colour: power, auspiciousness and life.

ZAPPY: Is the dīkṣā only for men?

PROF. BOLT: It is taken by men, women and children, according to the rules each devotee follows with their guru.

AROUND VIJAYAWADA

Caves, temples and the river

Undavalli caves. Across the river, the Undavalli caves are rock-cut temples of the 4th–5th centuries, with a large reclining Viṣṇu.

Mangalagiri. About 12 km away, the hill temple of Pānakāla Narasiṃha, where the god is offered jaggery water, pānakam.

Amaravati. Upstream, Amaravati, an ancient Buddhist site and one of the Pañcārāma Śiva temples.

Mogalrajapuram. Small rock-cut cave temples within the city, among the early cave shrines of the region.

The barrage. The Prakasam barrage across the Kṛṣṇā, by the hill, is a landmark of the city.

ZAPPY: How long should I spend?

PROF. BOLT: A day for the hill and the city’s caves; another for Mangalagiri and Amaravati.

DAILY WORSHIP

A busy hill

Kumkum archana. A favourite ritual is the kuṅkumārcana, worship with kumkum and the goddess’s names.

Offerings. Devotees offer saris, bangles, turmeric and kumkum, especially women, praying for family well-being.

Through the day. Worship runs from early morning to night, with special pūjās on Fridays and full-moon days.

Free food. The temple serves free meals, annaprasādam, to many pilgrims daily.

Other festivals. Śākambharī utsavam in Āṣāḍha, when the goddess is decorated with vegetables; Bhavānī Dīkṣā completion; Śivarātri at Malleśvara.

ZAPPY: Decorated with vegetables?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, for Śākambharī, the goddess who feeds the world. The temple is covered in fresh produce, later distributed.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Vijayawada has a major railway junction, a bus stand and an airport at Gannavaram. The hill is close to the city centre.
Up the hill. Steps, the ghat road and temple transport; on festival days special arrangements apply.
Timings. Early morning to night; check for changes during Dasara.
Dress. Modest or traditional dress; footwear outside. Phones may be restricted during festivals.
Dasara tip. Visit on a weekday before the busiest days, or come early in the morning.

ZAPPY: Is Dasara safe with such crowds?

PROF. BOLT: The city plans carefully, but go prepared: water, light luggage, a meeting point for your group.
