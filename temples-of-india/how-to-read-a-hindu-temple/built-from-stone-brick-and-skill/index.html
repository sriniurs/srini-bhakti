HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 6 OF 12 · MATERIALS

Built from Stone, Brick and Skill

How temples were made, from Ramappa to Thanjavur · explained and illustrated

From quarry to finial

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt follow a temple from the quarry to the finial: carved out of rock or built up in courses, in brick, sandstone, soapstone or granite, and kept standing by clever joints and foundations.

WHERE WE ARE

Every temple you visit was once a problem: how to raise stone high into the air, on soft ground, without cement, and make it last for centuries.

This part asks the builder’s questions. What is it made of? Where did the material come from? How is it joined? What holds it up? The answers are written in the walls if you know where to look.

Our main examples are the Ramappa temple in Telangana, with its floating bricks and sand-filled foundation, and the granite temples of the Cholas at Thanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram.

ZAPPY: Why so many materials?

PROF. BOLT: Builders used what the land gave them. Granite in Tamil Nadu, sandstone in central India, laterite in Kerala and Goa, brick on river plains.

CUT OUT OR BUILT UP?

The first question to ask

The story

Two ways. There are two basic ways to make a temple: carve it out of the living rock, or build it up from separate pieces.
Rock-cut. In a rock-cut temple the sculptors removed everything that was not temple. At Ellora the Kailāsa temple (8th century, under the Rāṣṭrakūṭas) was cut down from the top of a hill into a complete freestanding temple.
Monolith. At Māmallapuram the Pallavas carved five “rathas” from boulders in the 7th century, each a full-size model of a temple type in a single piece of granite.
Built. A structural temple is built course by course. Look for horizontal joints running across the wall: they are the courses.
The test. No joints at all, and the floor continues into the hillside? Rock-cut. Joints in rows, blocks of different sizes? Built. Both together? A later addition to an old cave.

ZAPPY: Which came first?

PROF. BOLT: Rock-cut shrines are among the earliest, but both methods ran side by side for centuries. Māmallapuram has both: the rathas and the built Shore Temple.

No joints = carved. Rows of joints = built.

BRICK TEMPLES

Building with fired earth

Very old. Brick is one of the oldest temple materials. The temple at Bhitargaon in Uttar Pradesh, of the Gupta period (around the 5th–6th century), is a tall brick shrine with terracotta panels.

Where stone is rare. On river plains with clay but little stone, as in Bengal and parts of the Ganga valley, brick was the natural choice, often with terracotta tiles carved or moulded with figures.

Under the plaster. Many South Indian towers have brick in their upper storeys, covered with lime plaster and stucco figures. A crack or a repair may show the brick inside.

Light at the top. Brick is lighter than solid stone, so builders often used stone below and brick above, reducing the weight on the walls.

Read the colour. Red, orange or dark purple surfaces with regular joints: brick. Look for the moulded edges of terracotta panels.

ZAPPY: Doesn’t brick wash away?

PROF. BOLT: Not fired brick, laid well with good lime mortar. Bhitargaon has stood for about fifteen hundred years.

RAMAPPA: BRICKS THAT FLOAT

A Kākatīya experiment

Where and when. The Rudreśvara temple, better known as the Ramappa temple, at Palampet in Telangana was begun in 1213 under the Kākatīya ruler Gaṇapatideva by his general Recharla Rudra. UNESCO listed it in 2021.

Floating bricks. The tower over the sanctum is built of very light, porous bricks, so light that they float on water. A light tower puts less load on the walls and foundation.

Stone below. The walls and pillars are of stone, with beams and pillars of a hard, dark stone (dolerite) carved and polished to a glassy finish.

A sand bed. The temple stands on a sandbox foundation: a pit filled with sand, on which the platform was built. Sand spreads the load and absorbs movement, helping the building ride out ground movement.

The bracket figures. Under the eaves are slender bracket figures of dancers in dark stone, among the finest of their kind.

ZAPPY: Why is it called Ramappa?

PROF. BOLT: By tradition, after its chief sculptor or architect, Rāmappa. It is one of the few Indian temples known by its maker’s name.

THE GRANITE GIANTS

How the Cholas built high

Granite. The Chola temples are built of granite, a very hard stone that is difficult to carve but extremely strong.

Thanjavur. The vimāna of the Bṛhadīśvara temple at Thanjavur rises about 66 m and was finished in 1010. Granite was brought to the site from quarries some distance away; how it was transported is still discussed.

Gangaikondacholapuram. Rājendra I, Rājarāja’s son, built a new capital and temple at Gangaikondacholapuram. Its vimāna, about 51 m high, has a gently curving outline, unlike his father’s straight-sided pyramid.

Hollow inside. The Thanjavur tower is not solid: above the sanctum it rises as a hollow shaft, with the weight carried down the thick walls. That is how it stays up.

The crown stone. The great dome-shaped stone at the top is often said to weigh around 80 tonnes; a well-known tradition says it was raised along a long earth ramp. Treat both as tradition, not measured fact.

ZAPPY: Was the ramp real?

PROF. BOLT: Ramps of earth were a common way to raise heavy stones, and the story is old. But no excavation has proved this particular ramp. Keep it as a good story.

HOLDING IT TOGETHER

Weight, fit and clamps

No cement. Most old Indian stone temples use little or no mortar between stones. The blocks are cut to fit closely and held by their own weight.

Clamps and dowels. Where blocks might slide apart, builders cut slots and set metal clamps or pins, often iron, sometimes sealed with lead. Look for small rectangular holes in fallen blocks: they held clamps.

Corbelling. To close a space without a true arch, each course projects slightly beyond the one below until the gap closes. Many temple ceilings and towers use this method.

Beams and slabs. Halls are roofed by stone beams laid across pillars, with slabs on top. That is why halls have so many pillars (Part 8).

Read the joints. Joints running straight across many blocks often mark building phases or repairs. Very fine joints mean careful work; wide joints filled with later cement are modern repair.

ZAPPY: What happens in an earthquake?

PROF. BOLT: Dry stone can shift and settle without cracking like rigid concrete. Many temples have stood through earthquakes because of it, though some have fallen.

WOOD, LATERITE AND METAL

Where stone is not the whole story

Laterite. In Kerala, coastal Karnataka and Goa, builders used laterite, a reddish stone that is soft when cut from the ground and hardens in air. It is easy to cut into blocks but too coarse for fine carving, so it is often plastered.

Timber. Kerala temples carry heavy rain on steep timber roofs, with carved wooden brackets, ceilings and figures. Our Kerala series has a whole article on how these are made.

Metal. Many roofs and finials are sheathed in copper, sometimes gilded. Flagstaffs and doors may be covered in brass or silver.

Stucco. In the South, the upper storeys of towers and gopuras are covered with lime stucco figures, painted bright colours and renewed every few decades.

The lesson. A temple is often several materials together: stone base, brick or laterite walls, timber roof, metal finial, stucco and paint. Read each layer separately.

ZAPPY: So the “stone temple” may not be all stone?

PROF. BOLT: Often not. The bright figures on a gopura are usually stucco over brick. Only the lower part is stone.

FROM QUARRY TO WALL

A block’s journey

Splitting. At the quarry, masons cut a line of small holes or slots, drove in wedges, and split the block from the rock. Look for rows of wedge marks on old quarry faces and even on temple stones.

Moving. Blocks were moved on sledges or rollers, pulled by people and animals, or floated on rivers and canals where possible.

Rough first. Most blocks were roughly shaped at the quarry, to save weight, and finished at the site. Carving was often done after the stone was in place.

Lifting. Ramps of earth, levers, wooden scaffolding and pulleys raised the blocks. The pieces you see high up each made that climb.

Unfinished clues. Unfinished temples and half-cut blocks, as at some Pallava sites, show each stage frozen in time. They are textbooks in stone.

ZAPPY: How long did it take?

PROF. BOLT: Thanjavur, an enormous project, was built in about a decade or so. Smaller temples could take a few years; some were never finished.

THE BUILDERS AND THEIR BOOKS

Sthapatis, guilds and śilpa-śāstra

The sthapati. The master builder, the sthapati, combined the roles of architect and chief mason. Families and guilds passed the craft from generation to generation, and some still do in Tamil Nadu.

The texts. Sanskrit texts such as the Mayamata and Mānasāra describe how to choose a site, test the soil, lay out a grid, proportion each part and make images.

The grid. Many texts lay out the temple on a square grid (the vāstupuruṣa maṇḍala), with the sanctum at the centre and deities assigned to each square. Proportions follow from a basic unit.

Texts and practice. Real temples do not follow any single text exactly. Builders adapted rules to site, material and patron. Use the texts as a guide, not as a blueprint.

Living tradition. Temple builders today still use these proportions and methods, which is why new temples in the old style look so convincing.

ZAPPY: Did they draw plans on paper?

PROF. BOLT: Plans were laid out on the ground with cords and pegs, and details drawn full-size on flat stones or walls. Some such drawings survive.

READ HOW IT WAS BUILT

A routine
Material. Brick, sandstone, soapstone, granite, laterite, wood? More than one?
Method. Carved or built? Find the joints.
Courses. Are courses even? Where do they change? Note any breaks.
Weight. Where is the heavy stone, where the light? Brick on top?
Repairs. Cement, new stone, different colour? Note them, without judgement.

ZAPPY: Can I tap the stones?

PROF. BOLT: Look, don’t knock. Never climb on or chip the stone. The building is fragile in ways you cannot see.
