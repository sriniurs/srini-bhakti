How to Read a Hindu Temple · Part 1

FROM GOPURA TO GARBHAGṚHA

How to read a Hindu temple plan

TEMPLES AND TĪRTHAS

HOW THIS WORKS: Walk in from the gateway, learn to read the buildings along the way, and try the method at Thanjavur. About 10 minutes.

FOREWORD

A walk from the gateway to the sanctum

The first time you walk into a temple, the tallest tower usually gets your attention. The plan makes sense when you ask a different question: what does each building do?

In this opening guide, Zappy and Prof. Bolt follow the route from the gateway to the inner shrine. Read it with a temple you know in mind. When you reach the drawings, see which spaces you recognise and which order you remember crossing them.

AT A GLANCE

Four things to check before you read a drawing
The north arrowTells you how the drawing is oriented. Turn the plan to match a known landmark before you trust your left and right.
The scale barLets you estimate whether a court spans a few strides or a substantial walk. Measure against the bar if the plan has been resized.
The drawing dateTells you which version of the site is recorded. On a composite plan, find the key for additions and reconstructions.
Solid and broken linesRead the legend. A broken line may indicate something overhead, a reconstructed outline, or a route, depending on the drawing.

TWO TOWERS, TWO JOBS

How to tell a gateway from a shrine tower

You are standing in front of a tall tower with a passage through its base. Beyond it, over another building, you can see a second tower. Which one marks the deity’s shrine? Before looking up at either roof, look down at the ground. The passage tells you that the first tower marks a crossing. The second may sit above the shrine itself.

In a South Indian complex, a gopura or gopuram is a monumental gateway. A vimāna, in the architectural usage followed here, is the shrine structure associated with the sanctum and its superstructure. At Thanjavur, for example, the gopura belongs to the enclosure entrance and the pyramidal vimāna rises above the principal shrine.[1]

Use the distinction before you learn a single style name. Trace each tower down to the building it belongs to. If the opening carries you through a wall, you are looking at an entrance. If the tower rises over the shrine, you have found a different kind of building. A distant photograph can flatten those two jobs into one silhouette.

ZAPPY: Which tower should I look for first?

PROF. BOLT: Find the entrance you can walk through. Then look for the structure the entrance is leading you toward.

CROSSING THE ENCLOSURE

What lies inside a prākāra

Once through the gateway, stop where there is room and look sideways. A wall may run to either side of the passage. Follow it with your eye and you have begun to find the prākāra, an enclosure. Larger complexes can contain several enclosures, with further thresholds between them. At Thanjavur, the enclosure includes a colonnade and subsidiary shrines.[1]

Think of a gate as a break in a line on a plan. That line helps you read the buildings inside it as a group. An enclosure might contain a court, several pavilions and smaller shrines. A second gate may begin another enclosure. Look back after crossing it. Seen from inside, the gate you just used often makes the boundary easier to understand.

A plan can show where you are without telling you everything about where you may go. In a working temple, follow the posted route and the directions of the people responsible for the space. Observe from a place that leaves the threshold clear for worshippers.

ZAPPY: Why does the plan show so many walls?

PROF. BOLT: Each one can mark a different enclosure. Count the boundaries you cross as well as the towers you see.

SPOT THE HALL

Let the columns show you where to look

A maṇḍapa is a hall or pavilion. You may recognise one by its supports and the space they make between them. An open pavilion may stand apart in a court. A hall may also be joined to the shrine, so its roof and columns become easier clues than the outline of the building.

Stand at one edge and look across the rows. Between a pair of supports is a bay. A line of bays can pull your eye toward a doorway, frame a view across the court, or allow space for people to gather. On a drawing, a row of dots or squares often represents those same supports. Imagine the roof spanning from one to the next and the flat plan starts to acquire height.

At Airāvateśvara in Dārāsuram, the front hall adds an unexpected clue. It was conceived as a chariot, with stone wheels, and inscriptions give it a specific name. The wheels are a clue to the building’s form. The pillars and its position on the way to the shrine help you identify its architectural job.[1]

ZAPPY: Those stone wheels belong to the hall?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Look for the pillars and roof first, then see how the carved wheels change the way you read the building.

FIND THE ROOM THE PLAN LEADS TO

From the hall to the threshold

Garbhagṛha is commonly translated as “womb chamber.” It is the innermost shrine chamber that houses the principal form worshipped there. In many temples it is relatively compact. Its doorway matters as a frame through which devotees may receive darśana, the devotional act of seeing and being seen by the deity. Where visitors stand, and how far they may approach, follows the practice of the temple.[4]

On a plan, find the principal shrine chamber by working backward from its doorway toward the hall. Some layouts show a short linking space between them, often called an antarāla. On an elevation, the roof or tower over the shrine gives a clue to its position. The two drawings answer different questions. The plan shows which rooms touch. The elevation shows what rises above them.

When you visit, notice how much of the shrine can be seen from the hall compared with a position farther back. If you are moving with a queue, you may have just a few seconds at the doorway. The layout still becomes legible when you follow that brief line of sight.

ZAPPY: Do I need to enter the inner room to find it on a plan?

PROF. BOLT: The doorway and the hall in front of it give you the relationship you need. Take your bearings from the public route.

PUT TWO KINDS OF MOVEMENT ON THE MAP

Approach and circumambulation

Take a blank page and draw a rectangle for the enclosure. Leave an opening for the gate. Add a hall and a small shrine chamber beyond it. Draw one line from the gate toward that chamber. This is an axis, a line that helps explain how parts align. Real approaches may be offset, interrupted or seen in stages. The line still helps you name the relationship you are looking for.

Next draw a loop where circulation around a shrine is possible. Pradakṣiṇā is ritual circumambulation, commonly clockwise, keeping the sacred centre on the right. Depending on the temple it can take place in a passage around the sanctum or on a route farther outside. The existence, location and availability of that route vary. UNESCO's description of Dārāsuram, for instance, specifies a sanctum without a surrounding passage.[1]

Mark the two movements separately. The straight line explains how you approach or look toward a centre. The loop explains how you keep that centre beside you while moving. Both may be present in a complex, sometimes at different scales.

ZAPPY: Should I draw every turn?

PROF. BOLT: Draw the relationship first. Add the actual turns once you know which route approaches and which route circles.

TRY IT AT BṚHADĪŚVARA, THANJAVUR

Read the Chola temple in three passes

Pick up the UNESCO site description and look at a photograph of Bṛhadīśvara alongside it. Start with the enclosure and identify the gateway. Now look for the great pyramidal mass above the Śiva shrine. UNESCO associates the complex with the Chola king Rājarāja I and records its consecration in 1009–1010 CE. The monumental gateway and the tower over the shrine are readily distinguishable here.[1]

Now look between them. The ICOMOS evaluation identifies a pavilion housing Nandi in the inner courtyard.[2] This gives you a third architectural clue. A pavilion can have an important place on the approach even though it is neither the main gate nor the main shrine. If you are sketching, mark it separately.

Finally, look outside the centre line. The same UNESCO description notes that the surrounding enclosure has a colonnade and subsidiary shrines. The central approach tells one story about the building. The smaller structures around the court tell you how much it leaves out. Give each a mark on the sketch so the court remains a space with edges and destinations.

ZAPPY: So the tallest tower wins this time?

PROF. BOLT: At Thanjavur it happens to point you to the principal shrine. The position of the tower is the part you can use elsewhere.

CARRY THE METHOD OUTSIDE TAMIL NADU

Take the same questions to Bhojpur

The South Indian gateway-to-sanctum example gives you a starting vocabulary. Across India, the skyline and the route change. You may find a comparatively simple entrance and a visually dominant shrine tower, commonly called a śikhara in descriptions of northern temples. UNESCO’s description of the Bhojeśvar temple at Bhojpur uses that term while also discussing the entrance and the platform associated with a hall.[3]

Keep the architectural questions while letting local terms describe the answers. Does the building mark entry through a boundary? Is it a space held up by pillars? Which chamber houses the principal deity? Can the site be walked around, and at what scale? If the answer is unclear, use the published plan or a local description to test your impression.

Photographs are especially good at showing roofs and especially bad at showing what lies behind them. A frontal image may hide subsidiary shrines. A bird’s-eye view may make two connected rooms look separate under different roofs. Put a ground-level view and a plan beside each other whenever you can.

ZAPPY: What if the temple has no giant gopura?

PROF. BOLT: Find the boundary and the shrine by following the building and the route. A giant gate is one way to make the entrance visible.

READ YOUR NEXT TEMPLE IN FIVE MINUTES

A field exercise for your next visit

From a permitted place outside, choose one gate and note the boundary it crosses. Inside, identify one court and one covered space. Find the direction of the principal shrine. If you can see a row of columns, ask whether it frames your view, carries you forward, or does both. Then see whether the available paths make a loop. A few thumbnail sketches will do.

When you compare your sketch with a published site plan, notice where your drawing left something out. You may have followed the visitor queue and missed an older axial relationship. You may have recorded a building you could walk through and overlooked nearby shrines that were visible from the court. The differences tell you something about the way the living temple was being used on that visit.

On the way out, turn around once. The court you crossed quickly on arrival may now give the clearest view of the gate. Seen from the other side, the tower that first drew your eye has become what it was all along: the place where you crossed from one space into another.

ZAPPY: What should I draw if I only have time for four marks?

PROF. BOLT: A gate, a boundary, a hall and a shrine. You can add the route between them from memory.

GLOSSARY

Four extra terms you may see on architectural drawings
AdhiṣṭhānaThe moulded base beneath the walls or supports of a shrine or pavilion.
DvārapālaA guardian figure at a doorway. At the inner gateway of Bṛhadīśvara, the IGNCA documents a monumental pair carved from granite. [5]
AmbulatoryAn architectural term for the passage or space available for walking around a central feature.
Section drawingA view through an imaginary vertical cut, useful for seeing the relation of floors, ceilings and towers.
