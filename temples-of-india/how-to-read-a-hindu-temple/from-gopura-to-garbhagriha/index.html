HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 1 OF 12 · THE PLAN

From Gopura to Garbhagṛha

How to read a temple plan · explained and illustrated

From the gateway to the sanctum

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt walk from the gateway inwards, one building at a time, and then try the method on the great temple at Thanjavur. By the end you can read the plan of almost any Hindu temple.

WHERE WE ARE

The first time you walk into a big temple, the tallest tower grabs your attention. But a temple makes sense only when you ask a different question: what does each building do?

Every Hindu temple, from a village shrine to a royal monument, is a path. It leads from the outside world, through a gate and an enclosure, past a hall where people gather, to a small dark room at the centre where the deity lives: the garbhagṛha, the “womb-house”.

This guide teaches you to read that path, part by part. Keep a temple you know in mind as you read.

ZAPPY: Do I need to know architecture to read a temple?

PROF. BOLT: Not at all. You need to notice where you walk, what you pass through and what you are being led towards. The names come later.

AT A GLANCE

The parts of a temple in one table
GopuraA gateway tower over an entrance in the enclosure wall
PrākāraAn enclosure: a walled court around the temple; big temples have several, one inside another
MaṇḍapaA hall or pavilion on pillars, where people gather, wait or watch rituals
AntarālaThe vestibule joining the hall to the sanctum
GarbhagṛhaThe sanctum, the small inner chamber that houses the deity
Vimāna / ŚikharaThe tower rising over the sanctum: vimāna in the South, śikhara in the North
PradakṣiṇāWalking round the sanctum clockwise, keeping the deity on your right
AxisThe straight line from the main gate through the hall to the deity

ZAPPY: Why two words for the tower?

PROF. BOLT: The South calls the sanctum and its tower together the vimāna; the North calls the tower the śikhara, “peak”. Part 4 shows you the difference.

TWO TOWERS, TWO JOBS

How to tell a gateway from a shrine tower

The story

The question. You stand before a tall tower with a passage through its base. Beyond it, over another building, rises a second tower. Which one marks the god?
Look down, not up. Before you look at the roofs, look at the ground. If you can walk through the bottom of a tower, it is a gateway, a gopura. It marks a crossing, not a shrine.
The shrine tower. The second tower stands over a closed room you cannot walk through. That is the tower of the sanctum, the vimāna. The god sits beneath it.
A trap in photos. From far away the two can look alike, and in later South Indian temples the gateways grew taller than the shrine towers. Size does not tell you which is which.
The rule. Trace each tower down to the building it stands on. A tower over a passage is a door; a tower over a closed room is the god’s home.

ZAPPY: So the tallest tower is not always the most important?

PROF. BOLT: In many great Tamil temples, no. The giant gateways came later. The shrine stays small and old at the centre.

Gopura = gate you walk through. Vimāna = tower over the god.

CROSSING THE ENCLOSURE

What lies inside a prākāra

Walls make groups. Once through the gateway, stop and look sideways. A wall runs to either side of the passage: you have found the prākāra, an enclosure. Everything inside it belongs together.

Rings inside rings. Big temples have several prākāras, one inside another, each with its own gateway on the axis. Each wall you cross takes you one step closer to the deity and one step further from the street.

What you find inside. A court with pavilions, a flagstaff and an altar stone on the axis, a hall for the god’s vehicle (Nandi for Śiva, Garuḍa for Viṣṇu) facing the sanctum, small shrines for other gods along the walls, kitchens, stores and sometimes a tank.

Look back. After you pass a gate, turn round. Seen from inside, the wall you crossed is easier to understand: it is a boundary, and the gate is a gap in it.

ZAPPY: Why does the plan show so many walls?

PROF. BOLT: Each marks a different enclosure. Count the boundaries you cross as well as the towers you see.

SPOT THE HALL

Let the pillars show you where to look

Maṇḍapa. A maṇḍapa is a hall or pavilion on pillars. Some stand alone in the court; others are joined to the shrine in front of the sanctum.

Bays. The space between four pillars is a bay. Rows of bays make aisles, and the widest aisle usually runs down the middle, pulling your eye towards the sanctum door.

On a plan. A row of small squares or dots on a temple plan is a row of pillars. Imagine a roof spanning from one to the next and the flat plan starts to rise.

Many halls. Temples may have a hall for gathering, a hall for dance and music, a wedding hall, a hall for the god’s vehicle. Part 8 is all about them.

A surprise. At Dārāsuram near Kumbakonam the front hall was built as a chariot, with stone wheels. The pillars still tell you it is a hall; the wheels tell you what it pretends to be (Part 10).

ZAPPY: Why so many pillars? Couldn’t they make a bigger roof?

PROF. BOLT: Stone beams are short. Each pillar holds up a short slab. So the bigger the hall, the more pillars: a forest of stone.

THE ROOM THE PLAN LEADS TO

Antarāla and garbhagṛha

Antarāla. Between the hall and the sanctum is a short passage or vestibule, the antarāla. It narrows the space and slows you down.

Garbhagṛha. The sanctum, the garbhagṛha, “womb-house”, is usually small, square, dark and windowless. It houses the main image or liṅga, and only priests enter.

The doorway. The sanctum doorway is a frame. Through it the devotee receives darśana, the sight of the deity, and is seen by the deity in return. Door guardians (dvārapālas) often stand on either side.

Ambulatory. In larger temples a passage runs round the sanctum inside an outer wall, so devotees or priests can walk around the deity: a sāndhāra plan.

Under the tower. The tower rises directly over the sanctum. The tallest part of the temple sits over its smallest room.

ZAPPY: Why make the most important room the smallest?

PROF. BOLT: Because it is not a meeting room. It is a womb, a seed, a cave. Its power comes from being small, dark and still.

TWO KINDS OF MOVEMENT

Along the axis and round the shrine

The axis. Most temples are laid out on a straight line from the main gate, through the flagstaff, the altar stone, the vehicle’s pavilion and the hall, to the deity. Walking it, you face the god all the way.

Facing east. Most sanctums face east, towards the rising sun, so the main gate is usually on the east; some face west, north or south for reasons of the deity or the site.

Pradakṣiṇā. The second movement is the circle. Devotees walk clockwise round the sanctum, keeping it on their right, often more than once. Big temples have several paths, one in each enclosure.

The water channel. On a Śiva temple the channel draining the abhiṣeka water from the sanctum, usually on the north side, is not stepped over; devotees turn back there and complete the circle in reverse.

Both together. So the temple plan is a line and a set of circles: you go in straight, you go round, and you come back out the same way.

ZAPPY: Why clockwise?

PROF. BOLT: The right side is the auspicious side in Indian custom. Keeping the god on your right is a sign of respect, like walking beside an elder.

TRY IT AT THANJAVUR

Reading the Bṛhadīśvara temple

The temple. The Bṛhadīśvara temple at Thanjavur, which its builder called Rājarājeśvaram, was built by the Chola king Rājarāja I and consecrated in 1010. It is part of the UNESCO World Heritage site “Great Living Chola Temples”.

Gates. You enter from the east through two gopuras, one after the other on the axis. Both are lower than the shrine tower: here, in an early Chola temple, the god’s tower still dominates.

The court. Inside lies a vast court, lined with a colonnade and small shrines. On the axis sits the pavilion of Nandi, Śiva’s bull, facing the sanctum. The present Nandi and its pavilion are later Nāyaka-period additions.

Halls to sanctum. Steps lead up into a chain of halls, then the vestibule and the sanctum with its huge liṅga, ringed by an inner ambulatory.

The vimāna. Over it rises the vimāna, about 66 m high, crowned by a dome-like top stone. You have walked the whole grammar: gate, gate, court, vehicle, halls, vestibule, sanctum, tower.

ZAPPY: Does every temple have all these parts?

PROF. BOLT: No. A small shrine may be just a room and a porch. But the order stays the same: outside to inside, step by step.

CARRY THE METHOD NORTH

The same grammar, a different skyline

Same order. Travel north to a temple like Kandāriyā Mahādeva at Khajurāho (11th century) and the order is the same: an entrance porch, halls, a vestibule and the sanctum, often on a high platform.

Rising roofs. Here the roofs of porch and halls rise step by step like a mountain range, building up to the tallest peak over the sanctum: the curved tower, the śikhara.

Fewer walls. North Indian temples usually have no great series of walled enclosures and gateway towers; the temple stands on its platform, and you walk round it there.

Read it the same way. Look for where you enter, where people gather, where the path narrows, and where it ends in a small dark room under the highest roof.

ZAPPY: So the plan is national, only the style is regional?

PROF. BOLT: A good way to put it. North, South, Kerala, Odisha: the path is shared; the costume changes.

READ YOUR NEXT TEMPLE IN FIVE MINUTES

A routine to carry with you
Find the gate. Where do you enter? Is there a tower over it? Which way does it face?
Count the walls. How many enclosures do you cross before the shrine?
Spot the halls. Where do people gather, wait, sit, watch? Which way do the aisles point?
Reach the door. Find the sanctum doorway and the tower above it. What deity, facing which way?
Walk the circle. Go round clockwise. What is on the outer walls of the sanctum? Where is the water channel?

ZAPPY: And if the temple is crowded?

PROF. BOLT: Then read it slowly over several visits. The building is patient. Follow the priests’ directions and never block the way of worshippers.
