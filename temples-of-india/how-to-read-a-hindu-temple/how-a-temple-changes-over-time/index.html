HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 11 OF 12 · PHASES

How a Temple Changes Over Time

Phases, additions, repairs and what survives · explained and illustrated

One temple, many centuries

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt learn to read a temple as a story told in layers: the first shrine, the halls and walls added later, the giant gateways, the repairs and renewals, and what survives as ruins.

WHERE WE ARE

A temple is rarely built in one go and then left alone. Most living temples have been growing, repaired and renewed for centuries.

A king builds the shrine. His successors add a hall. Later rulers add an enclosure, a gateway, a tank. Merchants pay for a pillar, a family for a shrine to the goddess. Roofs leak and are replaced; towers are replastered and repainted.

Reading a temple means learning to tell these layers apart, so you can say: this part is older, that part was added.

ZAPPY: Is renovation bad?

PROF. BOLT: Not at all. A living temple must be cared for. The question is how: what is kept, what is replaced, and whether the old evidence survives.

HOW A TEMPLE GROWS

From shrine to complex

The story

The core. Most temples begin with a sanctum and perhaps a porch. This core is usually the oldest part.
Halls. Later, halls are added in front or around: a bigger hall, a wedding hall, a hall for the vehicle. Each records a new patron and a new need.
Walls and gates. Enclosure walls and gateways may come centuries later, wrapping the old temple in new rings.
Shrines. Shrines to goddesses and other deities are added within the enclosure: in many South Indian temples the goddess’s shrine is a later addition.
Outwards. So a temple often grows from the centre outwards. The outer parts are frequently the newer ones; the centre is the oldest.

ZAPPY: Always?

PROF. BOLT: Not always. Sometimes the old core was rebuilt and the outer walls are older. That is why we check, not assume.

Centre usually oldest; outer rings often later. Check, don’t assume.

FOUR CLUES TO PHASES

Joints, style, material, inscriptions

Joints. Where a new building meets an old one, look for a straight joint: a vertical line where the courses do not interlock. Mouldings that stop abruptly are another sign.

Style. Pillar shapes, figure styles and moulding profiles change over time. Chola, Vijayanagara and Nāyaka pillars are easy to tell apart (Part 8).

Material. A change from granite to brick, or from one stone colour to another, may mark a new phase, or simply a different job. Check with the other clues.

Inscriptions. An inscription gives a date by which a wall existed (Part 3). Different kings on different parts of the temple suggest different phases.

Agreement. When joints, style, material and inscriptions agree, you can be fairly confident. When they disagree, you have found an interesting problem.

ZAPPY: So I become a detective?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Gather clues, weigh them, and write “probably” when you are not sure.

THANJAVUR’S LATER LAYERS

A Chola temple with Nāyaka and Maratha additions

Chola core. The great vimāna, the halls before it and the enclosure were built by Rājarāja I and consecrated in 1010 (Part 1).

Nandi. The large Nandi and its pavilion on the axis belong to the later Nāyaka period, replacing or supplementing an earlier arrangement.

More shrines. Shrines within the court, including the shrine of Subrahmaṇya and that of the goddess, were added by later rulers, Nāyaka and Maratha.

Paintings. The walls of the passage round the sanctum were repainted in the Nāyaka period over the Chola murals (Part 9).

Read it. So a visitor to Thanjavur walks through at least three dynasties’ work. The Chola temple is the core; later rulers wrapped it in their own devotion.

ZAPPY: Did the later rulers spoil it?

PROF. BOLT: They added what they thought a great temple needed. Their work is history too. Reading the layers lets us honour each of them.

WHY THE GATEWAYS GREW

Madurai and Srirangam

Small centre, huge gates. In many great Tamil temples the gateways tower over the sanctum. They were mostly added from the 12th century onwards, especially under the Vijayanagara and Nāyaka rulers.

Madurai. At the Mīnākṣī temple in Madurai, much of what you see, including most of the tall gopuras, dates from the 16th and 17th centuries under the Nāyakas, around much older shrines.

Srirangam. At Srirangam the Raṅganātha temple has seven enclosures. Its great southern gateway, the Rājagopuram, stood unfinished for centuries and was completed only in 1987, rising about 73 m.

Why?. Gateways were the public face of a temple, built by rulers and patrons who wanted to be seen. Each new enclosure needed new gates, and each generation built higher.

Read the scale. When the tallest tower is a gateway, ask: how old is the sanctum inside? Often the answer is: much older than the gates.

ZAPPY: So the newest part is the tallest?

PROF. BOLT: Very often in the South. It is a good reminder to trace towers down to the ground (Part 1).

RENEWAL AND CONSECRATION

The temple’s own rhythm of repair

Kumbhābhiṣeka. Living South Indian temples are renewed and reconsecrated in a ceremony called kumbhābhiṣeka, when sanctified water is poured over the finials. Many temples aim to hold one about every twelve years.

Before it. Before the ceremony the temple is repaired: towers replastered and repainted, stucco figures remade, floors relaid, roofs fixed.

Bright colours. That is why gopuras look so freshly painted: the stucco is renewed regularly. The colour is new, but the practice is old.

Losses. Renewal can also remove old evidence: old plaster, paintings and inscriptions can be covered or lost if care is not taken.

Balance. The best work keeps the temple alive for worship while protecting old fabric. Temple authorities, archaeologists and devotees increasingly work together on this.

ZAPPY: Why twelve years?

PROF. BOLT: It is a customary interval in many temples, linked with Āgama traditions. Practice varies by temple and circumstance.

WHEN A TEMPLE FALLS

Reading ruins and survivals

Ruins are evidence. A fallen temple tells its story through what survives: the platform, the lower walls, the scattered blocks. Even a single moulding can be dated by style.

Konārak. At Konārak the tower over the sanctum fell; the hall survives and was filled with sand in the early 20th century to stabilise it (Part 10).

Belur and Halebidu. The Hoysala temples of Belur and Halebidu lost their original towers; Somanathapura kept its own (Part 5).

Re-erected. Archaeologists sometimes re-erect fallen temples stone by stone from numbered pieces, a method called anastylosis. Look for numbers painted or cut on blocks.

Reuse. Fallen stones were often reused in later buildings: a carved block upside down in a wall, a pillar in a village well. Spotting them is a great field exercise.

ZAPPY: Why did temples fall?

PROF. BOLT: Weathering, weak foundations, earthquakes, lightning, floods, salt, neglect and conflict. Usually several causes together.

WHO PAID, WHEN

Patrons through the centuries

Many hands. Temples are built and rebuilt by many patrons: kings and queens, generals, ministers, merchants, guilds, village assemblies, devotees of modest means.

Small gifts. Many parts were gifts on a small scale: a single pillar, a doorway, a lamp-post, a well. Their donors are sometimes named in a short inscription or carved with folded hands.

Read the donor. When you see a small figure with folded hands at the base of a pillar or near a doorway, it may be the person who paid for it.

Recent patrons. Modern renovations are often recorded on marble plaques naming donors and dates. They are inscriptions too, and future historians will read them.

A temple of many. So a temple is not one person’s work but a community’s, across centuries. Reading the phases is reading that community.

ZAPPY: So the plaque by the door is history too?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. One day it will be as old as the Chola inscriptions are now. Note it down.

OLD, RESTORED OR NEW?

Telling them apart

Weathering. Old stone is softened, darkened, patched with lichen, its sharp edges worn. New stone is crisp, clean and uniform in colour.

Tool marks. Machine-cut stone has regular, very straight cuts and smooth faces. Hand-dressed stone shows chisel marks.

Cement. Grey cement in joints, or cement figures painted to look like stone, are modern. Old joints are usually tight with no mortar or with lime.

Paint. Bright enamel paint over stone and plaster is usually recent. Earth colours with fine lines are more often old (Part 9).

Say it plainly. In your notes, write “appears old”, “repaired” or “new”, and why. That is honest, useful and fair to the people who cared for the temple.

ZAPPY: Is a new temple less sacred?

PROF. BOLT: Not at all. Sacredness comes from worship, not age. We are only reading history, not ranking devotion.

READ A TEMPLE’S PHASES

A routine
Core. Find the sanctum and its walls. What style? Any inscriptions?
Additions. Walk outwards. Where do straight joints or style changes appear?
Gates. Are the gateways later than the sanctum? Trace them down.
Repairs. Note cement, new stone, new paint.
Phase plan. Colour your plan: oldest, later, recent. Write “probably” where unsure.

ZAPPY: Can I ask the priests?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, politely, at a quiet time. They often know a great deal about the temple’s history and recent renovations.
