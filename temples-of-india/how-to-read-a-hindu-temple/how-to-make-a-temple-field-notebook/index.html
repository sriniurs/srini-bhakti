How to Make a Temple Field Notebook

A method you can carry to any temple · explained and illustrated

Everything in one notebook

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt put the whole series into one method: what to carry, how to behave, how to sketch, orient, photograph and record, and how to turn a visit into notes you will still understand in ten years.

WHERE WE ARE

Eleven parts taught you to read plans, festivals, inscriptions, towers, walls, materials, water, halls, paintings, chariots and change.

This last part turns all of that into a habit: a field notebook. A notebook slows you down, makes you look, and keeps what you saw long after the photographs blur together.

You need no special training: a pencil, a notebook, a little patience and respect for the people who worship there.

ZAPPY: Why not just take photos?

PROF. BOLT: Photos record everything and explain nothing. A sketch forces you to decide what matters, and notes say why. Use both.

ZAPPY: Five pages per temple?

PROF. BOLT: For a first visit, yes. A big complex may need twenty; a village shrine fits on two.

BEFORE YOU START WRITING

Prepare, respect, orient

The story

Prepare. Read a little before you go: the temple’s deity, its main festivals, what is known about its history. Note questions you want to answer.
Check the rules. Find out the timings, dress code, and whether photography and sketching are allowed inside. Many sanctums do not allow photographs.
Respect. Remember that a living temple is a place of worship first. Do not block devotees, do not sit with your feet towards the deity, and step aside during rituals.
Ask permission. If you want to sketch for a long time or photograph inside, ask the temple office or a priest. Most people are glad to help when asked politely.
Orient. Before drawing anything, find north with a compass or phone. Note which way the sanctum faces. Everything else hangs on this.

ZAPPY: And if someone asks what I’m doing?

PROF. BOLT: Tell them honestly. Show your sketch. Many people will share stories about the temple you will never find in books.

Read, check rules, respect worship, ask, then find north.

PAGE 1: THE BASICS

Who, where, when

Name and place. Write the temple’s full name as used locally, its village or town, and district. Add the name on the board if different.

Date and time. Note the date and the time you arrived and left. Light and crowds change through the day; so does what is open.

Deity and direction. Main deity, other important shrines, and the direction the sanctum faces.

Who you asked. Names (if they agree) and roles of people who helped: a priest, a guide, a shopkeeper. Note what each told you.

First impressions. Three lines only: what struck you first? First impressions fade fastest and often notice what later visits miss.

ZAPPY: Why write the weather?

PROF. BOLT: Because light changes everything: carvings, colours, shadows. A note saying “overcast, flat light” explains a lot later.

PAGE 2: THE PLAN

Sketching by pacing

Walk the axis. Start at the main gate and walk to the sanctum door, counting paces. Then pace the width of the court. Your pace (about 0.7–0.8 m for many adults) is your ruler.

Rough is fine. Draw boxes for each building in roughly the right place and size. Do not worry about straight lines; worry about getting the order right.

Label. Gate, enclosure, flagstaff, vehicle, halls, vestibule, sanctum, sub-shrines, tank, kitchen, well. Use the words from Part 1.

North arrow. Always draw it. A plan without north is a puzzle.

Your route. Draw your route with a dotted line, including the circumambulation. Later, your photos will follow the same path.

ZAPPY: What if I can’t walk everywhere?

PROF. BOLT: Mark it “not seen”. An honest gap is more useful than an invented wall.

PAGE 3: THE ELEVATION

Towers and walls from the side

From the side. Stand far enough away to see the tower and hall from one side. Draw the outline only: base, walls, roof line, tower, top.

Tower type. Note Nāgara or Drāviḍa or other (Part 4): curve or storeys, number of tiers, the top stone and finial.

Walls. Mark the niches and what deities they hold, especially on the sanctum walls (Part 4). Note pilasters and window-like openings.

Base. Note the mouldings, any friezes (Part 5) and any inscriptions (Part 3).

Gateways. If there are gopuras, draw one too, with its number of storeys and roof shape. Compare its height with the sanctum tower.

ZAPPY: I can’t draw!

PROF. BOLT: You don’t need to. Boxes, lines and arrows with words are enough. The notebook is for seeing, not for exhibiting.

PAGE 4: DETAILS

Sculpture, inscriptions, paintings, water

Sculpture. Pick three figures. For each: where it is (wall, side, height), who it is (by attributes, Part 5), and one detail you liked.

Inscriptions. For each one: where, how many lines, which script if you can tell, and a photo number. Look it up later (Part 3).

Paintings. Where, what scenes, how many layers, condition. No flash (Part 9).

Water. Spout position, tank, wells, stepwell, inlets (Part 7).

Halls and chariots. Bay counts, pillar types, any wheels or horses on bases (Parts 8 and 10).

ZAPPY: That’s a lot for one page!

PROF. BOLT: Pick what this temple is rich in. A Hoysala temple fills the sculpture box; a Chola one the inscription box.

PHOTOGRAPHING WELL

Fewer, better, labelled

Wide, medium, close. For each important thing, take one wide shot showing where it is, one medium shot of the wall or object, and one close-up with a pen or coin for scale.

Follow your route. Photograph in the order of your dotted route. Later the sequence of files will match the plan.

Number in notes. Write the photo number or time in your notebook next to each note. This one habit saves hours later.

Light. Low, slanting light shows carving and inscriptions; flat light shows colour. No flash on paintings or in the sanctum.

Respect. Never photograph people at worship without their consent, and never where photography is forbidden.

ZAPPY: How many photos?

PROF. BOLT: Fewer than you think. Fifty labelled photos are worth more than five hundred unlabelled ones.

PAGE 5: QUESTIONS AND PHASES

What you don’t know yet

Questions. List everything you wondered about: Why does this hall face that way? Who is this figure? Is that wall later? Questions are the most valuable part of a notebook.

Phases. Colour your plan in phases using Part 11’s clues: oldest, later, recent, unsure.

Look-ups. Note what to look up: inscription volumes, ASI reports, UNESCO pages, temple histories, books.

Next visit. What would you look at next time, and at what time of day or which festival?

One sentence. Finally, write one sentence that sums up the temple for you. You will be surprised how useful it is years later.

ZAPPY: Should I answer all the questions?

PROF. BOLT: No. Some will stay open. The best notebooks are full of good questions.

AFTER THE VISIT

Turning notes into a record

Same day. Within a day, go through your photos and rename or tag them to match your notes and plan.

Clean copy. Redraw the plan neatly if you like, keeping the original sketch. The rough one is evidence; the neat one is for reading.

Check facts. Look up dates, names and inscriptions in good sources: ASI, UNESCO, state archaeology departments, temple authorities, published scholarship.

Cite. Write where each fact came from. A note “guide said” is fine, as long as you say it.

Share kindly. If you share online, credit the temple and helpers, follow photography rules, and avoid revealing details the temple asked to keep private.

THE SERIES IN TWELVE QUESTIONS

One question from each part
1–3. Where is the path to the sanctum? What happens when the god goes out? What do the inscriptions give?
4–6. Curve or storeys? What is on the wall, band by band? What is it made of, and how?
7–8. Where is the water? Where do people gather?
9–10. Is there paint? Is any part a chariot?
11–12. Which parts are older? What will you write down?

ZAPPY: That’s the whole series on one page?

PROF. BOLT: Twelve questions, one notebook. Carry them to any temple and it will start to speak.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the method comes from

Method: standard field-recording practice used in architectural history and archaeology, adapted for visitors: Archaeological Survey of India guidance for visitors to protected monuments (photography and conduct); UNESCO World Heritage Centre site pages for dates cited across the series.

Series references: see the Sources pages of Parts 1–11.
