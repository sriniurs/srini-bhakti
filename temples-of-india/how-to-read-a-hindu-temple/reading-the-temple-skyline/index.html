HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 4 OF 12 · TOWERS

Reading the Temple Skyline

Nāgara, Drāviḍa and the lesson of Pattadakal · explained and illustrated

A curve or a staircase?

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt learn the two great tower families of Indian temples, take apart a Nāgara śikhara and a Drāviḍa vimāna, and visit Pattadakal, where both stand side by side.

WHERE WE ARE

Parts 1 to 3 kept our eyes at ground level. Now look up.

The tower over the sanctum is the temple’s signature. From a distance, before you can see any image or carving, its outline tells you where you are and which tradition the builders followed.

The old Indian texts on architecture name several temple types. Two are easy to tell apart: the northern Nāgara, with a curved tower, and the southern Drāviḍa, with a stepped one. A third name, Vesara, is used for mixed forms, especially in Karnataka.

ZAPPY: Is it only about north and south?

PROF. BOLT: Mostly, but not strictly. Builders travelled, and some places used both. That is what makes Pattadakal so exciting.

AT A GLANCE

Tower words in one table
ŚikharaThe curved tower of a Nāgara temple; also, in the South, the dome-like top stone of a vimāna
ĀmalakaThe ribbed, cushion-shaped stone ring on top of a Nāgara tower
KalaśaThe pot-shaped finial at the very top
VimānaIn Drāviḍa usage, the sanctum with its stepped tower
TalaOne storey or tier of a Drāviḍa vimāna
HāraThe chain of miniature shrines round the edge of each tier
Kūṭa / śālāSquare and oblong miniature shrines in that chain
VesaraA mixed form, often linked with Karnataka

ZAPPY: Why do the words overlap?

PROF. BOLT: Because Sanskrit texts and modern scholars use them a little differently. Here we keep śikhara for the northern curve and vimāna for the southern tower.

THE OUTLINE TEST

A curve or a staircase?

The story

Squint. Stand far enough back to see the whole tower and half close your eyes. Let the carving blur. What shape is left?
A curve. If the sides rise in one continuous curve, bending inwards towards the top like a bamboo shoot, you are looking at a Nāgara tower, a śikhara.
A staircase. If the tower rises in distinct horizontal storeys, each smaller than the one below, like a stepped pyramid, it is a Drāviḍa vimāna.
Check the top. A Nāgara tower ends in a flat, ribbed ring stone, the āmalaka, and a pot. A Drāviḍa tower ends in a dome-shaped stone, often eight-sided or round, with a pot finial.
Then check the gate. In the South, gateway towers (gopuras) also rise in tiers but end in a barrel-vaulted roof, longer than it is wide. Do not confuse them with the shrine tower.

ZAPPY: And if neither fits?

PROF. BOLT: Then it may be a regional form: a sloping-roof Kerala shrine, a curved Bengal hut-roof, a Himalayan wooden temple, or a Vesara mix.

Curve with an āmalaka = Nāgara. Storeys with a dome = Drāviḍa.

INSIDE A NĀGARA ŚIKHARA

How the curve is made

Built in bands. A Nāgara tower is built up of many horizontal courses, each set slightly inwards from the one below, so that the outline curves.

Vertical lines. The faces of the tower are divided by projecting bands running up its height. In more complex towers, these offsets multiply and the tower looks ribbed.

A net of windows. The surface is covered with a net of horseshoe-shaped window motifs, called gavākṣa, “cow’s eye”, woven together like lacework.

The āmalaka. At the top, a large ribbed ring stone, named after the āmalaka fruit (Indian gooseberry), caps the tower. Small āmalakas also mark the corners of the storeys.

The pot. Above it sits the kalaśa, a pot finial, and often a flag or a weapon of the deity.

ZAPPY: Why gooseberry?

PROF. BOLT: The ribbed stone looks like the fruit cut across. It became one of the clearest signs of a northern temple.

ONE TOWER OR MANY?

Latina and śekharī

Latina. The simplest Nāgara tower has one smooth curve rising from the sanctum walls, a form called latina. Many early temples, like those in the Gupta and early medieval north, use it.

Śekharī. Later towers add smaller copies of the tower, half-towers called urushṛṅga, clustered on the sides. The tower becomes a mountain range rising to one peak: the śekharī form.

Khajurāho. At Khajurāho, the Kandāriyā Mahādeva temple (11th century) has dozens of smaller towers clustered round its main one, building up like a mountain.

Bhūmija. In Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan another form, bhūmija, arranges small towers in vertical rows on the faces, like stacked storeys of miniature śikharas.

The idea. All of them say the same thing: the temple is a mountain, the home of the gods. The more peaks, the grander the mountain.

ZAPPY: Is the mountain a real one?

PROF. BOLT: Often it is Meru, the cosmic mountain at the centre of the world, or Kailāsa, Śiva’s home.

INSIDE A DRĀVIḌA VIMĀNA

Storeys, chains and a crown

Storeys. A Drāviḍa tower rises in clear storeys, called tala. A small shrine may have one or two; Thanjavur’s great vimāna has thirteen.

Chains of shrines. Along the edge of each storey runs a parapet made of miniature shrines, called the hāra, “garland”. Square ones at the corners are kūṭa; long, barrel-roofed ones in the middle are śālā; small ones between are pañjara.

The crown. On top sits a single large dome-like stone, called the śikhara in southern texts. Its shape names the temple type: square, eight-sided or round.

The walls. Below, the walls of the sanctum are divided by pilasters into bays, with niches for deities on each side.

A palace for a god. The whole tower is a palace of many storeys, each a smaller copy of the house below, rising to the god’s own pavilion at the top.

ZAPPY: So the tower is made of little temples?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Read each tiny shrine on the tiers and you will see the whole temple repeated, smaller and smaller, up to the crown.

THE GODS ON THE TOWER’S WALLS

Who stands in the niches

Niches. Below the tower, the outer walls of the sanctum carry niches (devakoṣṭha) for deities. On a Drāviḍa temple there is usually one in the middle of each wall except the entrance side.

A Chola pattern. In many Chola Śiva temples the south niche holds Dakṣiṇāmūrti, Śiva as teacher; the west niche Liṅgodbhava, Śiva appearing in a pillar of fire, or sometimes Viṣṇu; and the north niche Brahmā. Gaṇeśa and Durgā often stand nearby on the walls of the vestibule.

Facing out. These deities face outwards, so devotees walking round the sanctum (Part 1) meet them one by one. The walk is a sequence of darśanas.

In the North. Northern temples also put deities in the central projections of the walls, often the forms of the main god, and crowd the walls with figures in bands.

Read the set. Once you know a region’s usual set, any change is a clue: a different deity, a later replacement, or a special local tradition.

ZAPPY: Why Dakṣiṇāmūrti in the south?

PROF. BOLT: His name means “facing south”. He teaches the sages sitting under a banyan tree, and the south is his direction.

PATTADAKAL: BOTH SIDE BY SIDE

A riverside lesson in stone

Where. Pattadakal, on the Malaprabhā river in northern Karnataka, holds a group of 8th-century temples built under the Chalukyas of Bādāmi, with a Jain temple of the 9th century. UNESCO listed it in 1987.

Two families. Here, within a few hundred metres, are temples with northern curved towers, such as the Pāpanātha, Galaganātha and Kāśīviśvanātha, and temples with southern stepped towers, such as the Virūpākṣa and Mallikārjuna.

The Virūpākṣa. The Virūpākṣa temple was built by Queen Lokamahādevī to celebrate the victory of her husband, King Vikramāditya II, over the Pallavas of Kāñcī. It is a large Drāviḍa temple with a Nandi pavilion and enclosure wall.

What it teaches. Same patrons, same stone, same river, same time, two different skylines. The style was a choice, not simply a matter of region.

A tip. Walk the site and sort each tower first by the outline test; only then read the labels. You will be surprised how quickly the eye learns.

ZAPPY: Why would one dynasty use both?

PROF. BOLT: Scholars still discuss it. Builders from different traditions worked there, and the Chalukyas ruled between north and south. Pattadakal shows their choices side by side.

OTHER SKYLINES

Regional answers to the same question

Odisha. Temples in Odisha are northern in family but have their own names: the curved tower is the rekha deul, and the hall in front, with its stepped pyramid roof, the pīḍha deul. The Liṅgarāja at Bhubaneswar is a famous example.

Kerala. In Kerala heavy rain and timber gave sloping, tiled or metal-sheathed roofs over square, round or apsidal shrines. Our Kerala series opens with a whole article on them.

Bengal. In Bengal, brick temples copied the curved thatched roof of village huts, the chālā roof, and covered the walls in terracotta panels.

Himalaya. In the hills, wooden temples rise in tiers of sloping roofs or use stone-and-timber walls suited to snow and earthquakes.

The lesson. The outline test still works: ask what shape the roof makes and why. Climate, material and tradition all leave their mark on the skyline.

ZAPPY: So there are more than two?

PROF. BOLT: Many more. Nāgara and Drāviḍa are the main families, but India has dozens of regional dialects.

VESARA: THE IN-BETWEEN

Karnataka’s mixed towers

Mixed. The texts name a third type, Vesara, sometimes translated “mule”, a mix of two parents. Scholars use it loosely for towers combining northern and southern features.

Karnataka. Later Chalukya and Hoysala temples in Karnataka often stand on star-shaped plans; their towers keep storeys, but the many projections and close-set tiers blur them into a rounded, almost conical outline.

Look for. Star-shaped plans, many small projections, tiers set so close they read as a curve, and a crown that may be round or bell-shaped.

Part 5. The Hoysala temples of Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura are the best place to study these. Part 5 takes you round their walls.

A caution. “Vesara” is a convenient label, not a strict rule. Describe what you see first; name it later.

ZAPPY: So I should not rush to label?

PROF. BOLT: Never. Describe the outline, the top, the plan and the walls. The label is the last thing you write.

READ A SKYLINE

A five-step routine
Outline. Squint. Curve or storeys? One peak or many?
Top. Ring stone and pot, or dome stone and pot?
Edges. Chains of miniature shrines along tiers, or bands of window motifs?
Plan. Square, stepped, star-shaped, round?
Other towers. Is the tall tower actually a gateway? Trace it down to the ground.

ZAPPY: What if the top is hidden by scaffolding or later work?

PROF. BOLT: Then note what you can see and come back. Temples are repaired and repainted; Part 11 is about that.
