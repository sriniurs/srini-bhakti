HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 7 OF 12 · WATER

Sacred Water and the Temple Plan

Tanks, kuṇḍas and stepwells · explained and illustrated

Where the steps go down

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt follow water through the temple: from the spout beside the sanctum to the tank, the stepped kuṇḍa at Modhera and the great stepwell of Rani-ki-Vav.

WHERE WE ARE

Most temples are built near water, and many make their own. Water is used to bathe the deity, to purify the devotee, to grow flowers, and to keep the town alive through the dry season.

So water leaves marks on the plan: a spout on the sanctum wall, a tank in the court or outside the gate, a stepped pool before a sun temple, a deep stepwell going down storey after storey.

This part shows you how to find these and read what they did.

ZAPPY: Is the tank part of the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Very often, yes. Many temples name their tank as their own sacred water, their tīrtha, and the festival year comes back to it again and again.

ZAPPY: Why so many words?

PROF. BOLT: Because each region built water differently, and each form had a different job: drain, bath, festival, storage.

THE SPOUT BESIDE THE SANCTUM

Where the god’s bath water goes

The story

The bath. Every day the deity is bathed in water and offerings: milk, curd, honey, sandal paste. This is abhiṣeka.
A channel. The water runs off the pedestal into a channel that passes through the sanctum wall.
The spout. On the outer wall it comes out through a stone spout, the praṇāla, often carved as a cow’s mouth (gomukha) or a makara. On Śiva temples it is usually on the north side.
Not stepped over. On a Śiva temple devotees walking round do not step over this channel; they turn back there and complete the circle in reverse.
A clue. When you walk round a sanctum, look for the spout. It tells you where the inner pedestal is, which direction the deity faces, and how worship is done inside.

ZAPPY: Is the water thrown away?

PROF. BOLT: Often it is collected, and some devotees take a drop as blessed water. In some temples it flows into a pit or garden.

Find the spout: it tells you about the worship inside.

THE TEMPLE TANK

A pool with steps on all sides

Everywhere in the South. Most large South Indian temples have a tank: a square or oblong pool with steps going down on all four sides, inside the walls or just outside.

Why steps?. Water levels rise and fall with the seasons. Steps on all sides mean that someone can always reach the water, whether the tank is full or nearly empty.

Many uses. Devotees bathe before entering. Priests draw water for worship. The tank stores rainwater and recharges wells. In festivals it becomes a stage (Part 2).

Read the steps. Count the flights. Look for landings where people stand, small shrines at the edges, and the inlet channels that bring rainwater in.

Kerala ponds. In Kerala the tank is usually a large pond outside the temple, with bathing ghats; the temple’s own article in our Kerala series shows how it fits the plan.

ZAPPY: Why are some tanks dry?

PROF. BOLT: Their inlet channels have been blocked by roads and buildings. Many towns are now reopening channels to bring the tanks back to life.

MODHERA: STEPS DOWN TO THE SUN

A kuṇḍa before a temple

Where. At Modhera in north Gujarat stands a temple to Sūrya, the sun god, built shortly after 1026–27 under the Chaulukya king Bhīma I.

Three parts. It has three parts on one axis: the stepped pool, the Sūrya Kuṇḍa; a separate pillared hall for gatherings and dance; and the shrine with its own hall.

The kuṇḍa. The kuṇḍa is a large rectangular pool reached by flights of steps arranged in terraces, with many small shrines set on the steps for different deities.

Facing the sun. The temple faces east. At the equinoxes the rising sun is said to shine through the doors towards the sanctum, where the image of Sūrya once stood.

Read the axis. Stand at the bottom of the kuṇḍa and look up: water, steps, hall, shrine and sky all lie on one line. Water is the first step of the path inward.

ZAPPY: Is the temple still in worship?

PROF. BOLT: No. The image is gone and the tower is damaged, but the kuṇḍa, the halls and the carvings make it one of the most beautiful places to read a plan.

RANI-KI-VAV, PATAN

A temple turned upside down

Where and who. Rani-ki-Vav, the “Queen’s Stepwell”, at Patan in Gujarat, was built in the 11th century as a memorial to King Bhīmadeva I, traditionally by his queen Udayamati. UNESCO listed it in 2014.

How it works. A stepwell is a well you can walk down into. Long flights of steps go down through pillared storeys to the water table, ending in a deep circular well shaft.

Seven levels. Rani-ki-Vav descends through seven levels of pillared galleries. As you go down, the air gets cooler and the light dimmer.

Sculpture. Its walls carry more than five hundred large sculptures, many of them forms of Viṣṇu, especially his avatāras, with goddesses, sages and celestial women.

Lost and found. Floods of the Sarasvatī river silted it up and it lay buried for centuries. The Archaeological Survey of India cleared it from the 1980s, which is one reason its carving is so well preserved.

ZAPPY: Why build a temple downwards?

PROF. BOLT: Because the water was down there. The builders turned a water store into a descending temple, where every step brings you closer to sacred water.

HOW TO READ A STEPWELL

Light, shade and direction

The long axis. A stepwell is built on one long axis, from the entrance at ground level to the well at the end. Like a temple, it is a path towards something sacred.

Landings. At intervals the stair widens into a landing with a pillared pavilion above. People rested there, out of the sun.

Walls as galleries. The side walls are covered with niches and figures, especially in the lower levels. Read them as you would a temple wall (Part 5), going down rather than round.

Light. Light falls from above and fades as you go down. Sculptors placed the most important figures where the light reaches.

Water level. Look for water marks and silt lines on the walls. They show how high the water once rose in different seasons.

ZAPPY: Are there other stepwells?

PROF. BOLT: Hundreds, especially in Gujarat and Rajasthan: Adalaj near Ahmedabad and the deep Chand Baori at Abhaneri are well known.

WELLS INSIDE THE TEMPLE

Rameswaram’s twenty-two tīrthas

Wells as tīrthas. Some temples hold sacred water not in a tank but in wells inside the enclosure. Each well is a tīrtha with its own name and story.

Rameswaram. At the Rāmanāthasvāmī temple in Rameswaram, pilgrims go from well to well inside the temple, having water from each poured over them, before darśana. The temple counts twenty-two tīrthas within its walls.

Sweet and salty. Pilgrims notice that wells a few steps apart can taste different. Each is named after a deity, sage or sacred place.

Read the route. The wells are visited in a fixed order. Following them is another way of walking the temple plan, from water to water.

Elsewhere. Many temples have a single sacred well in a corner of the court, used for the deity’s bath. Look for a small structure with a pulley.

ZAPPY: Why bathe before darśana?

PROF. BOLT: To arrive clean and cooled, body and mind. At Rameswaram the bath in the sea and the wells is part of the pilgrimage itself.

A TANK THAT GATHERS A CITY

Kumbakonam’s Mahāmaham

Kumbakonam. In Kumbakonam in Tamil Nadu the great Mahāmaham tank is lined with small shrines round its edges, linked with the city’s temples.

Every twelve years. The tank is the centre of the Mahāmaham festival, held about once every twelve years, when huge numbers of pilgrims bathe in it at the chosen time. A smaller festival, Māsimaham, is held every year.

The temples’ water. On the festival day, festival images from the town’s temples are brought to the tank. The tank links many temples into one sacred landscape.

Reading the town. A tank like this is not inside one temple; it belongs to the whole town. Map which temples bring their deities, and from which direction.

Elsewhere. Other towns have such shared tanks too, and riverside ghats serve the same purpose on rivers like the Kāverī and the Gaṅgā.

ZAPPY: Why twelve years?

PROF. BOLT: The timing follows the position of Jupiter in the zodiac, as with the Kumbh Melā. Calendars and water together set the great festivals.

WATER AND CARE TODAY

Reading problems as well as plans

Cut off. Many temple tanks once received water through channels from fields and streets. Roads, drains and buildings have cut these channels, and some tanks have dried or filled with sewage.

Revival. Temples, towns and volunteers have cleaned and reopened many tanks. When the inlet is open again, a tank can fill in a single good monsoon.

What to look for. Inlets, outlets, silt traps, a well at the bottom, steps worn by feet. These tell you how the tank once worked.

Do no harm. Never throw offerings wrapped in plastic into a tank. Follow the temple’s rules on bathing and photography.

Water as heritage. The tank is as much heritage as the tower. Reading a temple includes noticing whether its water is alive.

ZAPPY: Can a tank really fill up again?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Many have, once the channels were reopened. Old engineering, a little care, and the rain does the rest.

READ THE WATER

A routine
Spout. Walk round the sanctum. Where is the spout? What shape?
Tank. Is there a tank? Inside or outside the walls? How many sides have steps?
Flow. Where does water come in and go out?
Shrines. Any small shrines on the steps or a pavilion in the middle?
Festivals. Ask which festivals use the water, and when.

ZAPPY: And at a stepwell?

PROF. BOLT: Walk to the bottom, then back up. Note how the light and air change at each level.
