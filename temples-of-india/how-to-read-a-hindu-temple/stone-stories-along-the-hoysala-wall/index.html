HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 5 OF 12 · HOYSALA

Stone Stories Along the Hoysala Wall

Platforms, friezes and bracket figures · explained and illustrated

Read it band by band

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt walk round the Hoysala temples of Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura, reading the wall from the platform up: animals, scrolls, epics, gods and dancers.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 4 we read towers. Now we go close, to a wall where almost no surface is left plain.

Between the 11th and 14th centuries the Hoysala kings ruled much of present-day Karnataka. Their temples, built of a fine-grained greyish-green stone, are covered with carving so dense that visitors often do not know where to look.

The secret is that the wall is organised. It is built in bands, each with its own subject, from the platform at your feet to the eaves above your head. Read it one band at a time.

ZAPPY: Where are these temples?

PROF. BOLT: The three best known, Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura, are a short drive from Mysuru and Hassan. UNESCO listed them together in 2023 as the Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas.

NavaraṅgaThe central hall, with four central pillars and nine bays
Lathe-turned pillarA pillar with rings and bells turned on a lathe
SoapstoneThe fine, soft-to-carve chloritic schist the Hoysalas used

ZAPPY: Why soapstone?

PROF. BOLT: It is fine-grained and can be cut into tiny details. It is softer when quarried and is commonly said to harden on exposure, which suits deep carving.

THE WALL IN THREE ZONES

Where to start

The story

Start at your feet. You walk round on a platform, the jagatī. Its edge follows the temple’s star-shaped plan, so you see the wall turning in and out like a crown.
Lower zone. Just above the platform, the base of the temple is made of long horizontal bands, called friezes. Each runs round the whole building with one subject.
Middle zone. Above them, the wall itself carries large figures of gods and goddesses, each in its own frame under a little canopy.
Upper zone. Under the projecting eaves, angled brackets hold figures of women dancing, dressing or making music: the madanikās.
Then the roof. Above the eaves, the tower, where it survives, repeats the star plan in tiers. At Belur and Halebidu the original towers are lost.

ZAPPY: Is there a correct direction to walk?

PROF. BOLT: Clockwise, as devotees do. Many epic friezes are carved to be read in that direction.

Platform, friezes, gods, dancers, tower: read from the ground up.

THE FRIEZES AT HALEBIDU

Eight bands round the base

The Hoysaleśvara. The Hoysaleśvara temple at Halebidu, the Hoysala capital Dvārasamudra, was begun around 1121 during the reign of Viṣṇuvardhana. It has two shrines side by side, for Hoysaleśvara and Śāntaleśvara.

From the bottom. Its base carries a famous sequence of friezes, usually listed from the bottom as elephants, lions, a scroll, horsemen, another scroll, scenes from the epics and Purāṇas, makaras (sea creatures), and haṃsas (swans).

Strength to grace. Notice the order: heavy, strong animals at the bottom, carrying the building; lighter, more graceful ones above; stories in between at eye level.

Each animal different. Walk along the elephant frieze. Every elephant is in a slightly different pose; some have riders. The sculptors avoided mechanical repetition.

Find the story band. The epic band is at eye height. Stand back, find a character you know, and follow the story round the corners.

ZAPPY: How long are these bands?

PROF. BOLT: They run round the whole star-shaped building, turning dozens of corners. Walk one round and you have walked a long picture book.

READING A STORY ROUND THE CORNERS

The epic band

The epics. The stories come from the Rāmāyaṇa, the Mahābhārata, the Bhāgavata Purāṇa and the lives of Śiva and Viṣṇu. Many scenes are dramatic: battles, abductions, a god lifting a mountain.

Recognise by objects. Look for the clue each scene carries: a golden deer for the Rāmāyaṇa, Govardhana held up like an umbrella for Kṛṣṇa, Arjuna with his bow, the churning of the ocean with a serpent as rope.

Turning corners. The band turns in and out with the star plan. A single scene may wrap round a point; step back to see it whole.

Read with a friend. One reads the scene; the other tells the story from memory. It is the oldest way of using these walls.

When you are lost. Note one recognisable figure and its position (which side, how far from the door), and look it up later. Field notes save the day.

ZAPPY: Do all the stories go in order?

PROF. BOLT: Some sections follow an epic in sequence; others jump between stories. Scholars have mapped many of them. Read what you can recognise first.

THE GODS ON THE WALL

Large figures in the middle zone

Big figures. Above the friezes, on the wall itself, stand large images of gods and goddesses, each framed by pilasters and crowned with a little canopy of foliage.

Identify by attributes. Look at what each holds. Viṣṇu: discus and conch; Śiva: trident, drum, deer; Brahmā: four faces, book; Durgā: weapons, lion or buffalo; Gaṇeśa: elephant head, sweet.

Many forms. Hoysala walls love the many forms of Viṣṇu, such as the 24 forms named by how they hold their four emblems, as well as Narasiṃha, Varāha and dancing goddesses.

Jewellery. The ornaments are carved so finely that you can count the beads. This was the soapstone’s great gift.

Signatures. Look at the base of some images: a short label gives the name of the sculptor. Part of the pleasure of these walls is meeting their makers.

ZAPPY: Why so many forms of Viṣṇu?

PROF. BOLT: Belur and Somanathapura are Viṣṇu temples, built by kings and generals devoted to him. The walls are a gallery of his forms.

THE BRACKET FIGURES

Madanikās at Belur

Belur. The Chennakeśava temple at Belur was commissioned by King Viṣṇuvardhana in 1117. Under its eaves are the famous madanikās, bracket figures of women.

How they sit. Each is carved from a single stone and set at an angle between the top of a pillar or wall and the eaves, leaning outwards as if stepping off the building.

What they do. They dance, play drums and flutes, hold a parrot, wring their wet hair, look in a mirror, hunt with a bow. The mirror-holder, Darpaṇa Sundarī, is the most famous.

Inside too. A few madanikās are inside the hall, on top of the central pillars, where the light falls on them through the doorways.

Read them as a set. Count them, note their activities, and see how they turn the edge of the roof into a garland of life round the god’s house.

ZAPPY: Are they goddesses?

PROF. BOLT: They are celestial or idealised women, auspicious figures that bring beauty and life to the temple. They are not the deity worshipped inside.

INSIDE: TURNED PILLARS AND CEILINGS

The dark, glossy hall

Lathe-turned. Inside, the pillars of the hall look as if made on a giant potter’s wheel: rings, bells and discs, perfectly round and polished. They were turned on a lathe, the stone rotated against a cutting tool.

Each one different. No two are quite alike. At Belur one richly carved pillar is named after Narasiṃha, covered with tiny figures.

The navaraṅga. The hall’s centre is the navaraṅga, “nine bays”: four central pillars make one central bay surrounded by eight. The central bay is often used for dance and music.

Ceilings. Look up. Each bay has a carved ceiling, often a deep, layered dome ending in a hanging lotus bud, sometimes with gods round it.

Darkness and gloss. The polished stone glows in lamplight. The inner hall is meant to be seen in half-darkness, by flame.

ZAPPY: How did they turn such heavy stones?

PROF. BOLT: The likely method was to mount the shaft horizontally and rotate it against a fixed cutting edge. Exactly how is still discussed.

MEETING THE MAKERS

Signatures in stone

Named artists. Hoysala temples are unusual for how often sculptors signed their work. Short labels on pedestals give a name, sometimes a home town and a title.

At Belur. Belur’s madanikās and wall images carry names such as Dāsoja of Balligāve and his son Cāvaṇa, among others.

At Somanathapura. Mallitamma’s name appears on many images there, which is why scholars think he led a large share of the work.

Workshops. Signed or not, the walls were made by teams: masters, assistants, apprentices. Different hands show in faces, jewellery and proportions.

How to look. Kneel at the base of a wall figure and look for a short line of letters. Photograph it and the image together; the pair is a little document of authorship.

ZAPPY: Did all Indian sculptors sign?

PROF. BOLT: No, most did not. That is why these Hoysala labels are so treasured: they give us people, not just “the artist”.

SOMANATHAPURA: A COMPLETE SET

Three towers, one hall

When. The Keśava temple at Somanathapura, near Mysuru, was built in 1268 by Somanātha, a general of the Hoysala king Narasiṃha III.

Three shrines. It is a trikūṭa: three shrines open off one hall, each with its own star-shaped tower. Unlike Belur and Halebidu, the towers survive, so you see the whole design.

A cloister. The temple stands in a court lined with a pillared cloister of small cells, entered through a gateway hall.

Signed work. Many images carry the names of their sculptors. Mallitamma is one of the best known.

Read it all. Here you can practise everything: plan (Part 1), towers (Part 4) and the banded wall (this part) on one compact building.

ZAPPY: Why three shrines?

PROF. BOLT: Each held a form of Viṣṇu: Keśava, Janārdana and Veṇugopāla. The central Keśava image is missing, and the temple is no longer in worship.

READ A HOYSALA WALL

A routine
Platform. Walk round once on the jagatī. Count the star points on one side.
Friezes. List the bands from the bottom. Which animals, which scrolls?
Story band. Find one recognisable scene. Note its side and position.
Gods. Identify three wall figures by what they hold.
Eaves and inside. Count the bracket figures; then go in and look at the pillars and one ceiling.

ZAPPY: How long does this take?

PROF. BOLT: Give each temple at least two hours. You will still want to come back.
