HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 9 OF 12 · PAINTINGS

Temple Walls That Carry Paintings

Layers of paint from Thanjavur to Lepakshi · explained and illustrated

One wall, two centuries of paint

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt learn how temple paintings were made, find the Chola murals hidden under a later layer at Thanjavur, look up at the Lepakshi ceilings, and learn how to look without harming.

WHERE WE ARE

We tend to imagine Indian temples as bare grey stone. Many were once bright with colour: painted walls, ceilings, sculptures and stucco.

Paint is fragile. Smoke, damp, bats, whitewash and repainting have destroyed most of it. What survives is precious, and often hides in dark passages and high ceilings.

This part teaches you how temple paintings were made, where to look for them, and how to read the layers one painting can hide beneath another.

ZAPPY: Were sculptures painted too?

PROF. BOLT: Very often. Traces of colour survive on many stone and stucco figures. Today’s brightly painted gopuras continue that old habit.

ZAPPY: Where did blue come from?

PROF. BOLT: Blue was rarer and costlier. Many South Indian murals manage with ochres, white, black and green, with blue appearing more in later periods.

HOW A MURAL WAS MADE

From bare stone to a painted wall

The story

Prepare. The stone or brick wall was covered with layers of lime plaster, the last one fine and smooth.
Draw. The painter drew outlines, often in red ochre, sometimes correcting them; corrections can still be seen in some murals.
Colour. Flat areas of colour were filled in, then shading and details added, and finally strong dark outlines.
Wet or dry. If paint went on while the plaster was wet, the colour bound into the surface as it set: true fresco. Painting on dry plaster needed a binder and is more fragile. Many temple murals combine both.
Polish. Some traditions burnished the surface to a soft shine. Good lime plaster lasts for centuries if kept dry.

ZAPPY: What kills a mural?

PROF. BOLT: Damp most of all, then smoke, soot, bats, salt, careless cleaning and repainting.

Plaster, line, colour, outline: and keep it dry.

THANJAVUR: A PAINTING UNDER A PAINTING

The Chola murals in the dark passage

Where. Inside the Bṛhadīśvara temple at Thanjavur, a dark passage runs round the sanctum between two walls. Its walls were painted in the time of Rājarāja I, around 1010.

Covered over. About six centuries later, in the Nāyaka period, the walls were repainted, and a new painting was laid directly over the Chola one.

Found. In 1931 S. K. Govindasami of Annamalai University noticed the older painting showing through and identified the Chola murals beneath.

What they show. Among the scenes are Śiva as Tripurāntaka destroying the three flying cities, celestial dancers, and the story of the saint Sundarar and the Chēra king travelling to Kailāsa. One panel is widely identified as Rājarāja with his teacher.

Two layers, both valuable. Conservators have had to balance the two: the later paintings are history too. In places the upper layer has been carefully removed and preserved so the Chola layer can be seen.

ZAPPY: Can visitors see them?

PROF. BOLT: The passage is usually closed to the public, to protect the paintings. Photographs and books are the way most people see them.

LEPAKSHI: LOOK UP

Vijayanagara ceilings

Where. The Vīrabhadra temple at Lepakshi, in Andhra Pradesh, was built in the 16th century under the Vijayanagara empire by two brothers, Virūpaṇṇa and Vīraṇṇa.

The ceilings. Its halls have some of the finest surviving Vijayanagara paintings, mostly on the ceilings, in long strips and large panels.

A great image. Over one hall a huge painting of Vīrabhadra, the fierce form of Śiva, fills the ceiling. It is among the largest painted figures in Indian temple art.

Style. Figures stand in profile with large eyes, crisp dark outlines and flat colour, wearing richly patterned textiles. The costume details are a record of 16th-century dress.

Stories. Panels show the epics, the life of Śiva, and the donors themselves. Look for patrons with folded hands at the edge of a scene.

ZAPPY: Why on the ceilings?

PROF. BOLT: The ceilings were the largest dry, protected surfaces, out of reach of hands, lamp smoke and damp. That is also why they survived.

KERALA MURALS

Colour inside the shrine walls

A strong tradition. Kerala has a living tradition of temple mural painting, on the walls of the sanctum and the surrounding buildings, usually on lime plaster under the deep eaves.

Colours. Kerala murals use a characteristic palette of ochre red, yellow, green, white and black, with figures richly ornamented and densely packed.

Where to see. Temples such as Ettumanoor and Vaikom are known for their murals. The Mattancherry and Padmanabhapuram palaces have famous mural rooms too.

Still painted. Artists trained in the tradition still paint new murals and restore old ones, so you may see the same style in a brand-new temple.

Read the eaves. Look under the eaves on the outer walls of the sanctum. Murals there are protected from rain by the roof (see our Kerala series).

ZAPPY: Is the green paint from leaves?

PROF. BOLT: Traditionally green came from mineral and plant sources; the tradition guards its recipes. Many modern restorers use natural pigments.

NĀYAKA CEILINGS

Long painted stories

Strips of story. From the 16th to the 18th century, Nāyaka and Maratha patrons in Tamil Nadu painted long story strips on ceilings and corridor walls, often with short captions in Tamil or Telugu.

Chidambaram and others. Painted ceilings survive in the Śivakāmasundarī shrine at Chidambaram, in parts of Madurai, Srirangam, Kumbakonam and many smaller temples.

Local legends. Besides the epics, they tell the temple’s own story: how the deity appeared, the saints who sang there, the miracles of the place.

Read the captions. Even if you cannot read the language, the boxes show where one scene ends and the next begins. Follow them in order.

Watch for repainting. Many old ceilings have been overpainted with bright modern enamel. Compare colours and line: thin, careful lines and earth colours are usually older.

ZAPPY: Is repainting bad?

PROF. BOLT: It keeps a temple alive and bright, which matters to worshippers. But when it covers old work completely, a piece of history is lost. Good conservation finds a balance.

WHAT PAINTINGS TELL US

Dress, music and daily life

Dress. Painted figures show how people dressed: the patterns on textiles, the way a sari or a turban was tied, the jewellery. Lepakshi is especially rich in textile designs.

Music. Look for instruments: drums of many shapes, stringed instruments, cymbals, flutes, conches. Murals are one of our best records of temple music.

Dance. Dancers are shown in poses that can be compared with dance texts and with living dance forms.

Patrons. Painters often included the donors: a king and queen, a minister, a merchant family, with folded hands near the deity.

Everyday life. Markets, boats, horses, palaces, houses and gardens appear in the background of the stories. Read the background as carefully as the gods.

ZAPPY: So paintings are history books?

PROF. BOLT: Picture history books, if you read them carefully, and remember the painters were showing an ideal world, not a photograph.

TRACES OF COLOUR ON STONE

Painted sculpture and early fragments

Pallava traces. The Kailāsanātha temple at Kāñcīpuram, built by the Pallavas around 700, still has faint traces of painting in its small cells. At Panamalai, also Pallava, a painted goddess survives on a shrine wall.

Coloured stone. Many sculptures were once painted. Look in the deep folds and corners for faint red, yellow or blue where weather and cleaning have not reached.

Stucco. Stucco figures on towers and walls were always painted. Their bright colours are the most visible part of the painting tradition today.

Don’t touch. Never rub or wet a surface to see colour better. Oils from your fingers and water can damage the traces for ever.

Photograph. Use natural light and no flash where the temple asks. Flash fades and damages old pigments over time.

ZAPPY: So temples were colourful?

PROF. BOLT: Far more than we imagine. The grey stone we see today is often a temple that has lost its paint.

CONSERVING A PAINTED WALL

Why some passages are closed

Enemies. Damp rising through walls, leaking roofs, lamp soot, bat droppings, salts and touching hands are the main dangers to temple paintings.

Care. Conservators clean soot carefully, consolidate flaking paint, control damp and light, and sometimes close passages to visitors.

Two layers. Where two layers survive, as at Thanjavur, conservators must decide which to show and how to save both.

Your part. Do not touch, do not use flash where asked, do not lean on painted walls, and do not light lamps or incense near them.

Living temples. In living temples, worship continues alongside conservation. Good care respects both the devotee and the painting.

ZAPPY: Who looks after them?

PROF. BOLT: The Archaeological Survey of India, state archaeology departments, temple authorities and specialist conservators, often working together.

READ A PAINTED WALL

A routine
Find. Look under eaves, in passages, on ceilings, inside shrines and porches.
Surface. Plaster on stone, brick or wood? Signs of damp or flaking?
Layers. Any places where one painting shows through another?
Style. Earth colours and fine line, or bright modern enamel?
Story. Which scenes? Captions? Donors with folded hands?

ZAPPY: What if the light is bad?

PROF. BOLT: Wait for your eyes to adjust, use a small torch only if allowed, and never flash. Come back at a different time of day.
