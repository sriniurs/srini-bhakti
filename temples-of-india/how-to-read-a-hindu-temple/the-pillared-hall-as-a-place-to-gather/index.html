HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 8 OF 12 · MAṆḌAPAS

The Pillared Hall

Maṇḍapas, bays and the people who gather in them · explained and illustrated

Where the temple meets its people

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt read the maṇḍapa: how pillars and beams make bays, what each kind of hall is for, and how to read the carved pillars, from Hoysala lathe-work to Vijayanagara horses and yāḷis.

WHERE WE ARE

The sanctum is small, dark and for the god. The maṇḍapa, the pillared hall, is for people.

Here devotees wait for darśana, priests chant, musicians play, dancers perform, couples marry, scholars teach, and the festival image rests in state. A temple without halls is a shrine; a temple with halls is a meeting place.

Halls can be small porches or vast spaces with hundreds of pillars. This part shows how to read them.

ZAPPY: Why are the halls so full of pillars?

PROF. BOLT: Because stone beams are short. Each beam spans only from one pillar to the next, so a big hall needs a forest of them. Part 6 showed the engineering; here we read the use.

ZAPPY: So every grid of dots on a plan is a hall?

PROF. BOLT: Almost always. Count the rows and columns and you know the bays; find the widest aisle and you know the axis.

COUNTING BAYS

How a hall is put together

The story

One bay. Four pillars, four beams and a ceiling slab make one bay: the basic unit of every hall.
Nine bays. Sixteen pillars in a four-by-four grid make nine bays. The central bay is special: wider, higher, with the finest ceiling. This is the navaraṅga plan of many Karnataka temples.
Many bays. Add more rows and columns and the hall grows. Count them: a 7 × 7 grid of pillars makes 36 bays.
Find the aisle. In most halls the central aisle, on the temple axis, is wider than the others. It is the path to the sanctum.
Find the stage. Look for a raised platform, a wider central bay, or a carved ceiling. That is where dance, music or the festival image goes.

ZAPPY: Does the ceiling help?

PROF. BOLT: Very much. The richest ceiling marks the most important bay. Always look up.

Four pillars make a bay; the best ceiling marks the centre.

A HALL FOR EVERY PURPOSE

What happens in maṇḍapas

Waiting and worship. The halls in front of the sanctum hold devotees during worship, when the doors open and the lamps are waved.

Dance and music. A nṛtta or raṅga maṇḍapa was built for dance and music offered to the deity. Temple dance was a daily ritual in many South Indian temples.

Weddings. The kalyāṇa maṇḍapa hosts the annual wedding festival of the god and goddess, and often human weddings too.

Resting the god. During festivals the festival image is placed in a hall built for the purpose: a spring pavilion, a swing hall, a hall where it is decorated and shown.

Learning. Halls also served as places of teaching, recitation and discussion; inscriptions record endowments for reciting texts in them.

ZAPPY: How do I know which hall is which?

PROF. BOLT: Ask the temple; many halls have names on boards. If not, look for clues: a swing hook, a wedding dais, a Nandi, a stage.

THE VEHICLE’S PAVILION

Nandi and Garuḍa on the axis

On the axis. Between the gate and the sanctum, on the axis, many Śiva temples have a pavilion holding Nandi, the bull, facing the sanctum. Viṣṇu temples have Garuḍa in the same place.

Always looking. The vehicle gazes at the deity without interruption. Devotees often look at the deity between Nandi’s horns.

Size and date. At Thanjavur the great Nandi and its pavilion are later additions of the Nāyaka period. At Lepakshi a huge monolithic Nandi lies some distance from the temple.

Read the line. Stand behind the vehicle and look along its gaze. If it does not line up with the sanctum, ask why: a later shrine, a moved image, a second axis.

Whispering. Many devotees whisper their wishes into Nandi’s ear, believing he will pass them to Śiva.

ZAPPY: Is the Nandi always so big?

PROF. BOLT: No, it varies from a small figure on a pedestal to a giant pavilion. The idea is constant: the devotee who never looks away.

THE THOUSAND-PILLARED HALLS

Halls that are forests

A Nāyaka speciality. From the 16th century, under the Nāyakas and Vijayanagara rulers, South Indian temples built vast halls with hundreds of pillars. Tamil calls them āyirakkāl maṇḍapam, “thousand-pillar hall”.

Madurai. At the Mīnākṣī temple in Madurai the thousand-pillar hall was built in the 16th century, traditionally by Ariyanātha Mudaliyār, minister of the Nāyaka rulers. It is now partly a temple museum.

Not exactly a thousand. “Thousand” means “very many”: the actual count varies, and guides argue over it happily.

Why so big?. These halls held festival crowds, the god’s wedding, recitations and gatherings. They are the temple’s public square, roofed in stone.

Read the rows. Walk down one aisle and look across: from every angle the pillars line up in new patterns. The builders designed those views.

ZAPPY: Is a thousand-pillar hall the biggest kind?

PROF. BOLT: Among halls, yes. But the corridors of Rameswaram are longer still. Turn the page.

THE CORRIDORS OF RAMESWARAM

Halls stretched into avenues

Rāmanāthasvāmī. The Rāmanāthasvāmī temple at Rameswaram is famous for its corridors, which run round the temple between raised platforms lined with pillars.

Long. The third corridor is often described as the longest temple corridor in India. Standing at one end, the rows of pillars seem to go on for ever.

Platforms. The pillars stand on raised platforms on either side, leaving a lower walkway in the middle for processions and devotees.

Ceilings. The ceilings are painted with medallions, and the pillars carry carved brackets and figures. Many were built or extended by the Sētupati rulers of Ramnad in the 17th–18th centuries.

A hall that is a path. Here the hall and the circumambulation path become one: the people gather and walk at the same time.

ZAPPY: Why build such long corridors?

PROF. BOLT: For processions in all weather, shade from the coastal sun, and space for the many pilgrims who come to Rameswaram.

HALLS IN THE NORTH AND EAST

Balconies, dance halls and offering halls

Balconies. Many northern temples, as at Khajurāho, have halls with open sides and sloping seat-backs between the pillars (kakṣāsana), letting in light and air and offering a place to sit.

Stepped roofs. The roofs of northern halls rise in a pyramid of tiers or a bell shape, stepping up towards the tower (Part 1).

Modhera. At the Sun temple at Modhera (Part 7) a separate, richly carved pillared hall stands in front of the shrine, between it and the kuṇḍa.

Odisha. Odisha temples line up as many as four halls on the axis: the shrine (deul), the porch-hall (jagamohana), a dance hall (nāṭa mandira) and an offering hall (bhoga maṇḍapa).

Same purpose. Different shapes, same job: to gather people before the god, give space to music and dance, and prepare them for darśana.

ZAPPY: Why separate halls in Odisha?

PROF. BOLT: Each hall had its own ritual: waiting, dance, offering food. Building them one after another along the axis gave each its own place.

READING A PILLAR

From Hoysala rings to Vijayanagara horses

Base, shaft, capital. Read a pillar from the bottom: a base, a shaft (often square, eight- or sixteen-sided in alternation) and a capital with a bracket that receives the beam.

Hoysala. In Hoysala halls (Part 5) pillars were turned on a lathe into gleaming rings and bells.

Chola. Chola pillars are often square with chamfered sections and carved medallions, solid and restrained.

Vijayanagara and Nāyaka. From the 15th century pillars became sculptures in themselves: rearing horses with riders, and yāḷis, lion-like mythical beasts, carved in the round from the same block as the shaft.

Small panels. Many pillars carry small panels with deities, donors, dancers and stories. Kneel and look; some show the people who paid for them.

ZAPPY: Why horses?

PROF. BOLT: Horses were the prized imports of the Vijayanagara army. Carving them in the temple showed power, wealth and protection.

PILLARS THAT SING

The Viṭṭhala temple at Hampi

Hampi. The Viṭṭhala temple at Hampi, in the Vijayanagara capital, is famous for the slender stone columns clustered round the main pillars of its great hall.

Musical. When tapped, these columns ring with different notes, which is why they are called the musical pillars. Tapping is now forbidden to protect them.

How?. The tone depends on the stone, the column’s length and thickness, and how it is attached. Scholars and engineers have studied them, and the full explanation is still discussed.

The stone chariot. In the same courtyard stands the famous stone chariot, a shrine for Garuḍa built in the form of a chariot. Part 10 looks at it.

A hall for music. Read the hall as the temple did: a place where music and dance were offered to the deity, in an architecture that could itself make sound.

ZAPPY: Can I tap one?

PROF. BOLT: Please don’t. Centuries of tapping have damaged them, and the Archaeological Survey of India protects them now. Listen with your eyes.

READ A HALL

A routine
Count. How many rows and columns of pillars? How many bays?
Axis. Which aisle is widest? Does it point to the sanctum?
Centre. Where is the best ceiling? A stage, a platform?
Pillars. Turned, panelled or sculptural? Any donors carved on them?
Use. Ask what happens here today. Then imagine it on a festival day.

ZAPPY: Is sitting allowed?

PROF. BOLT: In many halls, yes, quietly. Sitting a while is the best way to understand a place built for gathering.
