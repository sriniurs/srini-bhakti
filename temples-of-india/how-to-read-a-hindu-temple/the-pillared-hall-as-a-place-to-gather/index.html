THE SPACE BETWEEN COLUMNS

Draw a pair of pillars, then mark what can happen between them. The interval is a bay. In a row of bays, some can lead traffic past one another, others frame a central opening, and some may hold objects or people during a ceremony. Draw the supports on a plan and leave the bays empty until you know which areas are accessible.

ZAPPY: Why draw the empty space first?

PROF. BOLT: The columns are the visible landmarks. The intervals tell you how a hall can actually be used.

THREE VIEWS OF ONE HALL

From the middle, test a straight view toward the shrine. From a corner, test a diagonal view that crosses several rows. From a doorway, notice whether pillars break the view into alternating openings and obstructions. Each position makes a different hall visible. On paper, draw sightlines from the same three points. A pillar hidden behind another in a photograph still belongs to the plan.

A HALL SHAPED AS A CHARIOT

At the Airāvateśvara complex, UNESCO identifies axial halls and the chariot form of the front maṇḍapa. Study the wheels in relation to the platform and architectural mass. This is the representation of a vehicle in stone. A living temple may also have portable ritual carriers, which involve different materials and arrangements. Read the stone form and the moving equipment on their own terms. [1]

A FREESTANDING HALL CHANGES THE ROUTE

At Modhera, consult the site description to place the sabhāmaṇḍapa among the shrine and the approach toward the tank. An interval between buildings can let you enter a hall from another side or pause outside before moving onward. Your plan should preserve the open ground. Filling it with a single arrow can conceal the alternatives it creates. [2]

EVIDENCE FOR WHAT HAPPENED THERE

A hall's capacity for movement or gathering does not establish a specific use for every period. Look for a name in a contemporary record, a dated image, a temple account or a documented performance. Describe what the layout permits separately from what people are recorded doing. This is especially important when a modern label such as “dance hall” seems to explain an entire building by itself.

FIELD EXERCISE

A sequence you can follow on a drawing or at the site
  1. 01Outside edge
  2. 02First bay
  3. 03Open centre
  4. 04Far threshold

Try placing two observers at opposite edges of a hall. Draw the diagonal lines from each to the far doorway. If columns interrupt one line but leave the other clear, the apparent symmetry of the building will not produce a symmetrical view from those two positions. A good plan helps explain why photographs disagree.

Now try an object rather than an observer. Sketch the clearance around a hypothetical carrier moving between two rows. The smallest bay or turning space matters more than the overall area. This is a way to ask informed questions about ceremony. Use a documented example to claim a particular carrier followed the route.

ZAPPY: Why record where the roof changes height?

PROF. BOLT: The vertical clearance can change what fits underneath, even when the path looks open on the plan.
