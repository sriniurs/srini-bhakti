HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 3 OF 12 · INSCRIPTIONS

What a Temple Inscription Can Tell Us

Lamps, land, donors and the words in stone · explained and illustrated

The wall that keeps the accounts

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt learn to find, date and read the writing cut into temple walls, and discover that it is mostly about gifts: lamps, land, gold, cattle and people.

WHERE WE ARE

Walk round almost any old South Indian temple and you will see lines of letters cut into the base of the walls, the pillars, even the floor. Most visitors walk past.

Those lines are inscriptions. They are the temple’s own records: who built it, who gave what, which village owed rice, which family kept the lamp burning, which musician was paid.

You do not need to read the old scripts to use them. This guide shows you how to find inscriptions, what kinds there are, what they usually say and where to look up a translation.

ZAPPY: Are inscriptions like signboards?

PROF. BOLT: More like account books and legal deeds, carved in stone so that no one could change them. They tell us about people as much as about kings.

AT A GLANCE

Inscriptions in one table
ŚāsanaA record or charter; a stone record is a śilāśāsana, a copper-plate one a tāmraśāsana
MeykkīrttiThe poetic royal eulogy that opens many Chola inscriptions, listing the king’s deeds
Regnal yearThe year of the king’s reign, the usual way of dating a Chola inscription
EndowmentA gift that pays for something for ever: a perpetual lamp, a daily offering, a festival
Sabhā / ūrVillage assemblies: the sabhā of a brahmin village, the ūr of an ordinary one
ImprecationThe closing curse on anyone who breaks the gift
ScriptsTamil, Grantha, Vaṭṭeḻuttu, Kannada, Telugu, Nāgarī and others, often more than one on the same wall

ZAPPY: Which language?

PROF. BOLT: In the Tamil country mostly Tamil, often with Sanskrit verses in Grantha script. Elsewhere Kannada, Telugu, Sanskrit and others.

FINDING AN INSCRIPTION

Where the words hide

The story

Look low. Most temple inscriptions are on the base mouldings of the sanctum and halls, at knee or waist height, running round the building in long lines.
Look everywhere. They also appear on pillars, door frames, the steps of tanks, slabs set up in the court, and on the pedestals of bronzes and images.
Notice the layout. A long inscription may run round a whole building, line after line, continuing round corners. Short ones sit in a single panel.
Do not touch. Never chalk, paint or rub an inscription to make it clearer. Photograph it in low, slanting light, which shows the letters best.
Look it up. Most important inscriptions have been copied and published, many in the series South Indian Inscriptions and Epigraphia Indica, and in the ASI’s annual reports on epigraphy.

ZAPPY: Who copies them?

PROF. BOLT: The Archaeological Survey of India has an epigraphy branch, based in Mysuru, that has made paper copies (estampages) of tens of thousands of inscriptions.

Look low, photograph in slanting light, then look up the published text.

HOW AN INSCRIPTION IS BUILT

The parts in order

Opening. Many inscriptions open with an auspicious word or verse, such as svasti śrī, “hail, prosperity”.

The king. Then comes the king. Chola inscriptions from Rājarāja I’s time onwards begin with a meykkīrtti, a set poem listing his conquests; scholars use these to date and identify kings.

The date. The date is given as the king’s regnal year, often with the day, month, star or weekday, which lets scholars work out the exact date.

The gift. Next: who gave, what, to which god, and for what purpose. This is the heart of most inscriptions.

Terms and guard. Then the terms (who manages the gift, what they must do each day), sometimes the witnesses, and finally a curse on anyone who breaks the gift.

ZAPPY: Why the curse?

PROF. BOLT: Because the gift had to last for ever, long after the giver died. The curse was the stone’s security guard.

A LAMP THAT NEVER GOES OUT

Reading a lamp endowment

The commonest gift. The single most common gift recorded on Tamil temple walls is a perpetual lamp: a lamp kept burning before the deity day and night.

Paid in animals. A lamp needs ghee. At Thanjavur, inscriptions from Rājarāja’s time record that each perpetual lamp was endowed with 96 ewes, or 48 cows, or 16 she-buffaloes, given to shepherds who had to supply a fixed measure of ghee every day.

Who did the work. The inscription names the shepherds who took the animals, and makes them and their families responsible. The animals were described as “not dying, not ageing”: the herd had to be kept up for ever.

People in the stone. So a lamp record tells you about a donor, a priest, a shepherd family, a village and an economy of milk and ghee. A whole social world sits in a few lines.

Look for the lamp. Next time you see a row of lamps in a temple, remember that once each one may have had a herd and a family behind it.

ZAPPY: Do the lamps still burn?

PROF. BOLT: Many temples still keep lamps lit before the deity, paid for in new ways. The old arrangements ended long ago, but the stone keeps the promise visible.

THE TEMPLE AS AN EMPLOYER

Thanjavur’s list of people

A great list. On the walls of the Bṛhadīśvara temple, Rājarāja I had inscribed lists of the people attached to the temple, with their duties and their pay in land shares.

Who they were. One long inscription lists about four hundred women dancers brought from other temples, and men who served the temple: dance masters, singers, drummers, conch-blowers, lamp-lighters, watchmen, tailors, goldsmiths, carpenters, accountants and others.

Paid in land. Each person received shares in the produce of temple land. The inscription names them, their village, their role and their share. Some are recorded with their family’s right to the post after them.

A city’s heart. Read this way, the temple is a great employer and a centre of the town’s life, not only a place of prayer.

Other temples. Many other temples record similar lists, smaller in scale. Look for them on base mouldings and on slabs in the court.

ZAPPY: Did all temples have so many people?

PROF. BOLT: No, Thanjavur was a royal foundation on an enormous scale. A village temple might have a priest, a garland-maker and a lamp.

LAND, FOOD AND FLOWERS

The other gifts on the wall

Land. The next most common gift after lamps is land, or a share of its harvest. The inscription gives the field’s boundaries, its size and the tax it is freed from, so it reads like a land deed.

Food. Many gifts pay for naivedya, the food offered to the deity: so many measures of rice a day, with ghee, curd, plantains or sugar. The record may give the recipe as well as the quantity.

Flowers. Others pay for a nandavana, a flower garden, and the gardeners who tend it, so that garlands reach the deity every day.

Festivals. Some gifts fund a single day: a festival, a bath of the deity on a star day, the recitation of hymns. Many Chola records pay singers to recite the Tamil hymns of the Tēvāram before the god.

Gold and jewels. Royal records list ornaments given to the bronzes, with their weight and stones, as carefully as a jeweller’s ledger.

ZAPPY: So the walls are like the temple’s budget?

PROF. BOLT: A budget written in stone, gift by gift, over centuries. Reading it shows what people valued: light, food, flowers, music.

WHEN A VILLAGE WROTE ITS RULES

The Uttaramērūr inscriptions

Where. At Uttaramērūr near Kāñcīpuram, the walls of a village temple carry inscriptions of the 10th century, from the reign of the Chola king Parāntaka I.

What they record. They set out the rules by which the village sabhā, its assembly, chose the members of its committees: who could stand, who could not, how long they served.

The pot. Names were written on palm leaves, put in a pot, and drawn out by a young boy before the assembly. The method is called kuḍavōlai, “pot-leaf”.

Why on a temple?. The temple wall was the most public, permanent and respected surface in the village. Putting the rules there made them binding and visible to all.

The lesson. A temple wall can be a village’s constitution. Inscriptions are not only about gods and kings; they are about how people organised their lives.

ZAPPY: Was it democracy?

PROF. BOLT: Not as we mean it today: only some men of the village could stand. But it is a remarkable record of rules, terms and lots, a thousand years old.

USING INSCRIPTIONS TO DATE A TEMPLE

A careful detective’s rules

The rule of the earliest. An inscription on a wall shows that the wall existed when the words were cut. The earliest inscription on a building gives a date by which it was built.

Not always the builder. Many inscriptions record later gifts to an existing temple. Do not assume the king named was the builder; check what the text says.

Watch for moves. Stones were sometimes reused in rebuilding, so an inscription can end up upside down, cut off or in the wrong place. A broken line is a clue to rebuilding.

Copies. Some temples recopied older records when they rebuilt a wall, sometimes saying so in the text. The script and wording then belong to the later date.

Combine clues. Use inscriptions together with style, building joints and the plan (Part 11 shows how). No one clue settles a date on its own.

ZAPPY: So inscriptions can mislead?

PROF. BOLT: Only if we read them carelessly. Read the whole record, note where it sits, and ask the published experts.

STONE AND COPPER

Records that travelled

Copper plates. Many land grants were engraved on sets of copper plates, strung on a ring and sealed with the royal seal. They were kept by the people who received the land.

Found by chance. Copper plates turn up in fields, houses and temple stores. Some of the longest early Chola records, like the Larger Leiden plates and the Tiruvālaṅgāḍu plates, are copper-plate grants.

Stone stays put. Stone inscriptions stay at the temple and record gifts made there. Plates can travel, be sold, or end up in museums far away.

Read them together. Historians combine the two: plates for royal genealogy and great grants, stones for the daily life of temples and villages.

Ask the temple. Some temples still keep old copper plates or later records. If shown one, note the seal and the number of plates, and photograph only with permission.

ZAPPY: Why copper?

PROF. BOLT: It does not rot like palm leaf, and it could be carried and shown in a dispute. A land deed you could hold in your hand.

READ YOUR FIRST INSCRIPTION

A field routine
Locate. Which building, which side, which moulding, how many lines? Sketch where it is.
Photograph. Take it in low, slanting light, in overlapping shots, with a scale such as a pen.
Recognise. Is it Tamil, Grantha, Kannada, Telugu or Nāgarī? Do you see any numbers or a king’s name?
Search. Look up the temple in published inscription volumes or ASI reports; many are online.
Ask what it gives. Lamp, land, gold, food, a festival, a person? Who benefits, who manages it?

ZAPPY: What if I can’t read any of it?

PROF. BOLT: Then start with the shapes. Even recognising which script it is tells you something about where and when it was cut.
