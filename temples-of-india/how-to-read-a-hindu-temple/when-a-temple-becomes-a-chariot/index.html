HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 10 OF 12 · CHARIOTS

When a Temple Becomes a Chariot

Wheels and horses at Konārak and Dārāsuram · explained and illustrated

A temple that drives across the sky

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt meet temples that are chariots in stone: the Sun’s chariot at Konārak, the chariot hall of Dārāsuram, the Garuḍa chariot at Hampi, and shrines in Tamil Nadu that ride on wheels.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 2 the festival image rode out in a wooden chariot. Some builders went further and built the temple itself, or part of it, as a chariot.

They carved wheels on the platform and horses or elephants in front, so that the stone building seems ready to roll.

This part shows you where to find these chariot temples and how to read their wheels, horses and meaning.

ZAPPY: Why make a stone building look like it can move?

PROF. BOLT: Because a chariot is how gods travel. A chariot-temple carries the deity across the sky, or through the world, for ever.

ZAPPY: Are the rathas at Māmallapuram chariots?

PROF. BOLT: They are called rathas, but they are rock-cut shrines in temple shapes, not chariots. The name came from local tradition.

READING A CHARIOT TEMPLE

Three things to find

The story

Wheels. Walk along the base. Are there carved wheels attached to the platform, with axles and spokes? Count them.
Animals in front. Look at the front, usually at the foot of the steps: horses, sometimes elephants, harnessed to pull.
Which part rides. Is the whole temple a chariot, or only the front hall, or only the sanctum? Each tells a different story.
Which god. A sun temple’s chariot is the Sun’s own chariot crossing the sky. A Viṣṇu shrine as chariot echoes Garuḍa or Viṣṇu’s own travels.
Read the meaning. Then ask: what does the number of wheels or horses stand for? Guides offer explanations; note them as explanations, not facts carved in stone.

ZAPPY: Why are there so many explanations?

PROF. BOLT: Because the builders left few written explanations. Later readers saw days, months and hours in the wheels. They are lovely readings; keep them as readings.

Wheels on the base, animals in front, then ask which part rides.

KONĀRAK: THE SUN’S CHARIOT

A temple drawn by seven horses

Who and when. The Sun temple at Konārak, on the coast of Odisha, was built around 1250 under King Narasiṃhadeva I of the Eastern Gaṅga dynasty. UNESCO listed it in 1984.

A chariot. The whole temple is designed as the chariot of Sūrya, the Sun: a high platform with 24 great carved wheels, twelve on each side, each about three metres across, pulled by seven horses.

Wheels as dials. The wheels are carved with spokes, medallions and tiny figures. The shadow of a spoke on some wheels can be used to tell the time, so people call them sundials.

What survives. The great tower over the sanctum has fallen; what stands now is the hall in front of it, the jagamohana, with its stepped pyramid roof. A separate dance hall stands in front.

Readings. Guides often explain the seven horses as the days of the week, the twelve pairs of wheels as the months and the spokes as the divisions of the day. Enjoy them as readings.

ZAPPY: What happened to the tower?

PROF. BOLT: It collapsed long ago; when and why is still discussed. In the early 20th century the surviving hall was filled with sand and sealed to keep it standing.

READING A KONĀRAK WHEEL

A clock, a calendar and a picture book

Spokes. Each wheel has eight thick spokes and eight thin ones between them, so the rim is divided into eight and then sixteen parts.

Medallions. At the hub and in the middle of each thick spoke are round medallions carved with figures: gods, women, animals, scenes of daily life.

The rim. The rim is carved with leaves, birds and animals, and tiny figures, so finely that you can spend minutes on one segment.

Telling the time. Stand beside a wheel in sunlight: the shadow of the axle pin falls across the spokes. People demonstrate reading the hour from it. It works best as a lovely demonstration of the Sun’s temple.

Read one wheel well. Don’t try to see all 24. Pick one, sketch it, and read its medallions clockwise from the top.

ZAPPY: Are all the wheels the same?

PROF. BOLT: The pattern is the same, but the carvings differ from wheel to wheel. Each is its own little gallery.

THE WALLS OF KONĀRAK

Musicians, elephants and three Suns

Musicians on the roof. The terraces of the surviving hall’s pyramid roof carry large figures of women musicians: drummers, cymbal-players and others, playing for the Sun as he rides.

Elephants below. Along the base run friezes of elephants, hundreds of them, in processions, hunts and scenes of daily life.

Three Suns. Niches on the outer walls of the sanctum hold three large images of Sūrya in a fine greenish stone, often described as the Sun at morning, noon and evening.

Everything else. The walls are crowded with figures: gods, dancers, warriors, animals, scenes of court and household. Read them band by band, as at Halebidu (Part 5).

The dance hall. In front stands the nāṭa mandira, a platform hall richly carved with dancers and musicians, where dance was offered to the Sun.

ZAPPY: Why so much music?

PROF. BOLT: The Sun’s journey was celebrated with music and dance, at dawn especially. The temple turns that daily celebration into stone.

DĀRĀSURAM: A HALL ON WHEELS

The Airāvateśvara temple

Where. The Airāvateśvara temple at Dārāsuram near Kumbakonam was built in the 12th century by the Chola king Rājarāja II. It is one of the three “Great Living Chola Temples” listed by UNESCO.

The front hall. Its front hall is built as a chariot: stone wheels are carved on its sides, and small horses at the front seem to pull it.

Steps as music. The steps up to it are famous: some are said to give musical notes when tapped. They are protected now; please don’t tap.

Small and rich. The temple is smaller than Thanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram but extremely richly carved, with tiny panels of dancers, musicians and stories on the base.

Read the hall. Here only the hall is the chariot. The sanctum and tower stay fixed: the devotee steps onto a chariot to approach the god.

ZAPPY: Why only the hall?

PROF. BOLT: Scholars suggest it shows the hall as the god’s chariot or as a moving pavilion for festivals. Read it as an idea built in stone.

HAMPI: A CHARIOT FOR GARUḌA

Stone on wheels in the Viṭṭhala court

Where. In the courtyard of the Viṭṭhala temple at Hampi, the Vijayanagara capital, stands the famous stone chariot.

A shrine. It is a small shrine for Garuḍa, Viṣṇu’s eagle mount, facing the main temple, as the vehicle faces the god (Part 8).

Chariot form. It is built in the shape of a festival chariot, with stone wheels at the sides and animals in front. It was built in the 16th century under the Vijayanagara rulers.

Famous. It is one of India’s most recognisable monuments and appears on the back of the Indian fifty-rupee note.

Read it. Compare it with a wooden festival chariot (Part 2): base, wheels, shrine above. The stone version freezes the procession for ever.

ZAPPY: Can the wheels turn?

PROF. BOLT: People say they once could. Today they are fixed and protected. Please don’t push them!

SHRINES THAT RIDE

Chariot sanctums in Tamil Nadu

Sanctum as chariot. In some Tamil temples the sanctum itself, not a hall, rests on a chariot base with wheels, pulled by horses or elephants.

Sārṅgapāṇi. At the Sārṅgapāṇi temple in Kumbakonam, the central shrine of Viṣṇu is built in the form of a chariot with wheels, drawn by horses and elephants.

Chidambaram. At Chidambaram the Nṛtta Sabhā, a hall associated with Śiva’s dance, has wheels and horses carved on its base.

Karakkōyil. Tamil calls such a chariot-shaped shrine a karakkōyil. Temples of this form are found in several places in the Kāverī delta.

Look for it. Walk round the sanctum base. Wheels may be small and worn. Once you have seen one, you will spot them in other temples too.

ZAPPY: So any part of a temple can become a chariot?

PROF. BOLT: Hall, sanctum or a separate shrine. What matters is the idea: the god is on the move, even in stone.

COMPARING THE CHARIOTS

What rides, where and why

Konārak. The whole temple is the Sun’s chariot, wheels on every side.

Dārāsuram. Only the front hall rides; the sanctum stays fixed.

Hampi. A separate shrine for the vehicle (Garuḍa) is the chariot.

Sārṅgapāṇi. The sanctum itself rides.

The pattern. Each builder chose a different part. When you meet a new chariot temple, ask: which part rides, which stays, and what does that say about the god and the devotee?

ZAPPY: Is there a chariot temple near me?

PROF. BOLT: Possibly. Many smaller temples have a few wheels on their base. Look at the base of the next temple you visit.

READ A CHARIOT TEMPLE

A routine
Wheels. How many? Where? Spokes and carvings?
Animals. Horses or elephants? How many? Facing which way?
Which part. Whole temple, hall, separate shrine or sanctum?
God. Which deity? Sun, Śiva, Viṣṇu, Garuḍa?
Readings. What do the guides say the numbers mean? Note it as a reading.

ZAPPY: And photographs?

PROF. BOLT: Photograph one wheel straight on, in full, and one detail. Two good pictures beat fifty blurred ones.
