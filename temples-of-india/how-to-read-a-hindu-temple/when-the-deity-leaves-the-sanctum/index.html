HOW TO READ A HINDU TEMPLE · PART 2 OF 12 · FESTIVALS

When the Deity Leaves the Sanctum

Festival images, vehicles and processions · explained and illustrated

The god comes out to meet the town

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt follow the festival image out of the sanctum, onto its vehicles, into the chariot and round the car streets, and learn to read the parts of a temple built for those days.

WHERE WE ARE

In Part 1 we walked inwards, from the gateway to the sanctum. On festival days the direction reverses: the god comes out.

The main image in the sanctum usually stays where it was installed. What travels is a second, portable image, the utsava mūrti, “festival image”, usually cast in bronze. It rides on vehicles, sits in a palanquin, climbs into a chariot, floats on the tank and visits the town.

Many parts of a temple make sense only on these days: the wide streets around it, the chariot shed, the tall doorways, the halls where the god rests, the tank with a pavilion in the middle. This guide teaches you to see them.

ZAPPY: Why would the god leave the temple at all?

PROF. BOLT: So that everyone can see the deity: those who cannot climb the steps, those not allowed inside in older times, the whole town at once. The festival brings darśana to the street.

AT A GLANCE

The festival vocabulary
Mūla mūrtiThe main image, fixed in the sanctum
Utsava mūrtiThe portable festival image, often bronze
VāhanaA vehicle: Garuḍa, Nandi, the swan, the serpent, the lion, the elephant, the horse
Pallakku / śibikāA palanquin carried on poles
Ratha / tērThe temple chariot, pulled by devotees on ropes
Ratha vīdhiThe car streets round the temple where the chariot runs
TeppamA float festival on the temple tank
BrahmotsavamThe great annual festival of many South Indian temples, usually lasting about nine days

ZAPPY: So there are two gods?

PROF. BOLT: One deity, two forms. Temples treat the festival image as the same deity, worshipped fully, only movable.

TWO IMAGES, ONE GOD

The fixed image and the festival image

The story

The fixed image. The mūla mūrti in the sanctum is stone, often large, and fixed to its pedestal. It is installed once, with long rites, and never moves.
The festival image. Beside or in front of it, or in a separate room, stands a smaller image, usually bronze. In a Śiva temple it may be a figure of Śiva with Pārvatī, or Naṭarāja, while the sanctum holds a liṅga.
Its base tells you. Look at the base of a festival bronze in a temple or a museum. Rings, lugs or holes along the base are for poles: the image was made to be carried.
A matching name. Often the festival image has its own name. At Tirumala the festival form of Veṅkaṭeśvara is called Malayappa Svāmī, and he goes out on the vehicles.
So read in pairs. Whenever you see a bronze in a temple, ask: which fixed image is this the travelling form of?

ZAPPY: Why bronze?

PROF. BOLT: It is strong, lighter than a stone image of the same presence, and can be cast with fine detail. The Chola bronze-casters of the 10th to 12th centuries made the most famous ones.

Mūla mūrti stays; utsava mūrti goes out.

THE VEHICLES

A different vehicle for each day

Vāhana. A vāhana is the deity’s mount. In the festival it is a large wooden or metal frame, often silver- or gold-covered, on which the bronze is seated and carried.

Day by day. In the big festivals of South Indian temples the god goes out every morning and evening on a different vehicle: the serpent, the swan, the lion, the eagle, the elephant, the horse, a tree, a palanquin. The chariot comes near the end.

Garuḍa and Nandi. For Viṣṇu the most popular evening is often the Garuḍa vāhana; for Śiva it is the Nandi, or the Ṛṣabha vāhana. At Tirumala the Garuḍa sevā of the Brahmotsavam draws the largest crowds.

Where they live. Look for a vāhana maṇḍapa or a store-room where these big frames are kept. A temple with many vehicles needs space and wide doors.

Read the wall. Many temples carve or paint the whole set of vehicles on a hall pillar or ceiling. Once you know them you can read the festival calendar off the stone.

ZAPPY: Do the vehicles carry the god or the god carry them?

PROF. BOLT: The bearers carry both! Twenty or thirty men may shoulder a big vāhana. The rhythm of their steps is part of the show.

DOORS, STEPS AND CLEARANCE

Reading a temple for something that must pass

Ask what must pass. When you stand in a gateway, look up. The opening of the big gateways in many South Indian temples is tall enough for a festival image on a tall vehicle or palanquin to pass.

Thresholds and ramps. Look at the floor. A worn groove, a removable threshold stone, a ramp beside steps: these may be signs of heavy things rolled or carried through.

Corners. Inside the enclosure, look for a wide path round the temple, often a paved circuit between the walls. On festival days the god is carried round it before leaving.

Resting halls. On the way, the image stops in halls built for it: a pavilion where it is shown, decorated and offered food. Some halls are used only once a year.

A rule. A temple plan is made for walking devotees on ordinary days and for carried gods on festival days. Read it both ways.

ZAPPY: What if the vehicle is too tall?

PROF. BOLT: Then the bearers lower it, or the festival takes another door. You can sometimes see where a doorway was widened.

THE CHARIOT

A temple on wheels

A moving shrine. The ratha (Tamil tēr) is a temple on wheels. Its lower part is a heavy wooden base, often carved all over with deities and stories. Above it, a frame of bamboo and cloth makes a tall, bright canopy that is rebuilt each year.

Pulled by everyone. Thick ropes run forward from the chariot. Hundreds or thousands of devotees take hold and pull. Pulling the chariot is a form of devotion, open to all.

Famous chariots. The Āḻittēr of the Tiruvārūr Tyāgarāja temple in Tamil Nadu is among the largest temple chariots in India. At Puri, three new chariots are built every year for Jagannātha, Balabhadra and Subhadrā.

Puri is different. At Puri the wooden images from the sanctum themselves travel in the Ratha Yātrā, to the Guṇḍicā temple and back. It is one of the most famous exceptions to the rule that the sanctum image stays.

Look for the shed. Near many temples stands a tall shed or a covered chariot standing in the street all year. Once you notice it, you know the temple has a car festival.

ZAPPY: How do they steer it?

PROF. BOLT: Slowly! Wedges are pushed under the wheels to stop and turn it. Some temples now use hydraulic brakes. It can take hours to go round.

THE CAR STREETS

When the town is the temple’s outermost enclosure

Streets round the temple. In many Tamil temple towns the streets run in squares round the temple: the inner streets close to the walls, the outer ones further away. The chariot runs round the outermost square.

Named for the festival. The streets are often named for the month or the festival when the god goes round them, or simply called the “car streets”. In Srīraṅgam the temple’s enclosures and streets run outwards, one inside another, into the town.

Madurai. In Madurai the streets around the Mīnākṣī temple are named after Tamil months, and the festival processions of those months go round them.

The temple as a town. So the temple plan does not end at the outer wall. The town’s own streets act as the outermost ring of the temple, used on festival days.

Look at a map. Open a map of a temple town. If the streets make squares round the temple, you have found its processional plan.

ZAPPY: Do people live on these streets?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The houses face the street so that families can see the god go past and offer at their doorstep. The god visits them.

THE GOD ON THE WATER

Float festivals and the tank pavilion

Teppam. In a teppam or float festival the festival image is placed on a decorated raft on the temple tank and taken round it, sometimes several times, often at night with lamps.

The pavilion in the middle. Many tanks have a small pavilion on an island in the centre. Look for it: it may be where the god rests during the float festival.

Madurai’s tank. At Madurai the great Teppakkuḷam tank, with its central island pavilion, hosts the float festival of Mīnākṣī and Sundareśvara.

Bathing the god. Many festivals end with the festival image being bathed in the tank or a river, the tīrthavāri, and devotees bathe with it.

Read the steps. Wide steps on all four sides let the crowd sit and watch. The tank is a theatre as well as a water store. Part 7 returns to temple water.

ZAPPY: Why water?

PROF. BOLT: Water cools, cleans and blesses. And a float on a lit tank at night is simply one of the most beautiful sights a temple offers.

FESTIVALS THAT LEAVE THE TOWN

When the god visits another place

Journeys. Some festivals take the god out of the town altogether: to a river, a hill, the sea, a garden, or another temple, sometimes many kilometres away.

Meeting other gods. In some places the deities of neighbouring temples are brought together on one day, each in its own palanquin or vehicle, so devotees see many at once.

The Chithirai festival. In Madurai’s Chithirai festival, the celestial wedding of Mīnākṣī and Sundareśvara is celebrated, and Kaḷḷaḻagar, the Viṣṇu of Alagar Koil, travels to the Vaigai river. Two temples’ festivals meet in one city.

Read the landscape. A small pavilion by a river, at a crossroads or on a hilltop, far from any temple, may be a halt on such a journey. Ask locals which god stops there.

Halts and offerings. Along the way, families and villages make offerings at each halt. The route itself becomes a map of who the god visits.

ZAPPY: How do they know the route?

PROF. BOLT: Memory, custom and temple records. Routes can stay the same for generations, and quarrels about them are remembered too!

READING A BRONZE IN A MUSEUM

From the gallery back to the festival

Bare and dressed. In a museum a festival bronze stands bare on a plinth, lit to show its modelling. In a temple it is dressed in silk, flowers and jewels, and much of the body is hidden.

Look at the base. Check the base for rings, lugs or holes where poles passed. The museum label often says if the bronze was used in processions.

Look at the back. The back is often less finished: it was meant to be seen from the front and sides as it passed.

Ask where it came from. A temple may have many bronzes. The label may tell you the temple, or only the region. Record both, and do not guess a temple from style alone.

Imagine it moving. Then picture it carried out through a gateway, swaying on poles, lit by torches, with music. The same object read two ways.

ZAPPY: Why are so many temple bronzes in museums?

PROF. BOLT: Some were dug up from hoards, buried in troubled times; some were sold or taken away. Several have been returned to temples in recent years.

TRY IT ON A FESTIVAL DAY

A watching routine
Find the image. Which bronze is going out, and what fixed image is it the form of?
Name the vehicle. Which vāhana is it on today? What does the vehicle tell you about the deity?
Trace the exit. Which gate does it leave by? Does it go round inside the walls first?
Map the route. Which streets, which halts, which other temples or pavilions?
Watch the people. Who carries, who pulls, who sings, who offers at the doorstep? A festival is made of people.

ZAPPY: Can I take photos?

PROF. BOLT: Usually in the street, yes, but follow the temple’s rules, keep out of the bearers’ way and never block devotees’ view.
