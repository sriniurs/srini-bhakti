MURḌĒŚVARA

Where the ātmaliṅga’s cloth fell into the sea

TEMPLES OF KARNATAKA

FOREWORD

When Rāvaṇa failed to lift the ātmaliṅga at Gōkarṇa, he tore away its wrappings and flung them down the coast. The last, the cloth, fell on a small hill in the sea. That hill is Murḍēśvara.

Here Śiva is a rough stone below the floor, seen by lamplight. Above him, a modern age has built one of the tallest Śiva statues in the world and a temple tower of twenty storeys.

This guide covers both: the old liṅga and the new hill.

Om Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A cloth became a temple?

PROF. BOLT: In the story, everything that touched the ātmaliṅga carried its power. So even its wrapping made a holy place.

ZAPPY: Murḍēśvara, Murudeshwar, Murdeshwar?

PROF. BOLT: All the same place, spelt in different ways. This guide uses Murḍēśvara.

WHERE IT IS

A hill in the sea

The town. Murḍēśvara is in Bhaṭkaḷ tālūk of Uttara Kannaḍa, on the coast road between Gōkarṇa to the north and the Tuḷu country to the south.

The hill. Kanduka giri is a small rocky headland with the sea on three sides. Temple, statue and tower all stand on it, so the whole complex can be seen from far out at sea.

Offshore. Netrāṇi, a small heart-shaped island, lies about ten nautical miles out.

MṚḌA AND THE BALL

Two names explained

🙏 Mṛḍa

Mṛḍa, “the gracious”, is one of the names of Śiva. Mṛḍēśa or Mṛḍēśvara, “gracious lord”, is the deity’s name here.

🗣️ Murḍēśvara

Spoken over centuries, Mṛḍēśvara became Murḍēśvara, which is also the town’s name.

⚽ Kanduka

Kanduka is a ball in Sanskrit. Kanduka giri is the “ball hill”, after its round shape at the sea’s edge.

🌊 Giri

Giri, a hill. A temple on a hill by the sea is the classic setting for a coastal Śiva.

ZAPPY: So the name means the Gracious Lord?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, a gentle name for a god whose great statue looks anything but gentle from the beach.

THE CLOTH ON THE HILL

The story from Murḍēśvara’s side
At Gōkarṇa. Rāvaṇa could not lift the ātmaliṅga that Gaṇeśa had set down. The full story is in the Gōkarṇa guide and in Rāvaṇa and Gōkarṇa.
The wrappings. In fury he flung away everything that had held it: the case, its lid, its string.
The last. Finally he threw the cloth that had wrapped the liṅga. It flew south and came down on Kanduka giri.
A shrine. Where it fell, Śiva was worshipped as Mṛḍēśa, and the cloth’s resting place became a shrine of the ātmaliṅga.

THE LIṄGA BELOW THE FLOOR

Rough, low and lamplit

Not a polished liṅga. The main deity is a rough rock in a hollow in the ground, about two feet below the floor, believed to be part of the ātmaliṅga.

Seen by lamplight. Devotees do not enter the sanctum. They see Mṛḍēśa from its threshold, as the priests hold oil lamps to light him.

Old under new. The towering modern complex is built around this small, ancient, unshaped stone.

ZAPPY: Why is it below the floor?

PROF. BOLT: It’s where the stone is: the shrine was built down to it, not the stone raised up. Many very old liṅgas sit low in the earth like this.

ZAPPY: Should one visit both?

PROF. BOLT: Pilgrims often do, with the three smaller pañca kṣētras between. Gōkarṇa gives the story’s centre; Murḍēśvara its last, flung piece.

FROM FISHERMEN’S SHRINE TO LANDMARK

How the hill changed
Old A small shrine on Kanduka giri, said to be some centuries old, known mostly to the local fishing community.
Decline At one stage the old temple was beginning to fall apart and needed rebuilding.
1990s The businessman R. N. Śeṭṭi (Rāma Nāgappa Śeṭṭi), begins a renovation that remakes the whole hill.
c. 2006 The great Śiva statue is completed.
May 2008 The Rāja gōpura is opened.
Since Murḍēśvara becomes one of the best-known sights of the Karnataka coast, for pilgrims and holidaymakers alike.

Granite and concrete. The temple itself is built mainly of granite in the Dravidian manner; the statue and many sculptures are modern concrete work.

THE RĀJA GŌPURA

Twenty storeys at the gate

🏗️ Size

20 storeys, about 249 ft high, opened in May 2008; often called among the tallest temple gateways anywhere.

🛗 A lift

A lift carries visitors up to look out at the statue, the hill and the sea from near the top.

🐘 Guardians

Two great elephants stand on either side of its steps.

🧭 Purpose

A gōpura marks the threshold from the world to the god’s domain; here it also gives a view of the whole sacred hill at once.

ZAPPY: A lift in a temple tower?

PROF. BOLT: A new idea for an old form. The gōpura is still a gate; this one simply lets you climb it.

RĀMĒŚVARA AND ŚANĒŚVARA

Two more shrines on the hill

🔱 Rāmēśvara liṅga

At the foot of the hill. Here, unusually, devotees may perform the sēvā themselves rather than through a priest.

🪐 Śanēśvara

A shrine of Śani, the planet Saturn, stands beside the figure of Akṣayaguṇa, with two life-size elephants on its steps.

THE FORT

Behind the temple

Where. The remains of Murḍēśvara Fort stand behind the temple on the headland, looking out over the sea.

Who. Local accounts trace it to the Vijayanagara period and say Ṭīpu Sultān of Mysore renovated it in the late 18th century. Its earlier history is not clearly recorded.

What remains. Walls, wells and watch-posts, with carving on parts of the walls.

ZAPPY: A Vijayanagara fort and a Ṭīpu renovation?

PROF. BOLT: So the local tradition says. The coast changed hands many times, and every power that held it wanted the headlands.

See also. Ṭīpu’s dealings with temples are covered in Ṭīpu and the Temples.
