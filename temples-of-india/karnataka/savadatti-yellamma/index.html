Savadatti Yellamma

Reṇukā on the Hill of Yallammanagudda · explained and illustrated

The Mother on the hill

HOW THIS WORKS: Reṇukā Yellamma of Savadatti: her hard story, the hill and its waters, neem vows, the great full moons, and the devadāsī custom and its end.

WHERE WE ARE

On a bare hill above the Malaprabhā, five kilometres from Savadatti, sits the Goddess the Deccan calls Yellamma, “mother of all”. She is Reṇukā, wife of the sage Jamadagni and mother of Paraśurāma. Twice a year, on full-moon days, the hill takes in crowds counted in lakhs from Karnataka, Maharashtra, Goa and Andhra, many bathing in her tanks, many walking round with neem leaves in their mouths.

This guide covers the hill and its names, Reṇukā’s story and its tellings, the temple of 1514 and its far older past, the shrines and sacred waters of the hill, the great full moons, the Goddess’s devotees, and, plainly, the devadāsī custom and its abolition.

ZAPPY: Why “Yellamma”?

PROF. BOLT: Commonly explained as “mother of all” (ella-amma) in Kannaḍa. Her devotees simply call her Amma.

The mother of everyone who climbs.

THE HILL

Yallammanagudda over the Malaprabhā

The hill. The temple sits on a hilltop once called Siddhācala, now Yallammanagudda, “Yellamma’s hill”, part of an east–west range also called Rāmagiri.

The river. It overlooks the Malaprabhā, dammed nearby at Navilutīrtha into a reservoir named Reṇukā Sāgara after the Goddess.

The town. The hill rises beside the old town of Savadatti (Saundatti), in the Dharwad–Belagavi country of north Karnataka.

Ancient ground. Megalithic tombs on the hill are older than any temple, and potsherds date from about the 3rd century BCE to the 3rd century CE.

Savadatti. The town has its own fort and old temples, with Raṭṭa inscriptions.

ZAPPY: People were on this hill before the temple?

PROF. BOLT: For two thousand years at least. A sacred hill tends to stay sacred.

A hill sacred for two thousand years.

STORY · REṆUKĀ

The pot of water and the axe

The story

Reṇukā’s story, in the form told at the temple and in the Purāṇas (tellings differ).

Reṇukā, a king’s daughter, married the sage Jamadagni. So pure was she, the story goes, that she could carry river water home in a pot of unbaked clay, held together by her purity alone.
One day at the river she saw a heavenly being (a gandharva) bathing with his companions, and for a moment her mind wavered. The pot dissolved.
Knowing what had happened, Jamadagni ordered his sons to kill her. The elder sons refused. The youngest, Paraśurāma, obeyed and beheaded his mother with his axe.
Pleased, Jamadagni offered him a boon. Paraśurāma asked that his mother be brought back to life (and his brothers restored), and she was.
In one telling, in his haste the head of another woman, dark-skinned, was set on Reṇukā’s body, which explains the Goddess’s dark face here. Another account says Reṇukā became a satī after Jamadagni was killed by Kārtavīrya Arjuna.

ZAPPY: A son who kills his mother is a hero?

PROF. BOLT: The story is not comfortable, and it is not meant to be. What the devotees hold on to is the end: the mother who was wronged, killed, and came back stronger, as the Mother of all.

Wronged, killed, and restored as the Mother.

YELLAMMA

The Goddess on the hill

Her names. Reṇukā; Yellamma or Ellamma; Jagadambā, “mother of the world”; and Yelumakkaḷatāyi, “mother of seven children”.

A fertility goddess. She is revered as a fertility goddess and counted among the Saptamātṛkās, the seven mothers; many believe her a form of Kālī.

Older than her story. Scholars think a goddess cult was present on the hill in the Cāḷukya period or before; the Reṇukā story gave the local Mother a place in the Purāṇic world. (Scholarly view.)

Her face. The Goddess is shown with a dark face, explained by the story of the replaced head.

Her household. Shrines on the hill honour Gaṇeśa, Mallikārjuna, Paraśurāma, Ekanātha and Siddheśvara.

ZAPPY: Mother of seven children?

PROF. BOLT: A title of a mother goddess of the people. She is above all the mother everyone can come to.

A mother goddess given a Purāṇic story.

THE TEMPLE OF 1514

Bomappa Nāyaka and the older temple

1514. The present temple was built in 1514 by Bomappa Nāyaka of Raybag.

Before him. Archaeological evidence points to a temple on the site between the mid-8th and mid-11th centuries, in the early Rāṣṭrakūṭa or late Cāḷukya period.

Style. The temple follows Cāḷukya and Rāṣṭrakūṭa styles: sanctum, antechamber and a large hall. Some sources note a Jain inscription referring to Rāṣṭrakūṭa kings.

1975. The Government of Karnataka took over management, providing free rest houses and health centres for pilgrims.

A busy hill. Hundreds come every day; lakhs on the great days.

ZAPPY: A Jain inscription in a Goddess temple?

PROF. BOLT: The Deccan’s stones were reused and its faiths lived side by side. It is common in North Karnataka.

An old hill, a 16th-century temple.

THE SACRED WATERS

Tanks and the healing well

At the foot. At the bottom of the hill pilgrims bathe in the Yogarbāvi Satyammā kuṇḍa before going up to worship.

Jogaḷa Bhāvi. A well called Jogaḷa Bhāvi is believed to cure skin diseases.

Three kuṇḍas. Behind the temple are three tanks: Kuṅkuma kuṇḍa, Yoni kuṇḍa and Arihan kuṇḍa, where devotees bathe and worship.

Paraśurāma Kṣetra. Beside the temple is the place where Paraśurāma is believed to have done penance.

Why water. In Reṇukā’s story everything turns on a pot of water. Her hill is full of it. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: A well that heals the skin?

PROF. BOLT: So pilgrims believe, and they come in thousands to bathe. Faith and cool water both help.

A story of water, a hill of waters.

NEEM LEAVES

The vow of the neem

Bevu. Neem (bevu) is sacred to the Goddess across the Deccan; her devotees wear neem leaves and offer them.

The circling. At the foot of the hill devotees perform nimmana, circling the Satyammā temple with neem leaves in their mouths; some also wear neem twigs on their clothes as a vow. (Practice varies.)

Why neem. Neem is bitter, cooling and healing, the plant of mother goddesses who protect from disease. (Common belief.)

Vows. Many come to fulfil a vow: for a child, for health, for a prayer answered.

The jōgatis. Women devotees called jōgatis carry the Goddess’s image in a basket (jaga) on their heads and sing her songs. (Described here as a devotional tradition.)

ZAPPY: Leaves in the mouth?

PROF. BOLT: A sign of humility before the Mother: you come silent and bitter-mouthed, and leave blessed.

Bitter leaves for a healing mother.

THE GREAT FULL MOONS

Lakhs on the hill

Two great days. Two full moons draw crowds of up to ten lakh each: Bharata Hunnime and Banada Hunnime.

Bharata Hunnime. Commonly placed on the Māgha full moon (Jan–Feb); one source places it in Mārgaśira. (Sources differ.)

Banada Hunnime. The Pauṣa full moon, day of Banaśaṅkarī too (see Part 5). (Sources differ on which moon is which.)

The jātre. On the great days the Goddess’s image is taken out in procession and worshipped as Śakti; festivals run between about October and April.

From four states. Carts, buses and tractors bring pilgrims from Karnataka, Maharashtra, Goa and Andhra Pradesh; many camp on the hill.

ZAPPY: Ten lakh on one hill?

PROF. BOLT: So the counts say. The hill becomes a city for a few days.

A hill that becomes a city for a full moon.

THE DEVADĀSĪ CUSTOM

A history, and its end

What it was. Yellamma’s temple was long associated with the custom of dedicating girls to the Goddess as devadāsīs. Over time the custom came to mean exploitation and poverty for many of the women and girls dedicated.

Abolished. The Government of Karnataka prohibited the practice by the Karnataka Devadasis (Prohibition of Dedication) Act, 1982.

Today. Dedication is illegal; the state and voluntary groups have worked on rehabilitation of former devadāsīs and on preventing dedications.

Not the Goddess’s doing. Devotees and reformers alike stress that the abuse of a custom is not the faith itself; the jātre is about the Mother’s power, not about any harm to her daughters.

Why we say this plainly. A reference guide that skipped it would be dishonest.

ZAPPY: Is the practice gone?

PROF. BOLT: It is banned, and much reduced. Reports of rare illegal dedications still surface and are prosecuted. The law is clear.

A hard history ended by law.

HER CHILDREN

Everyone who comes

The poor and the farmer. Yellamma is above all a goddess of the rural Deccan; her pilgrims are farmers, labourers and their families.

Transgender devotees. The temple is associated with transgender devotees (jōgappas), who are held to be among Reṇukā’s children and are a visible presence at the temple.

Women. Women carry her songs and her image; the jātre is described as a celebration of women’s power in the form of Śakti.

Four states. She is worshipped across Karnataka, Maharashtra (as Reṇukā of Māhūr and others), Goa and the Telugu lands.

Read more. Reṇukā’s son is in Paraśurāma, Daśāvatāra.

ZAPPY: A goddess for those others turned away?

PROF. BOLT: That is how many describe her: the Mother of everyone who has nowhere else to go.

The Mother of those with nowhere else to go.
