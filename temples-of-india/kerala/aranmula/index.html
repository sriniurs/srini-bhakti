PART 9 OF 10 · PĀRTHASĀRATHY · THE SNAKE BOATS · THE RIVER FEAST

Āranmuḷa

Kṛṣṇa, the Pampa and the palliyōḍams · explained and illustrated

A palliyōḍam on the Pampa

HOW THIS WORKS: First the Lord: Kṛṣṇa as Arjuna’s charioteer, brought here on a raft of six bamboos. Then the river: the boat of offerings, the great snake boats of the villages, their water festival, and the feast of 64 dishes served to their crews.

WHERE WE ARE

From the serpent grove of Mannārśāla in Part 8 we go inland to Āranmuḷa, a village on the river Pampa in Pathanamthitta district. It is the same river pilgrims bathe in on the way to Sabarimala, Part 3.

The temple’s Lord is Pārthasārathy, Kṛṣṇa as the charioteer (sārathi) of Arjuna (Pārtha), the Lord of the Bhagavad Gītā. Tradition says Arjuna himself installed him.

But Āranmuḷa is above all a temple of the river. Its villages own great palliyōḍams, snake boats that are the Lord’s own vessels, and its festivals are rowed, sung and eaten on the water’s edge.

ZAPPY: A temple festival on a river?

PROF. BOLT: Here the river is part of the temple. The Lord came by water, and the boats still come to him.

ZAPPY: Why is the charioteer the main form here?

PROF. BOLT: Because Arjuna installed him. The disciple worships the Lord as he knew him best: the friend who drove his chariot and taught him the Gītā.

WHERE IT IS

A temple on the Pampa

The river. Āranmuḷa sits on the bank of the Pampa, Kerala’s third-longest river, which rises in the hills near Sabarimala and flows down to the backwaters.

The temple. The temple stands on raised ground above the river, its main entrance facing east, with steps leading down to the water.

The karas. Around it lie the karas, villages or neighbourhoods, traditionally 52, each with its own palliyōḍam kept in a boat shed on the bank.

Neighbours. Four other Pāṇḍava temples stand in the region: at Tṛccittāṭṭ, Puliyūr, Tiruvanvaṇṭūr and Tṛkkoḍittānam.

ARJUNA’S TEMPLE

How Pārthasārathy came to the Pampa

The story

After the war. When the Kurukṣetra war was over and Parīkṣit crowned, the five Pāṇḍavas set out on a long pilgrimage and came at last to these rivers of Kerala.
Five temples. Each brother installed an image of Viṣṇu in Kṛṣṇa’s honour: Yudhiṣṭhira at Tṛccittāṭṭ, Bhīma at Puliyūr, Arjuna here, Nakula at Tiruvanvaṇṭūr, Sahadeva at Tṛkkoḍittānam.
Arjuna’s sorrow. Arjuna, tradition adds, built his temple to atone for killing Karṇa, his own unknown elder brother, on the battlefield.
From Nilakkal. The image first stood at Nilakkal, up in the hills near Sabarimala. Later it was brought down the Pampa on a raft of six bamboos, made, the story says, by people of the hill communities.
A name. So the village was called Āṟu-muḷa, “six bamboos”: Āranmuḷa. The day of the installation, Uttṛṭṭāti in Ciṅṅam, is still its great day.

ZAPPY: Did Arjuna really come to Kerala?

PROF. BOLT: That is the temple’s tradition, shared by all five Pāṇḍava temples. It ties Kerala’s rivers to the Mahābhārata.

Pārthasārathy: the Lord as Arjuna knew him, carried down the river on six bamboos.

THE LORD IN THE SANCTUM

Pārthasārathy, the charioteer

The image. The Lord stands about six feet tall with four arms, as Viṣṇu, though he is worshipped as Kṛṣṇa the charioteer. He faces east.

The Gītā’s Lord. Pārthasārathy is the Kṛṣṇa of the Bhagavad Gītā, who drove Arjuna’s chariot between the armies and taught him his duty. To worship him here is to remember that teaching.

Divya Deśam. The temple is one of the 108 Divya Deśams, called Tiruvāṟanviḷai in the hymns of the Tamil saint Nammāḻvār, which shows its fame a thousand years ago.

The walls. The temple has mural paintings dating to the early 18th century, in the Kerala style.

ZAPPY: Why does the charioteer have four arms?

PROF. BOLT: The same reason as at Guruvāyūr: Kerala sees Kṛṣṇa and Viṣṇu as one. The four arms say who he is; the name says how Arjuna knew him.

THE BOAT OF OFFERINGS

Tiruvōṇa tōṇi: how the snake boats came to Āranmuḷa

The story

A vow. A devout Brahmin of Kāṭṭūr, upstream on the Pampa, vowed to provide everything needed for the Lord’s feast on Tiruvōṇam, the great day of Ōṇam.
The boat. Every year he sent rice and provisions down the river in a boat, the Tiruvōṇa tōṇi, “the boat of Tiruvōṇam”.
Danger. Once the boat was attacked on the way. People of the villages along the bank rowed out in their boats to protect it.
The escort. From then on the villages sent their boats every year to escort the offering. The escort boats became the palliyōḍams, the Lord’s own snake boats.
Still sailing. The Tiruvōṇa tōṇi still comes to Āranmuḷa each Ōṇam with the offerings for the feast, a custom kept for centuries.

ZAPPY: So the boat race began as a bodyguard?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. A vow, an attack, and a whole river of villages rowing to protect the Lord’s rice. The boats remember it.

The snake boats were born as an escort for the Lord’s feast.

THE PALLIYŌḌAMS

The Lord’s own boats

What they are. A palliyōḍam is a long wooden snake boat, over 100 feet in some cases, with a low hull and a tall, curved stern topped with a gold-coloured finial and a flag. Paḷḷi means holy: these are the Lord’s vessels.

Owned by villages. Each palliyōḍam belongs to a kara, one of the villages round the temple, and is kept in its own shed. The village builds it, repairs it and rows it.

Treated as sacred. Rowers go barefoot, the boat is worshipped before launching, and it is never used for ordinary racing for prizes. Many see the palliyōḍam as Kṛṣṇa’s own form on the water.

The songs. Crews row to vañcippāṭṭu, boat songs, many about Kṛṣṇa, sung by singers standing in the middle of the boat. The rhythm keeps the oars together.

Āranmuḷa style. Compared with racing snake boats elsewhere in Kerala, Āranmuḷa’s palliyōḍams are a little different in form and are rowed for worship, not for a cup.

ZAPPY: Is the palliyōḍam like a chariot on water?

PROF. BOLT: A good way to see it: Pārthasārathy drove a chariot; here the villages row his chariot down the river.

UTTṚṬṬĀTI VAḶḶAMKAḶI

The water festival

When. The water festival is held on Uttṛṭṭāti star in the month of Ciṅṅam (August–September), a few days after Ōṇam. It marks the anniversary of the image’s installation at Āranmuḷa.

What happens. The palliyōḍams of the karas row up the Pampa in procession to the temple, with singing and drums, and then row in pairs and groups before crowds on the banks.

Worship, not a race. It is offered to the Lord as worship. The emphasis is on rhythm, singing and the beauty of the boats more than on who finishes first.

The temple steps. Crews land at the temple steps, offer worship, and receive the Lord’s blessing before the procession goes on.

ZAPPY: No winner at all?

PROF. BOLT: Some years prizes have been given, but the spirit is worship. The winner is the Lord who receives the boats.

VAḶḶA SADYA

The feast of 64 dishes

What it is. The Vaḷḷa sadya, “boat feast”, is a grand vegetarian meal offered by devotees to the crew of a palliyōḍam as an offering to the Lord. It is served in the temple dining hall and nearby halls over many weeks of the season.

64 dishes. A full Vaḷḷa sadya is said to have up to 64 dishes, many more than an ordinary Kerala sadya: rice, curries, pickles, pappaḍams, several pāyasams and more, all on a banana leaf.

Sung requests. The best part: the oarsmen ask for dishes by singing, in the style of the boat songs. A verse names the dish; the servers must bring it. Nothing asked for in song may be refused.

Who may offer. A devotee who makes the vow offers the feast to the crew of at least one kara’s palliyōḍam. The crew arrive by boat, singing, to eat it.

Why. The crew of the Lord’s boat are treated as the Lord’s guests. Feeding them is feeding him.

ZAPPY: What happens if they sing for a dish that isn’t ready?

PROF. BOLT: Then the kitchen has to find it! It is part of the fun, and part of the vow: the Lord’s crew must be satisfied.

MIRROR AND GOLDEN ATTIRE

Two treasures linked to the temple

🪞 The metal mirror

Āranmuḷa kaṇṇāṭi is a mirror made not of glass but of a polished metal alloy, a secret craft of a few families of Āranmuḷa. Tradition links its origin to the temple: craftsmen making a crown for the Lord found that their alloy reflected like glass.

🏷️ A protected craft

The mirror is a registered Geographical Indication of Kerala, and a mirror is often kept as an auspicious gift in Kerala homes.

👑 The golden attire

The taṅka aṅki, the golden robe that Sabarimala Ayyappa wears for the Maṇḍala pūjā, was gifted by Chithira Thirunal, the last Maharaja of Travancore, in 1973. It is kept at Āranmuḷa.

🛕 To Sabarimala

Each December it leaves Āranmuḷa in a decorated chariot on a four-day procession up to Sabarimala, received at temples along the way: the river temple and the hill shrine joined.

ZAPPY: So Parts 3 and 9 are linked?

PROF. BOLT: By the Pampa and by gold. The same river, and every year the same procession from Āranmuḷa to the hills.

A LONG HISTORY

From Nilakkal to the boat songs
Legend Arjuna installs Pārthasārathy at Nilakkal; the image comes down the Pampa on six bamboos
By 9th c. Nammāḻvār sings the temple as Tiruvāṟanviḷai
Medieval The Tiruvōṇa tōṇi and the escort boats begin, by tradition
Early 18th c. The temple’s mural paintings
Later The palliyōḍams of the karas become a central part of worship
1973 Chithira Thirunal gives the golden attire for Sabarimala, kept at Āranmuḷa

A village temple. Āranmuḷa is a temple carried by its villages: their boats, their songs, their kitchens. The karas keep it alive.
