TEMPLES OF KERALA · THE INTRODUCTION · STONE, WOOD AND COPPER

The Kerala Temple

How Kerala builds a house for God · explained and illustrated

A Kerala temple, opened up

HOW THIS WORKS: We start with why Kerala builds differently and who wrote the rules. Then we walk from the boundary wall inwards, one part per page, to the sanctum and its roof, and end with the crafts: laterite, wood, paint and measure.

BEFORE WE BEGIN

Look at a temple in Tamil Nadu and you see stone towers. Look at a temple in Kerala and you see roofs: low, wide, sloping roofs of copper and tile, tier above tier, sheltering wooden walls on granite bases, among coconut palms. Kerala developed its own way of building a house for God.

That way has a grammar. Every Kerala temple, small or great, is built as a set of enclosures, one inside another, with the sanctum at the centre. The parts have names, the proportions have rules, and the rules were written down by Kerala’s own masters in texts like the Tantrasamuccaya and the Śilparatna.

This guide explains that grammar in detail, part by part, so that when you step into any Kerala temple you can read it.

AT A GLANCE

The Kerala temple in one table

PlanFive nested enclosures (pañcaprākāra) from the sanctum court to the boundary wall

TextsTantrasamuccaya (15th c.), Śilparatna (16th c.), Manuṣyālayacandrikā, Taccuśāstra

CraftsWood carving, Kerala mural painting, bell-metal casting, laterite masonry

ZAPPY: Is it really Drāviḍa if there are no towers?

PROF. BOLT: The texts class it as Drāviḍa, the southern family. Kerala kept the plan and the rules, and changed the skin to suit its climate.

WHY KERALA BUILDS DIFFERENTLY

Rain, wood and a narrow green land

Rain. Kerala gets two monsoons and some of the heaviest rain in India. Flat stone roofs would leak and towers would stain and crack. So the roof became steep and wide, with deep eaves that throw water far from the walls.

Wood. The Western Ghats gave Kerala teak, jackwood and rosewood in plenty. Its builders became master carpenters, and roofs, walls, brackets and ceilings were made of carved timber.

Laterite. The red laterite stone of Kerala is soft when cut and hardens in air. It is easy to shape into blocks for walls and bases.

Granite for the base. Wood and laterite are kept off the wet ground by a moulded base (adhiṣṭhāna) of granite, which also carries the sanctum’s weight.

The result. A temple that looks like a grand Kerala house: low, spreading, shaded and cool, with the sanctum at its heart.

ZAPPY: So the climate designed the temple?

PROF. BOLT: The climate set the problem; the builders solved it beautifully. Function first, then beauty built on it.

WHO WROTE THE RULES

Texts, Tantris and master carpenters

The story

Tantrasamuccaya. Written in the 15th century by Cēnnās Nārāyaṇan Nampūtiri, it is Kerala’s great manual of temple ritual and includes the rules of temple building and consecration. Kerala temples are still run by it.
Śilparatna. A 16th-century Sanskrit treatise by Śrīkumāra, covering architecture, sculpture and painting, written under a Kerala ruler.
Other texts. The Manuṣyālayacandrikā, on houses, and the Taccuśāstra, the carpenter’s lore, share the same system of measure and proportion.
The Tantri. The temple’s hereditary chief ritualist decides the site, the orientation and the deity’s place, and consecrates the building.
The taccan. The master carpenter, the perumtaccan, turned the rules into timber. Kerala’s legends tell of Uḷiyannūr Perumtaccan, a carpenter of genius said to have built many famous temples and to have measured perfectly by eye.

ZAPPY: Was Perumtaccan a real person?

PROF. BOLT: Legend makes him a son of the scholar Vararuci, with a dozen famous brothers. Whether real or not, he stands for the master builders whose names were never written down.

Kerala temples are built to written rules, by masters who knew them by heart.

HOW IT DEVELOPED

From rock caves to copper roofs
8th–9th c. Rock-cut cave shrines, such as Kaviyoor and Vizhinjam, show the earliest surviving sacred architecture
9th–12th c. The Cēra kingdom: many structural temples rise; circular and apsidal sanctums appear; inscriptions record temple councils
12th–14th c. Temples grow larger, with nālambalams and outer walls; wood becomes richly carved
15th–16th c. The Tantrasamuccaya and Śilparatna codify the rules
16th–19th c. Murals, kūttambalams, lamp walls and gopurams added; royal patronage in Travancore, Cochin and Calicut
20th c. on Restoration; copper roofs renewed; conservation with traditional methods

ZAPPY: Why so few very old buildings?

PROF. BOLT: Wood and laterite need rebuilding. Many temples are ancient as sites, but their timber has been renewed again and again, following the same rules.

THE FIVE ENCLOSURES

Pañcaprākāra: the plan of every Kerala temple

Five rings. A full Kerala temple is laid out as five enclosures, one inside another, each a little more sacred than the one outside it. Smaller temples have fewer, but keep the same order.

1 · Antarmaṇḍala. The inner court, also called akatte balivaṭṭam: the śrīkōvil, the namaskāra maṇḍapam and the inner bali stones.

2 · Antahāra. The nālambalam, the cloister that encloses the inner court, with the kitchen and store rooms.

3 · Madhyahāra. The zone just outside it, with the vilakkumāṭam lamp wall and the outer bali stones.

4 · Bāhyahāra. The outer path where the deity is carried in procession (śīvēli), with the flagstaff and sub-shrines.

5 · Maryādā. The boundary wall with its gateways (gopuram). Outside lie the tank, the dining hall and the town.

ZAPPY: Is it like an onion?

PROF. BOLT: A sacred onion, yes. Each layer you pass makes you a little quieter, until you stand before the Lord.

THE ŚRĪKŌVIL

Anatomy of the sanctum, from base to finial

Adhiṣṭhāna. The base: courses of moulded granite that lift the shrine off the damp earth. Its mouldings follow set orders, like the bases of Tamil temples.

Bhitti. The wall: laterite blocks, often plastered, or carved wood; divided by pilasters, with niches and false doors on three sides and the real door facing the deity’s direction.

Prastara. The entablature: a beam course on which the roof frame rests.

Roof. One or two tiers (tala) of sloping roof on a timber frame, covered with copper sheets or clay tiles. A two-tier sanctum is a dvitala shrine.

Finial. At the peak, a gold or copper pot-finial (kalaśa or stūpi), the crown of the shrine, consecrated with the building.

Praṇāla. The water spout that drains the bath water of the deity, usually on the left side as you face the shrine. Devotees never step over its channel.

SHAPES OF THE SANCTUM

Square, circle, apse and more

⬛ Square (caturaśra)

The most common form, used for almost every deity. Often two-storeyed with a pyramidal roof.

⚪ Circular (vṛtta)

A special Kerala love: round shrines with conical roofs, common in the south of the state and often for Śiva and Viṣṇu. The inner chamber is still square.

🔘 Apsidal (gajapṛṣṭha)

“Elephant’s back”: straight sides and a rounded rear. Often for Śāstā (Ayyappa) or Durgā, and an ancient form.

▭ Rectangular and others

Rectangular (āyata) shrines house reclining or grouped images; octagonal and elliptical forms are rare.

ZAPPY: Does the shape depend on the god?

PROF. BOLT: Partly. Tradition links some shapes with some gods, but local custom and the Tantri’s choice matter too. There are round Śiva shrines and square ones.

INSIDE THE SANCTUM

Garbhagṛha, ambulatory and steps

Garbhagṛha. The innermost chamber, the “womb-house”, small, dark and windowless, where the deity is installed. Only priests enter.

Sāndhāra. Larger sanctums have a second wall around the garbhagṛha, with an enclosed passage between: a sāndhāra plan. Smaller ones have a single wall (nirandhāra).

Sopāna. A flight of granite steps, often with curved and carved side rails, leads up to the sanctum door from the court.

Mukhamaṇḍapa. Some shrines have a small closed porch (mukhamaṇḍapa) or vestibule (antarāla) in front; many have none, and the door opens straight onto the steps.

The door. Heavy wooden doors with bronze fittings, sometimes guarded by carved door-keepers (dvārapālakas) on either side.

ZAPPY: Why is the sanctum so dark?

PROF. BOLT: The womb is dark. The lamps inside let you see the Lord as a glow, not as an exhibit.

THE ROOF AND ITS FRAME

The crown of a Kerala temple

Timber frame. The roof is a frame of wooden rafters (kaḻukkōl) radiating from a central point or ridge, resting on wall plates, joined without nails by precise carpentry. It is the master carpenter’s showpiece.

Covering. Great temples cover the frame with copper sheets, which weather to a green-brown; others use clay tiles or, in old times, thatch.

Tiers. A second, smaller roof often rises above the first, with a short vertical wall between: a two-tiered (dvitala) shrine.

Gables. On square shrines, small decorated gable fronts (mukhappu) project from the roof, often with carved faces or kīrtimukha.

The finial. A gold or copper kalaśa crowns the top. On round shrines the roof is a cone ending in the finial.

ZAPPY: No nails at all?

PROF. BOLT: Traditional joinery uses pegs and interlocking joints. A good frame can be taken apart and reassembled.

NAMASKĀRA MAṆḌAPAM

The pavilion before the sanctum

What it is. A square, open pavilion on pillars, with a pyramidal roof, standing in the inner court directly in front of the sanctum. It is a separate building, not joined to the shrine.

What it is for. Here the priests chant the Vedas, prepare the sacred pots for abhiṣeka and perform rites such as kalaśa pūjā. The name means “pavilion of salutation”.

Its ceiling. The ceiling is often the finest carving in the temple: panels of the eight guardians of the directions, the nine planets, or scenes from the epics, with Brahmā at the centre.

Its size. Its size follows the sanctum’s by fixed ratios; some temples have none, small ones may have a simple platform.

ZAPPY: Can devotees sit in it?

PROF. BOLT: Usually not. It belongs to the priests and the rituals; devotees bow from the side.

THE NĀLAMBALAM

The cloister around the inner court

Four halls. Nāl is four, ambalam a hall: a continuous cloister on four sides enclosing the inner court, with tiled roofs on wooden pillars and a raised floor. From outside it is a wall; from inside, a verandah.

Valiyambalam. The broad front hall at the entrance, where devotees gather and where some rituals and performances happen.

Tiḍappaḷḷi. The temple kitchen, usually in the south-east corner (the corner of Agni, fire), where the offerings to the deity are cooked.

Maṇikkiṇaṟ. A sacred well inside or near the cloister supplies water for the deity’s bath; no other water is used.

Stores and shrines. The cloister also holds store rooms and small shrines for attendant deities such as Gaṇapati and Śāstā.

ZAPPY: Why is the kitchen in the south-east?

PROF. BOLT: Vāstu gives that corner to Agni, the fire god. A temple kitchen is itself a sacred fire.

THE BALI STONES

Seats for the Lord’s attendants

What they are. Small stones or stone pedestals, called balikkal or balipīṭha, placed around the sanctum and along the enclosures. Each is the seat of one of the deity’s attendants (bhūtagaṇa) or of a guardian of a direction (dikpāla).

Bali. In the śīvēli ritual a priest walks round with the deity’s image and offers cooked rice (bali) on each stone, so the Lord’s whole household is fed.

The great altar. Before the entrance stands the large valiya balikkal, often lotus-shaped, sometimes sheltered by its own pavilion. Devotees offer here and must not touch or step around it carelessly.

Inside and outside. There are two rings of bali stones: an inner one in the sanctum court and an outer one beyond the cloister. Together they mark the temple as the Lord’s palace, guarded on every side.

ZAPPY: Why feed invisible attendants?

PROF. BOLT: A king’s palace feeds all its servants. The temple is the Lord’s palace; no one in it goes hungry, seen or unseen.

LAMPS AND THE FLAGSTAFF

Vilakkumāṭam, dīpastambham and koḍimaram

Vilakkumāṭam. The outer face of the cloister is often clad in a wooden lattice holding hundreds or thousands of small brass lamps in rows: the vilakkumāṭam, “house of lamps”. On great nights they are all lit, and the temple glows.

Dīpastambham. A tall lamp tower, of bronze or stone, rising in tiers of little lamp cups, stands before the main entrance. It is lit at dusk.

Koḍimaram. The flagstaff (dhvajastambha) stands in line between the entrance and the sanctum, outside the cloister. A tall wooden core, often a single teak trunk, is sheathed in gold, copper or bronze rings, and crowned by the deity’s emblem: Garuḍa for Viṣṇu, Nandi for Śiva, a lion for the Goddess.

Flag-raising. A temple festival begins when the festival flag is hoisted on the koḍimaram (koḍiyēṟṟam) and ends when it comes down.

ZAPPY: Is the flagstaff just decoration?

PROF. BOLT: It is the temple’s spine, set in line with the deity, and consecrated like a shrine. Devotees prostrate before it.

GATEWAYS, WALL AND WATER

The outer temple

Gopuram. In Kerala the gopuram is usually a gatehouse, not a tower: one or two storeys with sloping roofs, a raised floor and a passage through the middle. A few temples near Tamil country, like Thiruvananthapuram, have Tamil-style towers.

Four gates. Large temples have a gateway on each side, with the main one facing the deity. Some gates open only for festivals.

The wall. The boundary wall (maryādā) of laterite marks the sacred ground. Within it runs the outer procession path.

The tank. A temple tank (kuḷam) with stepped sides, for bathing before worship and for the deity’s festival bath (āṟāṭṭu).

Ūṭṭupura. A long dining hall where devotees are fed; in some temples it is as famous as the shrine.

ZAPPY: Why not tall towers like Tamil temples?

PROF. BOLT: In Kerala the sanctum is the landmark, low and broad. The gatehouse is a doorway, not a beacon.

THE KŪTTAMBALAM

A theatre for the gods

Where. In larger temples, usually in the outer court to the south-east or right of the sanctum, stands a separate hall built only for performance: the kūttambalam.

The design. A tall rectangular hall with a steep roof, a square stage (raṅgamaṇḍapa) with its own pillars and roof inside, a green room behind, and space for spectators in front. Its proportions follow the Nāṭyaśāstra’s rules for a theatre.

What is performed. Kūṭiyāṭṭam, the ancient Sanskrit theatre of Kerala, recognised by UNESCO as an intangible heritage, and the solo arts of cākyār kūttu and naṅṅyār kūttu.

Acoustics and light. The wooden structure carries sound well, and performances are lit by a great bell-metal oil lamp at the stage front.

ZAPPY: A theatre as part of a temple plan?

PROF. BOLT: In Kerala, drama is an offering. The theatre is built facing the god, who is the first spectator.

LATERITE, GRANITE, TIMBER, METAL

What a Kerala temple is made of

🧱 Laterite (veṭṭukallu)

Red, porous stone cut straight from the ground in blocks. Soft when quarried, it hardens in the air. Used for walls, cloisters and boundary walls, usually plastered with lime.

🪨 Granite

Hard grey stone for the base (adhiṣṭhāna), steps, bali stones and, in some temples, whole walls and pillars.

🌳 Timber

Teak, jackwood and other hardwoods for the roof frame, pillars, brackets, doors, ceilings and wall panels. The choice of tree was ritual as well as practical.

🔔 Metals

Copper sheets for roofs; bell metal and bronze for lamps, flagstaff sheathing and finials; gold leaf for the kalaśa on great temples.

ZAPPY: Do temples still use these?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Restorations try to use the same materials and methods, including lime plaster and traditional joinery.

THE CARVER’S TEMPLE

Wood carving in Kerala temples

Brackets. Where roof rafters meet the walls, carved wooden brackets support the eaves. Many are figures: dancers, gods, musicians, animals, all set at an angle like caryatids.

Ceilings. The ceiling of the namaskāra maṇḍapam and of gateways is often divided into panels. A favourite plan has nine squares: Brahmā in the centre and the eight guardians of the directions around him. Some show the nine planets or scenes from the epics.

Walls. On some sanctums the whole wall is wood, carved with scenes from the Rāmāyaṇa and the Bhāgavatam in deep relief, panel after panel round the shrine.

The style. Kerala wood carving is lively and full of movement, with deep cutting and rounded forms, and is closely linked with the styles of murals and kathakaḷi.

ZAPPY: Why carve figures where no one looks?

PROF. BOLT: Someone looks: the Lord. And the devotee who walks round slowly. Kerala carvers hid treasures in the shadows on purpose.

THE PAINTED WALLS

Kerala mural painting

Where. Sanctum walls, cloisters and gatehouses of many Kerala temples, as well as palaces, carry murals painted from about the 15th to the 19th centuries.

Five colours. The style is known for its five colours (pañcavarṇa): red and yellow ochre, green, white and black, from natural minerals and plants. Figures are outlined in dark lines and filled with rich, warm colour.

Subjects. Gods and goddesses, especially Viṣṇu, Śiva and Kṛṣṇa, scenes from the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata, and tāntric forms. Figures are large, with full bodies, ornate crowns and long eyes.

Rules. Painters followed iconographic rules from texts like the Śilparatna for proportions, colours and attributes.

Revival. After a decline, the art was revived in the 20th century; Guruvāyūr runs an institute that teaches it.

ZAPPY: Why paint on walls that rain can reach?

PROF. BOLT: They are painted on the inner, sheltered walls, on lime plaster. The deep eaves of the roof protect them, part of the same design.

MEASURE AND PROPORTION

How a Kerala temple is sized

The module. Everything begins from the sanctum. Its width, chosen by the Tantri and the master builder, becomes the base unit; the heights of base, wall and roof, and the sizes of the maṇḍapam and cloister follow by set ratios.

The rod. Builders measure with a rod, the kōl, divided into smaller units. Different texts and families use slightly different standards.

Auspicious numbers. Before building, the dimensions are checked by traditional calculations (often called āyādi) so that the measurements yield auspicious results, as is done for Kerala houses too.

Orientation. The temple faces the deity’s direction, usually east or west, and the axis runs from the sanctum through the maṇḍapam, bali stone, flagstaff and main gate in a straight line.

The eye. Legends say masters like Perumtaccan needed no rod at all. Their skill was the rules, learned so well they became instinct.

ZAPPY: Why so much calculation?

PROF. BOLT: Because the temple is a body for the god. Its proportions are treated like the proportions of a human body: right, they bring life; wrong, they bring trouble.

A WALK INTO A KERALA TEMPLE

Putting it all together
The gopuram. You enter through the gatehouse in the boundary wall and wash your feet at the tank or tap.
Lamp tower and flagstaff. You bow before the dīpastambham and the gilded koḍimaram on the axis of the Lord.
The great bali stone. You pass the valiya balikkal at the entrance of the cloister, careful not to step over it.
Through the nālambalam. You enter the cloister’s front hall into the inner court, and walk round clockwise, past the bali stones of the attendants.
Before the Lord. At the steps of the śrīkōvil, with the namaskāra maṇḍapam beside you, you see the Lord in lamplight. You do not cross the water channel of the praṇāla.

ZAPPY: So every part I pass has a job?

PROF. BOLT: Every one. By the time you reach the sanctum, the architecture has prepared you.

The Kerala temple in five lines
Climate. Rain and timber gave Kerala low walls, granite bases and wide sloping roofs of copper and tile.

ZAPPY: What should I look for first in a Kerala temple?

PROF. BOLT: The roof over the sanctum and the line from the gate to the Lord. Then read everything else as a ring around that line.
