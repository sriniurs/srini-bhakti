Chottanikkara

The upper shrine and Kīḻkkāvu · explained and illustrated

Mēlkkāvu above, Kīḻkkāvu below

HOW THIS WORKS: First the legends: a forest chieftain’s calf and a saint by a pond. Then the two shrines, how the Goddess changes through the day above and blazes in the evening below, and how the two shape worship together.

WHERE WE ARE

From Vaḍakkunnāthan’s hill in Part 4 we come south to the outskirts of Kochi, to Chottanikkara, one of Kerala’s best-loved Devī temples. Devotees call her simply Chottanikkara Amma, Mother of Chottanikkara.

The temple has two shrines. Above stands the Mēlkkāvu, “upper grove”, where the Goddess is worshipped with Viṣṇu as Rājarājeśvarī. Below, by a pond, is the Kīḻkkāvu, “lower grove”, where she is the fierce Bhadrakālī.

The two shrines work together: calm and fierce, morning and night, blessing and protection. This guide explains each and how they shape the day.

ZAPPY: Two shrines for one Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: One Mother, two moods. Above she sits with Viṣṇu and blesses. Below she fights for you.

ZAPPY: Why do people chant Nārāyaṇa to a Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: Because at Chottanikkara she sits with Viṣṇu, and the name joins them. Every name of hers ends in Nārāyaṇa.

WHERE IT IS

Two levels of one temple

Near Kochi. Chottanikkara lies a short way south-east of Kochi in Ernakulam district, among paddy fields and coconut groves. The village has grown around its temple.

Above. The main temple, the Mēlkkāvu, stands on the higher ground inside its enclosure, with the Goddess’s sanctum, a shrine of Śiva and the attendant deities.

Below. Steps lead down to a large pond in the lower ground. Beside it, facing west, is the Kīḻkkāvu, the shrine of Bhadrakālī.

The order. Devotees worship above first, then walk down to the Kīḻkkāvu, especially in the evening for the guruti.

KAṆṆAPPAN’S CALF

How the Goddess was found in the forest

The story

A fierce chieftain. Long ago this land was forest, ruled by Kaṇṇappan, a fierce chieftain who sacrificed an animal to Kālī every day.
A calf spared. One day he brought a calf for sacrifice. His beloved daughter begged him to spare it, and he gave it to her as a pet.
A loss. The daughter died soon after. Grieving, Kaṇṇappan went to take the calf at last, but in its place he found two stones.
Lakṣmī and Viṣṇu. A wise man told him the stones were the Goddess and Viṣṇu, and that the calf had been the divine couple all along. Kaṇṇappan gave up killing and worshipped the stones for the rest of his life.
Forgotten and found. Centuries later a woman cutting grass sharpened her sickle on a stone in the overgrown spot, and it bled. The priest who came recognised the lost Goddess, and the temple rose there.

ZAPPY: Is the image just a stone?

PROF. BOLT: A red laterite stone, not fixed to the ground but set on sand, they say. It is covered by gold ornaments and a golden face, and worshipped as the Mother.

A killer’s grief became devotion: Chottanikkara begins with mercy.

THE UPPER SHRINE

Mēlkkāvu: the Mother with Viṣṇu

Rājarājeśvarī. In the upper sanctum the Goddess is worshipped as Rājarājeśvarī, queen of queens, together with Viṣṇu. Many call the pair Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa; the temple is counted among the Vaiṣṇava abhimāna kṣetras.

Three forms in a day. She is dressed and worshipped as Sarasvatī in white in the morning, as Lakṣmī in red at noon, and as Durgā in blue in the evening: learning, prosperity and protection, one Mother through the day.

Learning. Children are brought here to begin their letters, at a Sarasvatī maṇḍapam near the shrine.

Śiva and others. A Śiva shrine, Gaṇapati, Śāstā and the serpent deities stand in the enclosure; the sacred tree and shrines of Yakṣī and Jyēṣṭhā Bhagavatī are in the courtyard.

Mūkāmbikā. One tradition says the Goddess of Kollūr, Mūkāmbikā, is present here early in the morning, which is why the doors open so early.

ZAPPY: Why change her clothes three times a day?

PROF. BOLT: To show that one Mother carries every power. Morning for the student, noon for the householder, evening for the one in danger.

THE LOWER SHRINE

Kīḻkkāvu: Bhadrakālī by the pond

The story

The saint. Vilvamaṅgalam Svāmiyār, the saint who could see the gods (we met him at Guruvāyūr and Padmanābha), came to Chottanikkara.
The vision. Tradition says he saw the Goddess’s fierce power present by the pond in the lower ground, and that it had to be given a place of its own.
Bhadrakālī. So the Kīḻkkāvu was set up for Bhadrakālī, the form of the Goddess born, in Kerala story, from Śiva’s third eye to destroy the demon Dārika. She faces west, toward the pond.
Protector. Here the Mother is fierce on behalf of her devotees: she is prayed to for protection from harm, fear and troubling spirits.
Above and below. So the temple holds both: blessing above, protection below. Devotees visit both, in that order.

ZAPPY: Isn’t a fierce goddess frightening?

PROF. BOLT: Only to what harms you. Kerala sees Bhadrakālī as a mother whose anger is for her children, not against them.

Mēlkkāvu blesses; Kīḻkkāvu protects.
Other tellings connect the Kīḻkkāvu with a fearsome yakṣī subdued by Vilvamaṅgalam, and the pond with that event. Traditions differ.

THE GURUTI PŪJĀ

The evening rite at the lower shrine

What it is. Guruti means a red liquid. In the evening, at the Kīḻkkāvu, a priest prepares a mixture of water, turmeric and slaked lime, which turns deep red, and offers it to Bhadrakālī with lamps, drums and mantras.

Why red. The red water stands in place of a blood offering. The fierce Goddess is satisfied with a symbol; no life is taken.

When. Once held only on Fridays, it is now performed every evening, and is the most solemn moment of the temple’s day.

What it is for. Devotees attend it to pray for protection, for the removal of fear, and for loved ones who are suffering.

ZAPPY: So it isn’t real blood?

PROF. BOLT: Turmeric and lime. The fierce form is honoured fully, and nothing is harmed. That is Kerala’s answer to old sacrifice.

A PLACE OF REFUGE

Prayer for those in distress

A long reputation. For centuries families have brought relatives suffering from mental distress to Chottanikkara, believing the Mother heals troubles of the mind and frees people from what Kerala tradition calls evil spirits.

The nail tree. Near the Kīḻkkāvu stands an old pāla tree studded with nails. Tradition says the nails were driven in by sufferers as a sign that the troubling spirit had been bound to the tree and left them.

Staying on. Some families stay near the temple for days or weeks, attending worship daily and praying together.

Today. Many families now combine prayer here with medical care. Faith and treatment are not rivals.

ZAPPY: Does the temple replace a doctor?

PROF. BOLT: No, and wise devotees don’t treat it that way. Prayer gives strength and hope; doctors give treatment. Many Kerala families use both.

MAKAM TOḺAL

The great darśan of the year

The day. The temple’s great festival falls on the day of Makam star in the month of Kumbham (February–March), during the annual festival.

The vision. Tradition says that on this day the Goddess appeared to Vilvamaṅgalam in full splendour, dressed and adorned like a queen. On Makam day she is shown to devotees in that form.

The crowds. Huge numbers come for Makam toḻal, “worship on Makam”. Women especially pray for good marriages and happy homes.

Navarātri. Navarātri in Kanni (September–October) is the other great season, with Vidyārambham for children on Vijayadaśamī.

ZAPPY: Why is one darśan so special?

PROF. BOLT: Because it repeats the saint’s vision. On that day the Mother is seen as he saw her.

HOW THE TWO SHRINES SHAPE WORSHIP

A day at Chottanikkara
Dawn. The upper doors open early; devotees see the Mother as Sarasvatī in white. Students and teachers come first.
Noon. The great noon pūjā, with the Mother as Lakṣmī in red. Families pray for home and livelihood.
Evening. At dusk she is Durgā in blue, and the lamps are lit.
Down the steps. Devotees then walk down to the Kīḻkkāvu for the guruti pūjā to Bhadrakālī.
The chant. Through it all one chant joins the shrines: “Ammē Nārāyaṇa, Dēvī Nārāyaṇa, Lakṣmī Nārāyaṇa, Bhadrē Nārāyaṇa”.

ZAPPY: So the temple day is a journey downwards?

PROF. BOLT: From calm to fierce, from light to dark, from asking for gifts to asking for protection. Both are the same Mother.

A LONG HISTORY

From the forest to a pilgrim town
Legend Kaṇṇappan and the calf; the stones found again by a grass-cutter
Early Tradition dates the temple to around the 10th century; firm records are later
Medieval Vilvamaṅgalam sets up the Kīḻkkāvu
Later The temple comes under the Cochin royal house’s care
Today Managed by the Cochin Devaswom Board

A people’s temple. Chottanikkara’s fame spread not through kings but through families who came in hope and went home comforted. That is still its character.
