PART 6 OF 10 · BHADRAKĀLĪ · BHARAṆI · MUZIRIS

Kodungallur

Bhadrakālī, Bharaṇi and the Muziris landscape · explained and illustrated

Kodungallur Amma and her oracles

HOW THIS WORKS: First the Goddess and her legends: Dārika’s defeat and Kaṇṇaki’s arrival. Then the Bharaṇi festival, when oracles in red fill the temple. Finally the land around, the ancient port of Muziris, and what the historical sources actually say.

WHERE WE ARE

After the twin shrines of Chottanikkara in Part 5 we go north-west to Kodungallur, near where the river Periyar meets the sea. Its temple of Śrī Kurumba Bhagavatī, called simply Kodungallur Amma, is held by many to be the mother shrine of all Bhadrakālī temples in Kerala.

Here the Goddess is at her fiercest: eight-armed, holding the head of the demon Dārika. Once a year, at Bharaṇi, oracles from across Kerala gather in red, swords in hand, in one of the most intense festivals of India.

And the land around is ancient: this coast was home to Muziris, the port where Roman ships came for pepper two thousand years ago. Legend, festival and history are layered here, and this guide separates them carefully.

ZAPPY: Is this temple frightening?

PROF. BOLT: It is fierce, and proud of it. But devotees call her Amma, Mother. Her fierceness is aimed at what harms her children.

WHERE IT IS

Where the river meets the sea

The river mouth. Kodungallur sits near the place where the Periyar river reaches the Arabian Sea through a maze of backwaters. For two thousand years this was one of the great harbours of the Indian Ocean.

The temple ground. The temple stands in about ten acres of open ground shaded by huge banyan and peepal trees, more like an ancient sacred grove (kāvu) than a walled temple. That is why it is called Kurumbakkāvu, the grove of Kurumba.

Neighbours. Close by are the Tiruvañcikkuḷam Śiva temple, patron shrine of the old Cēra kings, and the Cēramān Juma Masjid, traditionally counted among India’s oldest mosques. Christians remember this coast as where Saint Thomas is said to have landed.

Muziris. A few kilometres south, at Pattanam, archaeologists have dug up remains of an ancient port town that many identify with Muziris.

BHADRAKĀLĪ AND DĀRIKA

The Goddess at her fiercest

The story

The demon. Dārika, a demon king, won a boon that no man or god could kill him, and began to terrorise the three worlds.
The gap. He had forgotten to ask protection from a woman. The gods went to Śiva.
The third eye. From Śiva’s third eye burst forth Bhadrakālī, black and blazing, born for one purpose: to end Dārika.
The battle. After a terrible fight, aided by her companion spirits, she cut off Dārika’s head. In her eight-armed image here, one hand holds that head.
Peace. Her fury cooled, she became the protecting Mother of Kerala. The story is retold in temple songs and in the ritual drama muḍiyēṯṯu across the state.

ZAPPY: Is Dārika in the Purāṇas?

PROF. BOLT: The Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa has the Goddess slaying many demons; the Dārika story is Kerala’s own, told in temple songs and drama. It is local and very old.

Bhadrakālī: born of Śiva’s anger, kept for her children’s protection.

KAṆṆAKI COMES TO KODUNGALLUR

A Tamil epic and a Kerala goddess

The story

The epic. The Tamil epic Cilappatikāram, “the tale of the anklet”, by Iḷaṅkō Aṭikaḷ, tells of Kaṇṇaki, whose husband Kōvalaṉ was wrongly executed in Madurai for stealing the queen’s anklet.
Proof. Kaṇṇaki broke her own anklet before the king: it held rubies, the queen’s held pearls. The king had killed an innocent man, and died of shame.
Fire. In her grief and rage Kaṇṇaki cursed Madurai, and the city burned. Then she walked west into the Cēra country.
The Cēra king. The epic says the Cēra king Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ brought a stone from the Himālaya to make her image and honoured her as a goddess.
Kodungallur. Kerala tradition says Kaṇṇaki came to Kodungallur, prayed to Bhadrakālī and merged with her, and some explain the town’s name, Koṭuṅ-kal-ūr, as “the place of the great stone”.

ZAPPY: So is the Goddess Kaṇṇaki?

PROF. BOLT: Many devotees, especially from Tamil country, see her so. Historians are cautious: the link is tradition, not record. Both can be held with respect.

A Tamil heroine and a Kerala goddess meet at Kodungallur, in tradition.
Other traditions say Paraśurāma installed the Goddess, or that the shrine was once Śiva’s. Some speak of a sealed chamber east of the sanctum. Traditions differ, and none of these can be checked.

THE GODDESS IN THE SANCTUM

Eight arms, jackwood and a north-facing door

The image. The Goddess is about six to seven feet tall, carved from the wood of a jackfruit tree. She has eight arms holding weapons and symbols, and one holds the severed head of Dārika. Her face is covered by a mask, and she is richly adorned.

Facing north. Her sanctum faces north, unusually. In the same inner structure the Seven Mothers (Brāhmī, Vaiṣṇavī, Māheśvarī, Indrāṇī, Vārāhī, Kaumārī and Cāmuṇḍā) also sit facing north, with Gaṇapati and Vīrabhadra.

Śrīcakra. A tradition holds that Ādi Śaṅkara installed a Śrīcakra here to calm the Goddess’s fierce power; devotees worship it near the sanctum.

The priests. Some of the inner rites are performed by the Aḍikaḷ, a hereditary community of worshippers, alongside Brahmin priests: a reminder of the temple’s very old, mixed roots.

Care of the image. Being wood, the image is not bathed; instead it is periodically coated with a protective preparation.

ZAPPY: Why does a fierce goddess face north?

PROF. BOLT: Traditions differ. Many say she faces the direction from which danger once came, keeping watch over Kerala.

THE BHARAṆI FESTIVAL

A month of fire, red and drums

The story

The season. The festival runs for about a month, from Bharaṇi star in Kumbham to a week after Bharaṇi in Mīnam (February–April). Pilgrims come especially from the north of Kerala, in groups, walking and singing.
Koḻikkallu mūṭal. It opens with the “covering of the cock-stone”: red cloths are laid over sacred stones with offerings of fowls. Fowls were once sacrificed here; animal sacrifice was later banned under the Cochin government, and accounts differ on how the rite is done today.
The oracles. The temple fills with veḷiccappāḍ, “those who bring light”: oracles in red, with anklets and curved swords, who enter trance and speak for the Goddess. Some strike their own foreheads with their swords.
Kāvu tīṇḍal. On Aśvati day, the day before Bharaṇi, the Kodungallur Tampurān, head of the local royal house, gives a signal from a platform. Crowds then rush round the temple three times, striking its roof with bamboo staves and throwing offerings over it: the Kāvu tīṇḍal, “touching the grove”.
Closing. After the festival the temple is closed for about a week, purified, and reopened.

ZAPPY: Why is it so wild?

PROF. BOLT: It retells Bhadrakālī’s victory, with her companion spirits running riot. It is also a festival of the people: for centuries castes kept out of other temples were welcome here at Bharaṇi.

Bharaṇi is Kerala’s most intense festival: trance, swords and a crowd running round a temple.
The festival’s traditional songs (Bharaṇi pāṭṭu) are famously rough and earthy; their meaning and their place are debated, and many groups now sing devotional songs instead. We note the tradition without reproducing it.

THE VEḶICCAPPĀḌ

The oracles of Kerala

Who they are. A veḷiccappāḍ is a man or woman who serves a Bhagavatī shrine and, in trance, becomes her voice. They are found across Kerala, but Kodungallur at Bharaṇi sees the largest gathering of them anywhere.

Their marks. Red robes, a red cloth on the head, jingling anklets (ciḷampu), a belt of bells, and the curved sword (vāḷ) of the Goddess. Many keep their hair long.

In trance. In trance they dance, speak blessings and warnings, and sometimes strike their foreheads with the sword. For devotees this is the Goddess acting through a human body.

A hereditary role. The role often runs in families and is tied to a local shrine. At Kodungallur they come, in a sense, home to the mother shrine.

ZAPPY: Is the trance real?

PROF. BOLT: To devotees, yes: it is the Goddess. To others, a powerful state of devotion. Either way, it is old, sincere and deeply Keralan.

THE MUZIRIS LANDSCAPE

A port that traded with Rome

A famous name. Greek and Roman writers knew a port on this coast called Muziris, Tamil Muciri. Sangam-age Tamil poems describe Yavana (foreign) ships arriving with gold and leaving with pepper.

The trade. Black pepper, pearls, ivory and fine cloth went west; Roman gold and wine came east. A Greek papyrus of the 2nd century, the “Muziris papyrus”, records a loan for cargo shipped from here.

Lost. The port faded long ago. A traditional account links its end to a great flood of the Periyar in 1341, which reshaped the river mouth and raised Kochi as the new harbour.

Pattanam. Since 2007 excavations at Pattanam, a few kilometres south of Kodungallur, have found Roman amphora pieces, beads and other remains of an Indian Ocean port town. Many scholars identify it with Muziris; others are cautious.

The temple and the port. Did the Goddess watch over this port? Tradition likes to think so. What can be said is that this landscape has been a place of meeting, of peoples, faiths and goods, for two thousand years.

ZAPPY: Was the temple there in Roman times?

PROF. BOLT: We do not know. The port is attested by texts and digs; the temple’s age is tradition. Keep the two kinds of evidence apart.

WHAT THE SOURCES SAY

Separating legend, tradition and history

📜 Legend

Dārika’s slaying and Kaṇṇaki’s arrival come from Kerala temple songs and the Tamil Cilappatikāram. They tell what the Goddess means, not when the temple was built.

🔥 Living tradition

The Bharaṇi rites, the oracles and the Aḍikaḷ priests point to very old, pre-Brahmanical layers of worship that later merged with temple ritual. That is how many scholars read them.

🏺 Historical evidence

Muziris is real: Greek, Roman and Tamil texts and the finds at Pattanam attest an ancient port nearby. The temple’s own age is not dated by inscriptions known to us.

⚖️ How to hold them

Honour the legends as the temple’s truth, the rites as living heritage, and the evidence as history. Do not let one stand in for another.

ZAPPY: Isn’t it disappointing that the temple can’t be dated?

PROF. BOLT: Not at all. It means the worship is older than anyone wrote down. The festival itself is the record.

A LONG HISTORY

Port, kingdom and grove
c. 1st–3rd c. Muziris trades with Rome; Sangam poems describe it
Legend Kaṇṇaki comes to the Cēra country; the Goddess is honoured at Kodungallur
9th–12th c. The later Cēra kings (Cēramān Perumāḷs) rule from Makōtai, at Kodungallur
1341 A great flood of the Periyar is said to silt up the old harbour
Later The Kodungallur royal house becomes the temple’s patron; the Tampurān presides at Bharaṇi
20th c. Animal sacrifice is banned; the festival continues in changed forms
2007– Excavations at Pattanam

A capital. For a time Kodungallur, as Makōtai or Mahōdayapuram, was the capital of the Cēra kings of Kerala, and its temples and port were the heart of the state.
