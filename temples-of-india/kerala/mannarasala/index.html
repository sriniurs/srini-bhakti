Mannārśāla

Serpent worship in a sacred grove · explained and illustrated

The sacred grove of Mannārśāla

HOW THIS WORKS: First the legends: Paraśurāma’s land of serpents, a forest fire and a mother who bore a serpent child. Then the grove and its images, the priestess, and the offerings that bring families here from all over Kerala.

WHERE WE ARE

From the streets of Vaikom in Part 7 we go south, near Haripad in Alappuzha district, to Mannārśāla. It is not a walled temple but a sacred grove of tall trees, its paths lined with tens of thousands of stone serpent images.

Here the god is Nāgarāja, the king of serpents, worshipped with his consorts Sarpayakṣī and Nāgayakṣī. And here, uniquely among Kerala’s great temples, the chief priest is a woman: the Mannārśāla Amma, the eldest lady of the family that tends the shrine.

Serpent worship in Kerala is ancient, rooted in the groves (sarpakkāvu) that once stood beside almost every home. Mannārśāla is its greatest centre.

ZAPPY: Why would anyone worship snakes?

PROF. BOLT: In Kerala, serpents are guardians of the land, the water and the family line. A grove left for the snakes is also a grove left for the earth.

ZAPPY: A woman as chief priest in a Brahmin temple?

PROF. BOLT: At Mannārśāla, for centuries. The tradition comes from the legend itself, as you will see.

WHERE IT IS

A grove near Haripad

In the grove. Mannārśāla lies a short way from the town of Haripad, in the low wet country of Alappuzha district. The temple stands in a grove of tall trees, shady and quiet, with no towers or high walls.

The images. Along every path, at the foot of every tree, stand serpent images in stone: one hood, three hoods, five hoods, some coiled, some rising. Many have been offered by families in thanks over the generations.

The family house. Beside the temple is the illam, the ancestral house of the Mannārśāla family. The priestess lives here, and the most secret worship is done in its cellar, the Nilavara.

A kāvu. Mannārśāla is the grandest example of the Kerala sarpakkāvu, the serpent grove once found beside countless homes: a patch of forest left untouched for the snakes and their god.

PARAŚURĀMA AND THE SERPENTS

Why Kerala keeps groves for its snakes

The story

Salt land. When Paraśurāma raised Kerala from the sea (as we saw at Vaḍakkunnāthan), the new land was salty and barren, and it swarmed with snakes. No one could live there.
A prayer. Paraśurāma prayed to Nāgarāja, king of serpents. The serpent king appeared, and his serpents spread their poison over the land, which turned it sweet and fertile.
A promise. In return Nāgarāja asked that serpents be honoured in the new land. Paraśurāma promised that every household would keep a grove for them.
Mannārśāla. He chose this spot for the serpent king’s own shrine, installed Nāgarāja with Sarpayakṣī, and gave the worship to a learned Brahmin family, the ancestors of the Mannārśāla family.
The groves. That, Kerala says, is why so many old homes have a sarpakkāvu: a promise kept from the beginning of the land.

ZAPPY: Poison made the land fertile?

PROF. BOLT: Poison and medicine are close in Kerala’s Āyurveda. The story says the serpents healed the soil: guard them and they guard you.

Every serpent grove in Kerala echoes Paraśurāma’s promise.

THE FIRE AND THE SERPENT CHILD

The legend of the Mannārśāla family

The story

A childless couple. Long ago Vāsudēvan and Śrīdēvi of the Mannārśāla family had no children. They prayed to Nāgarāja day after day.
The fire. A great forest fire swept the land (many link it to the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest in the Mahābhārata). Burnt and dying serpents crawled into the pits around the couple’s home.
Cooling the earth. The couple poured water and cooling medicines on the hot soil and nursed the serpents. Tradition says the place was named for this: maṇṇ-āṟiya-śāla, “the place where the earth was cooled”, became Mannārśāla.
The blessing. Nāgarāja appeared and blessed Śrīdēvi with children. She bore a human son and a five-hooded serpent: Nāgarāja himself, born as her child.
The Nilavara. The serpent child told his mother he would stay in the cellar of the house, the Nilavara, in eternal meditation, guarding the family. He is believed to be there still.

ZAPPY: A mother gave birth to a serpent?

PROF. BOLT: That is the family’s sacred story. It explains why the mothers of this family, and not the fathers, became the god’s priests: he is their child.

The god of Mannārśāla is the family’s own son: hence its priestess-mother.

MANNĀRŚĀLA AMMA

The priestess-mother

Who she is. The chief priestess, called Mannārśāla Amma or Valiyamma, “the elder mother”, is traditionally the senior-most woman who has married into the Mannārśāla family. When she takes office she gives up ordinary family life and dedicates herself wholly to the serpent king.

Why a woman. In the legend, the serpent god is Śrīdēvi’s own son. So the mother of the family serves him as a mother serves a child: this is the root of the custom.

Her duties. She performs the most sacred rites: the special worship on Āyilyam days, and the worship in the Nilavara, the cellar of the illam where Ananta is believed to meditate. Only she may enter it.

In procession. At the great festivals she carries the image of Nāgarāja in her own hands in procession from the temple to the illam, a sight that draws huge crowds.

Rare in Kerala. Mannārśāla is often described as the only major Kerala temple with a woman as its chief priest.

ZAPPY: Do the men of the family serve too?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, in the daily rituals of the temple. But the supreme rites belong to the Amma, and devotees seek her blessing above all.

THE SERPENT IMAGES

Stone offerings in their tens of thousands

Everywhere. The grove holds tens of thousands of serpent images (sources give 30,000 to over 100,000). They stand along paths, under trees, around the shrines.

Offered in thanks. Many are thank-offerings: couples who prayed here for a child return after the birth and offer a new serpent image, so the grove grows with every family’s joy.

Shapes. Some show a single hood, others three, five or seven; some are coiled, some paired as a couple. They are often smeared with turmeric, sacred to serpents.

Consecrated. The images are installed with rites and become part of the grove, never removed.

ZAPPY: Do people bring images from home?

PROF. BOLT: Families often have them carved and consecrated here. Each one is a story: a prayer made, a child born, a promise kept.

THE OFFERINGS

An upturned vessel and a bowl of milk

🫙 Uruḷi kamiḻttal

The most famous offering. A childless couple places a large bell-metal vessel, an uruḷi, upside down before Nāgarāja and prays for a child. When the child is born they return to turn the vessel upright and give thanks.

🥛 Nūṟum pālum

Milk mixed with rice flour and turmeric, offered to the serpents. It is common to all serpent shrines in Kerala.

🌼 Turmeric and flowers

Turmeric powder is sacred to the serpent gods. Devotees offer it and receive it back as prasādam, a cooling blessing.

🙏 For healing

Families also come to pray for relief from skin diseases and from fears they connect with offended serpents, and for the safety of their homes.

ZAPPY: Why turn a vessel upside down?

PROF. BOLT: An empty vessel turned over is a closed womb waiting to be filled. When the prayer is answered, you turn it right side up: the home is full.

ĀYILYAM

The serpent star and the great procession

The serpent star. Āyilyam (Āśleṣā) is the birth star of the serpents. Worship is special on Āyilyam day every month.

The great festivals. The greatest are the Āyilyam days of Tulām (October–November), Kanni (September–October) and Kumbham (February–March). The Tulām Āyilyam, often called Mannārśāla Āyilyam, is the biggest.

The procession. On these days the serpent images of the temple and the grove are carried in procession to the illam, the Amma herself carrying Nāgarāja. There she performs the Āyilyam pūjā.

ZAPPY: So the god goes home to his mother?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. On Āyilyam the serpent king is carried to the family house: the son visits the mother.

THE GROVE AS A FOREST

What sacred groves protect

Islands of forest. Kerala’s serpent groves are small patches of old forest that faith kept uncut. Ecologists value them as islands of native trees, medicinal plants, birds and small animals in a crowded land.

Water. Groves often stand beside ponds and help hold rainwater in the soil, feeding wells nearby. Elders say a house with a kāvu never lacks water.

Disappearing. As families moved and land was divided, many home groves were cleared, and their serpent images moved to temples like Mannārśāla. The big grove here keeps the old idea alive.

Faith and nature. The rule is simple: do not cut, do not burn, do not harm the snakes. The result is a forest.

ZAPPY: So worshipping snakes saved the trees?

PROF. BOLT: In Kerala, often, yes. Respect for the serpent became respect for the whole grove.

A LONG HISTORY

Legend The forest fire; Vāsudēvan and Śrīdēvi cool the earth; the serpent child
Tradition The mothers of the family become chief priestesses
20th c. The temple is rebuilt and grows into Kerala’s best-known serpent shrine
Today The family still manages the temple, with the Amma at its head

A family’s faith. Unlike the royal temples of this series, Mannārśāla grew from one family’s devotion, kept for generations, until all Kerala came to share it.
