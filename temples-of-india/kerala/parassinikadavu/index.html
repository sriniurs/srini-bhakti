Muthappan

Meeting Muthappan in performance · explained and illustrated

Muthappan blesses a devotee

HOW THIS WORKS: First who Muthappan is: a foundling, a hunter, a god who drank toddy. Then how he is worshipped, not as an image but as a living performer who speaks to each devotee, and what makes this North Kerala shrine different from every other temple in the series.

WHERE WE ARE

Our journey ends in the far north of Kerala, at Parassinikkadavu near Kannur, on the banks of the Valapattanam river. After nine temples of images and sanctums, this one is different: here the god comes out to meet you.

Muthappan is worshipped through theyyam, the ritual performance of North Kerala, in which a performer in costume and face paint becomes the deity. At Parassinikkadavu this happens every day. Devotees stand before him, tell him their troubles, and hear him answer.

He drinks toddy, eats dried fish, loves dogs and welcomes everyone, of every caste and religion. This guide explains the legend, the performance, and why North Kerala calls him simply “grandfather”.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A god you can talk to?

PROF. BOLT: That is the point of Muthappan. In other temples you look at the Lord. Here he looks at you, takes your hand and speaks.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
DeityMuthappan, “grandfather” or “elder father”, a form linked with Śiva (and in some tellings Viṣṇu)
Two formsTiruvappana (Valiya Muthappan, the elder) and Veḷḷāṭṭam (Cheriya Muthappan, the younger)
WorshipThrough daily theyyam: a performer becomes Muthappan and blesses devotees in person
ShrineThe Parassini Maḍappura, on the Valapattanam river near Kannur and Taliparamba
OfferingsDried fish, toddy and meat; prasādam of boiled pulses and tea
DogsMuthappan’s companions; honoured at the shrine and given prasādam first
Open to allDevotees of every caste and religion; free food for all
FestivalPuttari Tiruvappana, the harvest festival, in Vṛścikam (Nov–Dec)

ZAPPY: Why “grandfather”?

PROF. BOLT: Because that is what a frightened woman called him in the story, to beg his mercy, and the name stuck. It is affectionate, not formal.

WHERE IT IS

A river shrine in North Kerala

North Malabar. Parassinikkadavu lies in Kannur district, in North Malabar, about 20 km from Kannur town and 10 km from Taliparamba. This is the heartland of theyyam.

A maḍappura. Muthappan’s shrines are called maḍappura, not kṣetram. The Parassini Maḍappura, on the riverbank, is the most important of many across North Kerala.

By the river. Devotees come by road and, traditionally, by boat across the river. The shrine faces the water.

Not a Brahmin temple. The shrine is managed by a hereditary family, and its worship follows North Malabar folk tradition rather than the tāntric rules of the temples in Parts 1–9.

THE BOY FROM THE RIVER

How Muthappan came into the world

The story

A childless couple. Ayyankara Vāḻunnavar, the lord of Eruvessi, and his wife Pāḍikkuṭṭi Amma had no children. She was a devoted worshipper of Śiva.
By the river. One day, after bathing in the river, she found a radiant baby boy lying among flowers on the bank, and took him home as a gift from Śiva.
A strange child. The boy grew up unlike anyone in that strict household. He went hunting in the forest with bow and dogs, ate meat and drank toddy, and fed the poor.
The scolding. His parents begged and then scolded him. In answer he showed them his cosmic form, blazing with fire, and they understood that he was divine.
Leaving home. He left home for the hills, and his mother, though grieving, let him go. He wandered North Malabar, helping the poor and the hunted.

ZAPPY: Why a god who breaks the rules?

PROF. BOLT: Muthappan stands outside the rules of caste and ritual purity. That is exactly why the poor and the excluded loved him first.

Muthappan began as a foundling who would not live by the rules.

THE TODDY-TAPPER AND HIS WIFE

How Muthappan got his name and his offerings

The story

The thief. One day a toddy-tapper named Chandan found an old man drinking the toddy from his palm tree. Angry, he raised his bow to shoot.
Turned to stone. Before he could loose the arrow, he fell senseless, as if turned to stone.
The plea. His wife ran up and, seeing who the stranger must be, begged him, calling him “Muthappa!”, “grandfather”, to spare her husband.
The offering. Chandan revived. In thanks she offered what she had: boiled gram, slices of coconut, fish burnt in the fire, and toddy.
Ever since. Muthappan accepted it with pleasure. That simple meal became his offering forever, and “Muthappan” became his name.

ZAPPY: A god who accepts toddy and fish?

PROF. BOLT: He accepted what a poor woman had. That is the heart of Muthappan: he takes what you can give, not what the priests prescribe.

Muthappan’s offerings are a poor family’s meal: gram, coconut, fish and toddy.
Tellings differ in details and in the names. The story is sung in the theyyam’s own songs (tōṟṟam) at each performance.

TWO FORMS, ONE GOD

Tiruvappana and Veḷḷāṭṭam

Two together. Muthappan is worshipped in two forms who appear together: Tiruvappana, also called Valiya Muthappan, the elder, and Veḷḷāṭṭam, Cheriya Muthappan, the younger.

Who they are. Traditions differ. Many say Tiruvappana is Viṣṇu and Veḷḷāṭṭam Śiva; others see both as forms of Śiva, the younger as fierce Bhairava. Either way, Muthappan holds the great gods together in one folk deity.

How they look. Tiruvappana wears a tall, ornate headdress and full costume; Veḷḷāṭṭam’s costume is simpler. Both hold a bow and arrow, the hunter’s weapons.

No fixed image. Muthappan has no stone image in a sanctum like the other temples of this series. He is present in the performer during the theyyam, and in a symbolic form at the shrine between performances.

ZAPPY: So the god is wherever the performance is?

PROF. BOLT: In a way, yes. When the performer is dressed and possessed, Muthappan is there. That is why devotees bow to the man.

MEETING MUTHAPPAN

What happens in the daily performance

The story

Preparation. The performer, from a hereditary community of theyyam artists, fasts and prepares. His face is painted in bright patterns (mukhattezhuttu) and he is dressed in the god’s costume.
The song. Drummers and singers chant the tōṟṟam, the sacred song of Muthappan’s life, calling the god into the performer.
Possession. As he looks into a mirror and the drums rise, the performer becomes Muthappan. He dances, steps through the shrine, and takes the offerings.
The meeting. Then devotees line up. Muthappan takes each one by the hands, listens to their troubles, speaks to them in Malayalam, and blesses them, often touching their head.
Every day. At Parassinikkadavu this happens daily, morning and evening, nearly all year: rare, since most theyyams are performed only in a short season once a year.

ZAPPY: What does he say?

PROF. BOLT: Whatever you need to hear. People ask about illness, jobs, marriages, quarrels. He answers each in person. Many say the words stay with them for years.

At Parassinikkadavu the god speaks back.

WHAT IS THEYYAM?

North Kerala’s living gods

The word. Theyyam (from daivam, god) is the ritual performance of North Kerala in which a performer becomes a deity, a hero or an ancestor, for a night or a day.

Hundreds of gods. There are hundreds of theyyams: goddesses, heroes who died for justice, ancestors, protective spirits. Each has its own costume, song and story.

The performers. Theyyam is performed by men of particular hereditary communities. For those hours, people of every caste, including the highest, bow before them as before the god.

The season. Most theyyams are held in shrines and family groves from about October to May. Muthappan at Parassinikkadavu is the great exception, performed through the year.

The meaning. Many scholars see theyyam as one of India’s oldest living forms of worship, older than temple ritual, where the divine is met in a human body.

ZAPPY: So a performer of a so-called lower caste is worshipped as god?

PROF. BOLT: During the theyyam, yes, by everyone. North Kerala has lived with that reversal for centuries.

FISH, TODDY AND TEA

What is offered, and what is given back

🐟 Fish and toddy

Dried or burnt fish and toddy are offered to Muthappan, as Chandan’s wife first offered them. They are part of the ritual and are shared out.

🫘 Boiled gram and tea

The prasādam everyone receives is boiled pulses (usually green gram or black beans) with coconut slices, and tea. Simple, filling, shared by all.

🍛 Free meals

The shrine feeds every visitor free of charge, and during the festival also gives free lodging. No one is turned away hungry.

🙏 No barriers

Devotees of every caste and religion come and are served together. Muthappan’s shrine has long been known for this openness.

ZAPPY: Toddy at a shrine? Isn’t that against temple rules?

PROF. BOLT: Against Brahmin temple rules, yes. Muthappan belongs to an older, folk tradition with its own rules, and toddy is one of them.

THE DOGS OF MUTHAPPAN

A god who loves dogs

The hunter’s companions. Muthappan was a hunter, and hunters keep dogs. In his stories dogs always follow him, and in some tellings he is linked with Bhairava, the form of Śiva who rides a dog.

At the shrine. Dogs live freely around the Parassini Maḍappura and are treated as Muthappan’s own. Figures of dogs stand at the shrine’s entrance.

First share. Tradition says the prasādam is offered first to the dogs. A story tells of a time when dogs were driven away and the theyyam could not be performed until they were welcomed back.

A lesson. A god who will not be worshipped while his dogs are chased away: Muthappan’s care reaches the lowest creature.

ZAPPY: So no dog is ever chased away here?

PROF. BOLT: That is the custom. Muthappan’s dogs are guests of honour.

PUTTARI TIRUVAPPANA

The harvest festival

Puttari. Puttari means “new rice”: the harvest. The great festival, Puttari Tiruvappana, is held in the month of Vṛścikam (November–December), when the first rice of the harvest is offered to Muthappan.

The flag. The festival opens with the hoisting of a flag and goes on for several days with special theyyam performances.

Hospitality. The shrine gives free food and lodging to everyone who comes for the festival.

Year-round. Beyond the festival, the daily performances and Muthappan’s open meals make Parassinikkadavu a pilgrimage at any season.

ZAPPY: A harvest festival for a hunter god?

PROF. BOLT: Muthappan cares for the farmers and the poor. The first rice goes to the one who feeds everyone.

A LONG HISTORY

A folk god of North Malabar
Legend The foundling of Ayyankara; the hunter; Chandan and his wife
Tradition Muthappan comes to Parassinikkadavu and settles by the river
Generations A hereditary family keeps the Maḍappura; theyyam artists perform the god
20th c. The shrine’s openness to all draws devotees from across Kerala
Today Muthappan maḍappuras and veḷḷāṭṭam performances spread wherever North Keralites live, even abroad

A people’s god. Muthappan’s worship grew from the forests, the toddy-tappers’ palms and the hunters’ paths of North Malabar. It still belongs to them, and to everyone who comes.

THE TEN TEMPLES, LOOKING BACK

What the series has shown

Many ways to the divine. Ten temples, ten ways of meeting God: a child Kṛṣṇa in lamplight, a Lord seen through three doors, a forest vow, a host of festivals, a Mother calm and fierce, oracles in red, a temple whose streets changed history, a serpent grove kept by a mother, a river of boats, and a god who speaks back.

One grammar. Most share Kerala’s temple grammar: sloping roofs, lamp walls, tāntric rites, feasts. Mannārśāla and Parassinikkadavu show older, folk layers beneath it.

Open doors. The series has also shown Kerala opening its doors: Vaikom’s roads, Guruvāyūr’s gates, Kodungallur’s Bharaṇi, Muthappan’s meal for all.

ZAPPY: Which one should I see first?

PROF. BOLT: The one closest to your heart. Each part is complete on its own; together they are Kerala.
