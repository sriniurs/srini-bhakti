Sabarimala

The forest pilgrimage and the eighteen steps · explained and illustrated

Pilgrims at the eighteen steps

HOW THIS WORKS: First the story of Ayyappa, from his birth to the tigress. Then the pilgrim’s vow and the bundle on the head, the forest path from Erumeli to Pampa, and the climb up the eighteen steps to the shrine.

WHERE WE ARE

After the royal city of Part 2 we go into the hills. Sabarimala is a forest shrine in the Western Ghats, reached on foot, open only in set seasons, and visited each year by vast crowds of pilgrims dressed in black.

Its Lord is Ayyappa, also called Dharma Śāstā and Hariharaputra, “son of Hari and Hara”. Every pilgrim keeps a hard vow for forty-one days, carries a two-part bundle on the head, and greets everyone, even strangers, as “Swāmi”.

This guide tells Ayyappa’s story, explains the vow and the bundle, walks the old forest path, and climbs the eighteen steps.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why does everyone call each other Swāmi?

PROF. BOLT: Because on this pilgrimage every pilgrim is treated as Ayyappa himself. At the shrine door it says so in one word: Tattvamasi, “you are that”.

ZAPPY: A temple that is closed most of the year?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The shrine opens for the pilgrim season and a few days each month. The forest gets it back the rest of the time.

WHERE IT IS

A shrine in the forest hills

In the forest. The shrine stands on a hilltop in the Periyar tiger reserve, among the 18 hills of the Western Ghats in Pathanamthitta district. There is no town: just the forest, the river and the shrine.

The old path. The traditional route begins at Erumeli, crosses the Azhutha river and the steep Karimala, and reaches the river Pampa after several days on foot. This is said to be the path Ayyappa himself took.

Pampa. All pilgrims, whether they come through the forest or by the shorter roads, meet at Pampa. From there everyone climbs on foot to the shrine, the sannidhānam.

Śabarī’s hill. The hill is named after Śabarī, the forest woman of the Rāmāyaṇa who waited for Rāma with fruits she had tasted for sweetness. A spot on the path, Śabarīpīṭham, is where she is said to have prayed.

THE BIRTH OF AYYAPPA

Son of Hari and Hara

The story

The boon. The demoness Mahiṣī, sister of Mahiṣāsura whom the Goddess had slain, won a boon: no one could kill her except a son born of Śiva and Viṣṇu. She thought such a child impossible.
Mohinī. At the churning of the ocean Viṣṇu had taken the form of the enchantress Mohinī. From the meeting of Śiva and Mohinī a son was born: Hariharaputra, “son of Hari and Hara”.
The bell. The baby was left on the bank of the Pampa with a bell (maṇi) tied at his neck (kaṇṭha), so he was named Maṇikaṇṭha.
The king. Rājaśekhara, the childless king of Pandalam, was hunting in the forest, heard the baby cry, and took him home as his son.
A blessed home. The palace prospered. Soon the queen bore a son of her own, and Maṇikaṇṭha grew up a gifted, gentle boy.

ZAPPY: A son of two male gods?

PROF. BOLT: Mohinī is Viṣṇu in a woman’s form. The story is a way of saying Ayyappa joins the two great traditions, Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava, in one person.

Hariharaputra: the child who unites Śiva and Viṣṇu.

MAṆIKAṆṬHA AND THE TIGRESS

How the prince proved who he was

The story

The plot. A minister, fearing the gifted foster-prince would be made heir, turned the queen against him. She feigned a terrible headache.
The cure. The court physician, part of the plot, said only the milk of a tigress could cure her, sure that the boy would die trying to fetch it.
Into the forest. Maṇikaṇṭha, about twelve, set off alone. In the forest he met and destroyed Mahiṣī, fulfilling the purpose of his birth; Erumeli remembers this battle.
The tigress. He came back riding a tigress, with tigers following: in the story the gods themselves had taken tiger form. The terrified court fell at his feet.
The arrow. Revealing who he was, he forgave them, shot an arrow into the hills, and asked the king to build him a shrine where it fell. That spot is Sabarimala, where he sits in meditation.

ZAPPY: Why does he sit in meditation, not ride a tiger?

PROF. BOLT: At Sabarimala he is a naiṣṭhika brahmacārī, a lifelong celibate in yoga. The tiger story is his past; the yogī is how he stays.

The prince became the yogī of the hills.
Tellings differ in detail. Many add Mālikappuram: Mahiṣī, freed from her curse, became a maiden who wished to marry him; he promised to wed her in a year when no first-time pilgrim comes. She waits in her own shrine beside his.

AYYAPPA’S COMPANIONS

A Muslim friend, a warrior and a waiting maiden

🕌 Vāvar

A warrior, in many tellings a Muslim from overseas, who fought Ayyappa and then became his closest friend. His shrine stands beside the Ayyappa temple at Erumeli and near the eighteen steps. Pilgrims pray at both: a rare and loved sign of harmony.

⚔️ Kaḍutta

A loyal warrior-companion who, tradition says, guards the steps with Vāvar. Pilgrims honour him on the path and at the shrine.

🌸 Mālikappuram

The Goddess of the shrine just beside Ayyappa’s, linked in popular telling to the freed Mahiṣī who waits to marry him. Women pilgrims are themselves lovingly called “Mālikappuram”.

👑 Pandalam

The royal house that raised him. The palace still keeps Ayyappa’s ornaments and sends them up the hill each year for Makaravilakku.

ZAPPY: A mosque on a Hindu pilgrimage?

PROF. BOLT: At Erumeli, yes. Pilgrims dance from the Ayyappa temple to Vāvar’s mosque and back. Friendship is built into the route.

THE FORTY-ONE DAYS

The pilgrim’s vow

The beads. The vow begins when a pilgrim wears a māla, a string of tulasī or rudrākṣa beads with a small image of Ayyappa, put on by a senior pilgrim, the guru swāmi, or a priest.

Forty-one days. From then, for one maṇḍalam of 41 days, the pilgrim lives like a monk: celibacy, vegetarian food, no alcohol or tobacco, bathing twice a day, prayer morning and evening, and plain black, blue or saffron clothes. Many go barefoot and leave hair and beard uncut.

Everyone is Swāmi. A pilgrim under vow is addressed as “Swāmi” or “Ayyappa”, and addresses everyone else the same way. Caste and rank fall away; even strangers become fellow seekers.

First-timers. A pilgrim on the first journey is a kanni swāmi, given special care and duties on the path. An experienced guide leads the group.

The chant. On the path and at home the group chants swāmiyē śaraṇam ayyappa, “O Lord, Ayyappa, you are my refuge”.

ZAPPY: Why forty-one days?

PROF. BOLT: A maṇḍalam is the traditional period for a discipline to take root. By the end the pilgrim walks into the forest already changed.

THE BUNDLE ON THE HEAD

Irumuḍi: two knots, two halves

Two parts. Irumuḍi means “two knots”. The black cloth bundle has a front half for the Lord and a back half for the pilgrim.

The ghee coconut. The heart of the front half is a coconut emptied of its water and filled with ghee, the neyyttēṅṅa. The filling is done with prayer at home, in the ceremony called keṭṭunira, before the journey starts.

Offerings and food. With it go rice, turmeric, camphor and other offerings. The back half carries food for the road.

Only with the bundle. Only a pilgrim carrying the irumuḍi on the head may climb the eighteen steps. At the shrine the ghee is poured over the Lord in abhiṣeka, and the empty coconut is thrown into the great fire, the āḻi.

What it means. Many read the ghee as the soul and the coconut shell as the body: the pilgrim offers the self to the Lord and burns the shell.

ZAPPY: The coconut is me?

PROF. BOLT: That is one reading the gurus give. You carry yourself up the hill and pour yourself out at the end.

THE FOREST PATH

From Erumeli to Pampa

The story

Erumeli. The journey begins with the Peṭṭa Tuḷḷal: pilgrims smear their bodies with colour and ash, wear leaves, carry arrows and bundles of vegetables, and dance from the Ayyappa temple to Vāvar’s mosque. It recalls Ayyappa’s victory over Mahiṣī.
Azhutha. At the Azhutha river each pilgrim picks up a small stone and drops it on a hillside further on, Kallidumkunnu, the “hill where stones are laid”, said to mark where Mahiṣī’s body was buried.
Karimala. The climb over Karimala, the “black hill”, is the hardest stretch: steep, rough and long. Pilgrims pray to the guardian of the hill before they start.
Pampa. The path descends to the river Pampa, as holy here as the Gaṅgā. Pilgrims bathe, offer rites for their ancestors, and share a meal, the Pampa sadya. Lamps are floated on the river on one night of the season.
The last climb. From Pampa they climb past Neelimala and Appachimedu to Śabarīpīṭham, and at Śaramkutti the first-timers plant the arrows they carried from Erumeli.

ZAPPY: How long is the old path?

PROF. BOLT: Several days of walking from Erumeli, sleeping in the forest. Most pilgrims today drive to Pampa and climb only the last stretch.

The path is part of the worship: every hill and river has its story.

THE EIGHTEEN STEPS

Pathinettām paḍi

The climb. The shrine stands on a platform reached by eighteen steps, now sheathed in metal. Only a pilgrim with the irumuḍi on the head may climb them; at the foot each breaks a coconut.

Why eighteen. Traditions differ, and that is part of the beauty. Some say the steps stand for the eighteen hills around the shrine, each with its own deity. Others count the five senses, the eight passions, the three guṇas, and knowledge and ignorance, to make eighteen things a seeker climbs over.

Steps as a lifetime. Older devotees say a pilgrim who has climbed them for eighteen seasons may plant a coconut sapling on the hill, a sign of a long faithful life.

Coming down. Pilgrims descend the eighteen steps backwards, facing the Lord, unwilling to turn their back on him.

ZAPPY: Which explanation is right?

PROF. BOLT: All of them, and none is required. You climb with the bundle, the chant and the vow. The meaning grows with each year.

THE LORD ON THE HILL

Ayyappa in yoga, and the word at the door

The posture. Ayyappa sits with knees raised and a band, the paṭṭabandham, tied round them, the seat of yogīs in long meditation. His right hand shows the cin mudrā, thumb touching forefinger: the sign of the self united with the absolute.

The image. The present image is of five metals (pañcaloha). It replaced an older image after a fire damaged the shrine in 1950.

Tattvamasi. Above the shrine is written tat tvam asi, “you are that”, the great saying of the Chāndogya Upaniṣad. The pilgrim who has spent forty-one days being called “Swāmi” arrives to read that the Lord and the seeker are one.

The ghee bath. The ghee carried in the coconut is poured over the Lord, and a share comes back as prasādam. The sweet aravaṇa pāyasam and appam are the shrine’s best-known offerings.

ZAPPY: So I climb all this way to be told I am God?

PROF. BOLT: To be told what you always were. That is why every pilgrim is called Swāmi from the first day.

MAKARAVILAKKU

The great night of the year

The story

The season. The pilgrim season runs from the start of Vṛścikam (mid-November) through the Maṇḍala pūjā about 41 days later, and then to Makaravilakku at Makara Saṅkrānti in mid-January.
The ornaments. A few days before, the sacred ornaments, the Tiruvābharaṇam, leave the Pandalam palace in a procession that walks through the forest to the shrine. Tradition says a kite circles above it.
Adorned. On the evening of Makara Saṅkrānti Ayyappa is adorned with the ornaments for the dīpārādhana, the waving of lamps.
The flame. At that moment a flame, the Makaravilakku, is seen three times on Ponnambalamedu, a hill facing the shrine, and a bright star, the Makara jyoti, is watched for in the sky. The temple board has said in court that the flame is lit by people as a ritual; devotees keep the two lights distinct and honour both.
Closing. A few days later the season ends; the Pandalam royal representative has the last darśan and the shrine closes.

ZAPPY: Is the flame a miracle or a ritual?

PROF. BOLT: The temple’s own administration calls it a lamp lit in worship, like the waving of lamps inside. Devotees bow to it either way.

Makaravilakku: the Lord in his royal ornaments, and a light on the facing hill.

WHO MAY GO

A long tradition and a long debate

The custom. Because Ayyappa here is a lifelong celibate, the shrine’s custom has been that women between about 10 and 50 do not make the pilgrimage. Girls and older women go, and are fondly called Mālikappuram.

The case. In 2018 a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court held, by four to one, that the restriction was unconstitutional. Review petitions followed, and the wider questions of religious freedom were referred to a nine-judge bench, which heard them in 2026.

Two deep convictions. One side holds that a deity’s own nature and a temple’s custom must be respected; the other that public worship must be open equally to all. Both are held sincerely by devotees and citizens alike.

Our approach. This guide records the custom and the case, and takes no side.

ZAPPY: Which side is right?

PROF. BOLT: That is for the courts, the community and each devotee’s conscience. Our job here is to explain both fairly.

A LONG HISTORY

From the Pandalam kings to today
Legend Maṇikaṇṭha shoots his arrow; the Pandalam king builds the first shrine
Medieval Śāstā worship spreads in Kerala; Sabarimala becomes the chief forest shrine of Śāstā
19th c. The pilgrimage grows under the Travancore kingdom
1950 Fire damages the shrine; a new five-metal image is installed
1985 The eighteen steps are covered in five metals
Today Managed by the Travancore Devaswom Board; the Tantri is from the Tāḻamaṇ family
2018–26 The entry case before the Supreme Court

From local to national. Once a hard walk taken by small groups from nearby villages, the pilgrimage now draws devotees from across South India and beyond. The forest, the vow and the chant remain unchanged.
