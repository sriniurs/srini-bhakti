Vaḍakkunnāthan

The Śiva temple at the heart of Thrissur · explained and illustrated

The temple on the hill, and the Pūram on its ground

HOW THIS WORKS: First the legends: Paraśurāma’s first Śiva temple and the liṅga of ghee. Then a walk through the enclosure, its shrines, theatre and murals, and finally the geography of Pūram, Kerala’s greatest temple festival.

WHERE WE ARE

From the forest of Sabarimala in Part 3 we come back to the plains, to Thrissur, Kerala’s “cultural capital”. Its name is often explained as Tṛ-śiva-pērūr, “the town of the holy Śiva”, and the Śiva in question is Vaḍakkunnāthan, “the Lord of the North”.

His temple stands on a low hill in the middle of the city, inside a high wall with four great gateways. Around it lies the open ground called Tekkinkāḍu, and around that the city’s ring road. The whole city is drawn in circles around this one shrine.

Once a year that ground fills with elephants, drums and umbrellas for Thrissur Pūram. This guide explains the temple first, and then how the festival uses its space.

With the blessings of the Lord,

ZAPPY: Why is Śiva called the Lord of the North?

PROF. BOLT: The name refers to the shrine’s position on the north side of the old temple complex: the northern Lord, as opposed to the shrines to the south. Over time it became his name.

ZAPPY: The Lord is buried in ghee?

PROF. BOLT: Covered, not buried. Centuries of ghee offerings have built a mound over the liṅga, and it is said never to melt or smell.

WHERE IT IS

A temple at the centre of three circles

The hill. The temple stands on a raised mound in the very centre of Thrissur, enclosed by a massive stone wall with a gateway (gopuram) on each of the four sides.

The ground. Around the wall lies Tekkinkāḍu, “the teak forest”: once a real forest, cleared under the Cochin king Śaktan Tampurān (ruled 1790–1805), now a great open ground of red earth.

The round. Around the ground runs a circular road, the Swaraj Round, from which the city’s main roads leave like spokes. Thrissur is literally a city built around a temple.

The neighbours. Two goddess temples stand close by: Pāṟamēkkāvu, east of the ground, and Tiruvambāḍi, a Kṛṣṇa temple to the north-west whose Bhagavatī also leads the Pūram. Their processions meet on this ground.

PARAŚURĀMA’S FIRST TEMPLE

How Kerala and its first Śiva shrine came to be

The story

The warrior. Paraśurāma, Viṣṇu’s sixth avatāra, had wiped out the corrupt warrior-kings of his time again and again. Weighed down by the killing, he gave away all the land he had won.
The axe. Left with no land of his own, he stood on the western shore and hurled his axe (paraśu) into the sea. The ocean drew back as far as the axe flew, and the land of Kerala rose from the water.
A land of temples. To make the new land holy he founded temples along it: tradition counts 108 Śiva temples, and Vaḍakkunnāthan is named first among them.
Śiva comes. In one telling Paraśurāma prayed to Śiva on Kailāsa to come and live in the new land, and Śiva came with Pārvatī and his family to rest on this hill.
The first. So Vaḍakkunnāthan is honoured as Kerala’s first and senior Śiva shrine, and the city grew up around it.

ZAPPY: Did Kerala really rise from the sea?

PROF. BOLT: Geologists will tell you the coast has shifted over long ages. The legend says the same thing in a story: this land was won from the water, and made holy.

Kerala’s own origin story begins with an axe, and its first temple is here.

THE LIṄGA UNDER GHEE

What lies in the sanctum

A mountain of ghee. The liṅga of Vaḍakkunnāthan cannot be seen. It lies under a great mound of ghee, built up from offerings over centuries and said to stand several feet high. Devotees see the gleaming mound, decorated with gold crescents, serpent hoods and flowers.

It does not melt. The temple’s tradition says the ghee never melts in Kerala’s heat and never goes rancid, and is believed to have healing power; a little is given to devotees as prasādam.

Facing west. The sanctum faces west. Behind Śiva, in the same structure and facing east, sits Pārvatī, so the divine couple sit back to back, each watching over one half of the world.

Snow of Kailāsa. Many explain the ghee as the snow of Kailāsa: here in tropical Kerala, Śiva is still on his white mountain.

ZAPPY: Why ghee?

PROF. BOLT: Ghee is the purest offering in Vedic worship, poured into the sacred fire. Here it is poured onto the Lord himself, and stays.

THE THREE SANCTUMS

Śiva, Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa and Rāma

Three in a row. Inside the inner enclosure stand three main sanctums side by side: Vaḍakkunnāthan to the north, Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa in the middle, and Śrī Rāma to the south.

Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa. The middle shrine holds Śiva and Viṣṇu as a single figure, half Śaṅkara, half Nārāyaṇa. Placed between Śiva and Rāma, it says aloud what Kerala keeps whispering: the two are one.

Śrī Rāma. The Rāma shrine is said to have been installed by Paraśurāma too: one avatāra of Viṣṇu honouring the next.

Others. Gaṇapati, Śāstā (Ayyappa), Kṛṣṇa, Siṃhodara and Paraśurāma himself have their own small shrines. Devotees walk a set order of worship round them.

Gaṇapati’s appam. A favourite offering here is appam, sweet rice cakes fried in ghee, made for Gaṇapati.

ZAPPY: Is it a Śiva temple or a Viṣṇu temple?

PROF. BOLT: Both, by design. The name on the gate is Śiva’s, but Viṣṇu is never far away.

WOOD, STONE AND PAINT

The enclosure, the theatre and the murals

A wooden temple. Vaḍakkunnāthan is a classic of Kerala architecture: laterite and stone below, carved wood and sloping copper-sheeted roofs above, inside a nine-acre walled ground.

The theatre. The kūttambalam is a temple theatre where the ancient Sanskrit drama kūṭiyāṭṭam and the solo storytelling cākyār kūttu and naṅṅyār kūttu are performed as offerings. Its carved wooden pillars and roof are among the finest in Kerala.

The murals. The walls carry Kerala mural paintings in deep red, yellow and green, with scenes from the Mahābhārata and images such as Vāsukiśayana (Viṣṇu on the serpent) and Nṛttanātha (Śiva as dancer).

Conservation. A long restoration using traditional materials (lime, jaggery, wood) won the temple the UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Excellence for cultural heritage conservation in 2015.

ZAPPY: A theatre inside a temple?

PROF. BOLT: In Kerala the drama itself is worship. The actor performs for the god first and the audience second.

ŚAṄKARA AND VAḌAKKUNNĀTHAN

A birth, and an end, linked to this hill

The story

A childless couple. Śivaguru and Āryāmbā of Kāladi in central Kerala had no child. Tradition says they came to Vaḍakkunnāthan and prayed for a son.
The choice. In a dream Śiva offered them a choice: many ordinary sons who would live long, or one son of great wisdom who would live a short life. They chose the one.
Śaṅkara. The son was Śaṅkara, the great teacher of Advaita Vedānta, born about the 8th century. Many hold that he was Śiva’s own grace, and his name means “one who makes peace”.
The end. One Kerala tradition holds that Śaṅkara’s life ended here at Vaḍakkunnāthan; others place it at Kedārnāth or Kāñcī. Traditions differ.
Remembered. The temple remembers him, and a small memorial within the grounds is pointed out by priests.

ZAPPY: Was Śaṅkara really born from a prayer here?

PROF. BOLT: That is Kerala’s tradition, and the Śaṅkara-vijaya biographies tell versions of it. Historians cannot check a dream, but the bond between Śaṅkara and this temple is old and deep.

Kerala’s greatest philosopher is remembered as a gift of Vaḍakkunnāthan.

THRISSUR PŪRAM

The festival of festivals

The story

How it began. The great festival of the area was once at Ārāṭṭupuḻa. In 1798, the story goes, the Thrissur temples arrived late after heavy rain and were refused entry. The Cochin king Śaktan Tampurān answered by creating a festival of their own at Vaḍakkunnāthan.
Ten temples. Ten temples of the city send their deities, on caparisoned elephants, to pay respects to Vaḍakkunnāthan. They are grouped in two sides, led by Pāṟamēkkāvu and Tiruvambāḍi. Vaḍakkunnāthan is the host and does not process.
The gate opens. The day before, an elephant carrying the goddess of Neythalakkāvu pushes open the temple’s south gate, which stays closed the rest of the year: the Pūra viḷambaram, the announcement of Pūram.
Drums. On the day comes the Maṭhattil varavu, a great pañcavādyam of Tiruvambāḍi, and inside the temple under an ilañji tree, the thunderous Ilaññittara mēḷam of Pāṟamēkkāvu.
Umbrellas. In the evening the two sides line up their elephants face to face on the southern ground and raise sets of brilliant umbrellas in turn, the Kuḍamāṟṟam, “exchange of umbrellas”. Fireworks follow through the night.

ZAPPY: Why doesn’t Vaḍakkunnāthan himself go out?

PROF. BOLT: Because Pūram comes to him. The goddesses and gods of the city visit the Lord of Thrissur; he receives them.

Pūram is ten temples visiting one: the city paying respects to its Lord.

THE GEOGRAPHY OF PŪRAM

How the festival uses the temple and its ground

Into the temple. Each visiting deity enters the temple ground, pays respects to Vaḍakkunnāthan, and leaves. The south gate, closed all year, opens for Pūram alone.

Two sides. The Tiruvambāḍi group approaches from the west and north-west, the Pāṟamēkkāvu group from the east. Their great percussion concerts are held in set places: Tiruvambāḍi’s in front of the Naḍuvil maṭham, Pāṟamēkkāvu’s inside the temple walls.

The south ground. At dusk both sides come out of the south gate onto the Tekkinkāḍu ground and face each other, about fifteen elephants each, for the Kuḍamāṟṟam.

Farewell. The next day the two goddesses take formal leave of each other at the ground, promising to meet again next year.

ZAPPY: So the whole city is the stage?

PROF. BOLT: The temple is the centre, the ground the stage, the round the gallery. Śaktan Tampurān designed a festival to fit his city.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Other great days

Ānayūṭṭu. In the monsoon month of Karkkiṭakam (July–August) dozens of elephants gather at the temple to be fed rice balls, jaggery and fruit, often after a great Gaṇapati homa. Many believe it brings blessings on the city.

Mahāśivarātri. Śiva’s great night (February–March), when thousands of lamps are lit on the temple walls.

Tiruvātira. Śiva’s birth star in Dhanu (December–January), especially kept by women with songs and the tiruvātirakkaḷi dance.

Navarātri. Nine nights of the Goddess, with recitals in the theatre.

ZAPPY: Why feed elephants in the rains?

PROF. BOLT: Karkkiṭakam is the month of healing and care in Kerala. The elephants rest from festivals and are given a feast of their own.

A LONG HISTORY

From the teak forest to a heritage award
Legend Paraśurāma founds the temple as the first of 108 Śiva shrines
8th c.? Tradition links the birth of Śaṅkara to prayers here
Medieval The temple is run by Nampūtiri assemblies; the Yōgātirippāḍ is elected from Thrissur
14th–15th c. Thrissur is contested between the Zamorins of Kozhikode and Cochin
1790 Śaktan Tampurān becomes king of Cochin and makes Thrissur his capital
1798 The first Thrissur Pūram
2015 UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Excellence for conservation

The king who shaped a city. Śaktan Tampurān (Rāma Varma, ruled 1790–1805) cleared the teak forest around the temple, laid out the round, and created Pūram. Thrissur as we know it is his plan built around Vaḍakkunnāthan.
