PART 7 OF 10 · KHARA’S LIṄGA · THE LORD OF THE FEAST · THE SATYAGRAHA

Vaikom

The temple streets and the Vaikom Satyagraha · explained and illustrated

The roads round the temple, 1924

HOW THIS WORKS: First the shrine: a demon’s three liṅgas, a tiger-footed sage and the Lord who feeds everyone. Then the streets around it, and the 604 days of the Vaikom Satyagraha that opened them.

WHERE WE ARE

From the fierce Goddess of Kodungallur in Part 6 we go to Vaikom, on the shore of the Vembanad lake in Kottayam district. Its temple of Śiva, Vaikkattappan, the Lord of Vaikom, is one of the oldest and greatest Śiva shrines of Kerala.

Vaikom is famous for two things that seem very different. The Lord here is the Annadāna prabhu, the Lord of the free meal, who wants everyone fed. And the roads around his temple were once closed to most of the people of Kerala because of their caste.

In 1924 those roads became the stage of the Vaikom Satyagraha, India’s first great non-violent campaign against untouchability. This guide tells both stories, the temple’s and the streets’, honestly and side by side.

ZAPPY: Why is it called the tiger’s abode?

PROF. BOLT: After the sage Vyāghrapāda, whose name means “tiger-foot”. He was given tiger’s feet, the story goes, to climb trees for flowers for Śiva.

WHERE IT IS

A temple town by the lake, and its four roads

By the lake. Vaikom lies on the eastern shore of the great Vembanad lake, in Kottayam district. Boats and paddy fields surround it, and the temple stands at the centre of the old town.

The enclosure. The temple stands inside a high wall in a large compound, with gateways on each side and a great flagstaff before the eastern entrance.

The roads. Public roads run round the temple wall on all four sides. Until 1925 boards near them warned people of “lower” castes not to use them: the roads that the Vaikom Satyagraha set out to open.

Three temples. Vaikom is linked with two other Śiva temples nearby, Eṭṭumānūr and Kaḍuthuruthy. Devotees try to visit all three before the noon pūjā on a single day.

THE DEMON’S THREE LIṄGAS

How Śiva came to Vaikom

The story

Penance. The demon Khara, a great devotee of Śiva, did severe penance. Pleased, Śiva granted his wishes and gave him three liṅgas drawn from his own being.
The journey. Khara carried them south from the Himālaya, one in his right hand, one in his left, one held at his neck.
Stuck. Tired, he set them down to rest. When he tried to lift them again, they would not move: Śiva had chosen to stay. The liṅga from his right hand stayed at Vaikom, the one from his left at Eṭṭumānūr, the one from his neck at Kaḍuthuruthy.
The sage. Khara, freed by his devotion, gave the Vaikom liṅga into the care of the sage Vyāghrapāda, who had followed him unseen, and asked him to worship it.
The vision. On the eighth day of the dark half of Vṛścikam, Śiva and Pārvatī appeared to Vyāghrapāda here. That day is still the temple’s great festival, Vaikattaṣṭami.

ZAPPY: A demon who was a devotee?

PROF. BOLT: Purāṇic “demons” are often great devotees. Khara’s penance brings Śiva to Kerala; his name means “harsh”, but his gift was gentle.

Vaikom, Eṭṭumānūr and Kaḍuthuruthy: three liṅgas, one gift.

THE LORD OF THE FEAST

Vaikkattappan, who wants everyone fed

The liṅga. In the sanctum Śiva is worshipped as a liṅga, Vaikkattappan, “the Lord of Vaikom”. Tradition says Paraśurāma himself set the temple’s rules of worship.

Three forms in a day. Like the Goddess at Chottanikkara, the Lord is worshipped in different forms through the day: as Dakṣiṇāmūrti, the teacher, in the morning; as Kirāta, the hunter who tested Arjuna, at noon; and as Śiva with Pārvatī in the evening.

Annadāna prabhu. Vaikkattappan is called the Lord of the free meal. Feeding devotees, annadānam, is the temple’s greatest offering, and the morning meal, prātal, is famous. A devotee who sponsors it is said to please the Lord more than with any other gift.

The cook’s story. A favourite legend says the Lord once came in disguise as a hungry Brahmin to the temple kitchen. Devotees believe he still eats with those who eat here.

ZAPPY: Why is food the best offering?

PROF. BOLT: Because Śiva here is a host, not a guest. He wants his visitors fed before anything else. A temple of feeding: remember that when we reach the streets.

VAIKATTAṢṬAMI

The festival of the vision

When. Vaikattaṣṭami falls on the Aṣṭamī of the dark half of Vṛścikam (November–December), the day Śiva and Pārvatī appeared to Vyāghrapāda. The festival lasts about twelve days.

Father and son. On the night of Aṣṭamī, Subrahmaṇya, Śiva’s son, comes in procession from the nearby Udayanāpuram temple, returning victorious from his battle with the demon Tāraka. Father and son meet at Vaikom: the kūṭippūja, a moving night of drums and elephants.

The feast. The festival is also a great feast. Thousands are fed, true to the Lord of the free meal.

The farewell. Before dawn the son takes leave of the father, a sad and tender moment for devotees.

ZAPPY: A god visiting his father after a war?

PROF. BOLT: That is Kerala: the gods are a family. Vaikom keeps the reunion every year.

THE STREETS BEFORE 1924

What the roads meant

Untouchability. In the Travancore of the early 1900s, people of many castes counted as “avarṇa”, Ezhavas, Pulayas, Parayas and others, were not only barred from temples but forbidden even to walk on the public roads near them, so as not to “pollute” the upper castes.

At Vaikom. Boards near the Vaikom temple warned such people away from the roads around the shrine. A person might have to take a long detour to reach the market, the school or the court on the other side.

A contradiction. The same temple fed everyone in its feast hall. Reformers asked: how can the Lord of the free meal want his own people kept off the road?

The voice. In 1917 T. K. Madhavan, an Ezhava leader and follower of Śrī Nārāyaṇa Guru, raised the demand for temple entry in his paper Deśābhimāni. In 1921 he met Gandhi and won his support.

ZAPPY: Was this only at Vaikom?

PROF. BOLT: No, across Kerala. Vaikom was chosen because it was a great temple with roads used by everyone, and the injustice was plain to see.

THE VAIKOM SATYAGRAHA

604 days of non-violent protest

The story

Three men. On 30 March 1924 three volunteers, a Pulaya, an Ezhava and a Nair, walked together towards the forbidden road. They were stopped and arrested. Every day after, new volunteers took their place.
The leaders. T. K. Madhavan, K. P. Kesava Menon, K. Kelappan and George Joseph led the campaign; most were jailed. E. V. Ramasamy “Periyar” came from Tamil Nadu and was imprisoned with hard labour. Sikh Akālīs came from Punjab and ran a free kitchen for the volunteers.
Patience. Volunteers stood at the barriers day after day, through the monsoon, sometimes in floodwater up to their chests, without striking back when beaten.
The upper castes march. In November 1924 Mannathu Padmanabhan led a savarṇa jāthā, a march of upper-caste supporters, from Vaikom to Thiruvananthapuram, carrying a petition with thousands of signatures to the Regent Maharani.
Gandhi. Gandhi came to Vaikom in March 1925, met temple trustees, and negotiated with the government. Śrī Nārāyaṇa Guru gave the campaign his support.

ZAPPY: Did the satyagrahis win?

PROF. BOLT: Partly, in 1925. Three of the four roads were opened. The eastern road, and temple entry itself, had to wait till 1936.

Vaikom brought non-violent protest against caste to Kerala, and inspired the Guruvāyūr Satyagraha of 1931.

HOW IT ENDED, AND WHAT FOLLOWED

From open roads to open temples

The settlement. In November 1925, after talks between Gandhi and the Travancore police commissioner W. H. Pitt, the campaign was withdrawn. New roads were opened round the temple on the north, south and west for all; the eastern approach stayed closed.

Unfinished. Many felt the result fell short: the temple itself remained closed to most Hindus. But the principle that public roads belong to all had been won.

1936. On 12 November 1936 the Maharaja of Travancore issued the Temple Entry Proclamation, opening all government temples in the state to all Hindus regardless of caste. Vaikom’s doors opened at last.

Remembered. A memorial stands at Vaikom, with a statue of Periyar, and the centenary of the satyagraha was marked in 2024 by both Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

ZAPPY: So the protest led to the temple doors opening?

PROF. BOLT: Step by step. Vaikom opened the roads; Guruvāyūr pressed on in 1931; Travancore opened its temples in 1936; Guruvāyūr in 1947. Part 1 and Part 7 are one story.

THE TEMPLE AND THE STREET

Why this belongs in a bhakti guide

One Lord, one road. The satyagrahis did not attack the temple; they asked to walk to it. Many were devotees. Their demand was, at its heart, a bhakti demand: let every devotee reach the Lord.

The Guru’s word. Śrī Nārāyaṇa Guru, who blessed the campaign, had already given Kerala the motto “one caste, one religion, one God for humankind”.

The feast hall. The Lord of Vaikom was always the Lord of the free meal. The satyagraha asked the town to live up to its own god.

Today. Today anyone of any caste walks those roads and enters the temple. That ordinary fact is the satyagraha’s monument.

ZAPPY: Is this political?

PROF. BOLT: It is history, recorded fairly. Devotees and reformers agree today that the roads should be open. Vaikom is where that agreement began.
