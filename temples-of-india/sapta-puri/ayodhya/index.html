SAPTAPURĪ · THE SEVEN CITIES OF LIBERATION · 1 OF 7

AYODHYĀ

RĀMA’S CITY ON THE SARAYŪ
AN ILLUSTRATED REFERENCE GUIDE · SACRED STORIES · HISTORY · PLACES · PRACTICE

FOREWORD

In the Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā is a city that had everything a kingdom could desire: broad streets, fine houses, strong walls and a king whose subjects loved him. Yet its most famous story begins when its beloved prince agrees to leave it.

For fourteen years, Rāma lives away from the throne that should have been his. When he returns, Ayodhyā is no longer only a royal capital. It has become an idea: a place where duty is stronger than power, a promise matters even when it hurts, and a good ruler is judged by how he treats his people.

On the Sarayū today, pilgrims come to a living city of temples, old neighbourhoods and changing streets. The new Rāma Janmabhūmi temple is its most visible landmark, but it is only one part of a much older sacred landscape: Hanumān Garhī, Kanak Bhawan, the ghats, the parikramā paths and countless retellings of Rāma's life.

This reference guide follows Ayodhyā through its names, its texts, the stories of the Ikṣvāku dynasty, the city's documented history, its shrines and festivals, and a pilgrim's route along the Sarayū.

WHERE IT IS

A city along the Sarayū

Ayodhyā lies in the Gangetic plain of Uttar Pradesh on the Sarayū, often identified here with the Ghāghra river system. The river is not merely scenery: bathing, dawn prayer, ancestor rites and the ghāṭs form one half of the pilgrim’s day; darśan in the temple lanes forms the other.

The city’s sacred geography is not a neat circle around one building. Rāmkoṭ is the dense old core associated with Rāma’s citadel; Hanumān Garhī rises within that sacred neighbourhood; Kanak Bhawan and other shrines lie in the same walkable devotional landscape. The Sarayū flows along the northern side.

Remember the distinction: The Rāmāyaṇa’s Ayodhyā is a vast literary and sacred capital. Modern Ayodhyā is a living city with archaeological layers and a changed urban fabric. Treat the epic description as the tradition’s portrait, not as a street map verified by excavation.

THE NAME

A-yodhyā: the unconquerable

The Sanskrit Ayodhyā is commonly explained as “not to be fought against” or “unassailable.” The name expresses an ideal of a protected, well-governed city rather than a claim that no military conflict ever occurred here.

In the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa describes the city built by Manu and ruled by the Ikṣvāku dynasty: great roads, gardens, gates, wealth and skilled people. The literary scene creates a model of royal order. The wider region is called Kosala; the word Sāketa is also associated with this ancient urban landscape in Buddhist and other historical sources.

Name What it conveys
Ayodhyā The unconquerable city
Sāketa An ancient name used in several textual traditions
Kosala The wider historical region and kingdom
Rāmkoṭ The sacred city-centre linked with Rāma’s fortress/court
Rāma Janmabhūmi The place revered as the birth-site of Rāma

The city is simultaneously a geographical settlement and a religious idea: what would a kingdom look like if justice, duty and compassion ruled it?

THE SAPTAPURĪ VERSE

Why Ayodhyā comes first

ayodhyā mathurā māyā kāśī kāñcī avantikā | purī dvāravatī caiva saptaitā mokṣadāyikāḥ ||

“Ayodhyā, Mathurā, Māyā, Kāśī, Kāñcī, Avantikā, and the city of Dvārāvatī—these seven are held to bestow liberation.”

Ayodhyā heads the conventional list. Mokṣa, in this context, means release from the cycle of rebirth; the verse voices a religious conviction about the sacred merit of these places, not an outcome that historical research can measure. Some recensions and printed attributions differ, so it is safer to call this the traditional Saptapurī verse than give one rigid chapter number.

ZAPPY: Why is a king’s birthplace a city of liberation?

PROF. BOLT: Because Rāma is worshipped as Viṣṇu in human form. For a devotee, remembering him is not just remembering a good ruler; it is a path toward the divine.

THE RĀMĀYAṆA · I

Birth, four brothers and the bow

King Daśaratha of Ayodhyā has three queens—Kausalyā, Kaikeyī and Sumitrā—and longs for heirs. The epic tells of a sacrifice and the birth of four sons: Rāma, Bharata, Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna. Rāma, born to Kausalyā, is revered as an avatāra of Viṣṇu.

As a youth, Rāma accompanies the sage Viśvāmitra, protects his sacrifice, and travels to Mithilā. There, he lifts and breaks Śiva’s mighty bow in King Janaka’s court and marries Sītā. Their marriage is celebrated in the ritual calendar as Vivāha Pañcamī.

The plot matters for Ayodhyā because Rāma is not first introduced as a miracle-working god. He is a son, brother, student and prince, asked to choose between personal happiness and obligation. That tension gives the city its moral weight.

Person Place in the story
Daśaratha King of Kosala, father of the four princes
Kausalyā Rāma’s mother
Kaikeyī Bharata’s mother; asks for the two boons
Sumitrā Mother of Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna
Sītā Daughter of Janaka; Rāma’s wife
Hanumān Devotee whose service becomes a model of bhakti

THE RĀMĀYAṆA · II

Exile, return and Rāma-rājya

On the eve of Rāma’s coronation, Kaikeyī claims two old boons: Bharata should reign and Rāma should go into the forest for fourteen years. Rāma accepts; Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa accompany him. Daśaratha dies in grief. Bharata refuses to enjoy a kingdom gained in this way; he places Rāma’s sandals on the throne and governs as his representative.

The exile leads to Sītā’s abduction by Rāvaṇa, the alliance with Sugrīva and Hanumān, and the war in Laṅkā. The ending brings the family back to Ayodhyā. The celebration of their homecoming is a major devotional explanation for Dīpāvalī, though the festival has several regional and theological meanings.

Rāma-rājya becomes shorthand for an ideal state whose ruler is accountable to dharma. The later portions of the epic also raise difficult questions about kingship, Sītā’s trials and private suffering. Different traditions and modern readers respond to them differently; the Rāmāyaṇa is not a single uncomplicated moral slogan.

ZAPPY: Why did Bharata keep the sandals on the throne?

PROF. BOLT: They signified stewardship. He could govern, but he would not pretend the kingdom had ceased to belong to Rāma.

THE TEMPLE TODAY

Śrī Rāma Janmabhūmi Mandir

The large new Rāma Mandir stands at the site revered by Hindus as Rāma’s birthplace. Its architecture uses a North Indian Nāgara vocabulary: a rising śikhara, carved stone columns and progressively sacred spaces leading toward the garbhagṛha. The principal worshipped form is Rāma Lallā, the child Rāma.

22 January 2024 was the date of the consecration (prāṇa pratiṣṭhā) of the deity in the new temple. That is an exact historical event; it should not be confused with a claim that all planned works were complete on the same day. Temple access, queues and visitor rules have continued to evolve.

For a first-time pilgrim, understand three ideas: the image (a child, not the crowned king), the place (a remembered birth-site) and the ritual (daily worship, which makes the site a living shrine rather than only a monument).

ZAPPY: Why worship Rāma as a little child?

PROF. BOLT: Because devotion can be protective and tender. Here, the king of Ayodhyā is first welcomed as someone to love and care for.

HISTORY · I

Ancient city, texts and archaeology

Ayodhyā appears in Sanskrit epic and Purāṇic literature and is associated with the ancient kingdom of Kosala. Buddhist and Jain traditions also remember the Sāketa/Ayodhyā region; the city belongs to more than one religious memory. Material evidence attests settlement and changing occupation in the area, but archaeology cannot independently date Rāma’s lifetime or verify each narrated episode.

This distinction is essential: a literary tradition can preserve a place’s significance without functioning as a modern archaeological report. The location of shrines, sects and worship practices shifted over centuries as cities were built, damaged and rebuilt.

The medieval and early-modern history of the disputed Janmabhūmi site is complex. The Bābrī Masjid stood there for centuries before its demolition in 1992. Claims about earlier structures, dates and causes have been debated by historians, archaeologists and the parties in litigation. A responsible reference guide identifies claims and the evidence base rather than equating a religious belief with a proven excavation result.

HISTORY · II

Dispute, court judgment and a new temple

Date What happened
16th century A mosque known as Bābrī Masjid was built at the disputed site; its exact early history has been debated
1949 Idols were placed inside the mosque, sharpening a decades-long dispute and leading to restrictions on access
6 December 1992 The Bābrī Masjid was demolished by a mob; the demolition was unlawful and produced widespread communal violence
2019 India’s Supreme Court awarded the disputed land for a Hindu temple through a trust and directed that five acres at an alternative site be allotted for a mosque
5 August 2020 Temple foundation ceremony (bhūmi pūjan)
22 January 2024 Consecration of Rāma Lallā in the new mandir

The 2019 Supreme Court judgment is a legal resolution of a property and religious-site dispute, not a judicial proof of the epic events. The Court also addressed the illegality of the 1949 desecration and the 1992 demolition. The history is part of the city’s modern story and deserves precise, non-triumphal language.

The transformation of access roads, pilgrim facilities and public space has been rapid. For visitors, check current official arrangements rather than relying on an old blog’s entry timings.

HANUMĀN GARHĪ

The guardian before the king

Hanumān Garhī is a hilltop fortress-like temple in the old town. Pilgrims traditionally visit Hanumān before Rāma, as though asking permission from the devoted guardian of Ayodhyā. The ascent up its steps is part of the experience; the shrine is small and the devotional energy intense.

In the Rāmāyaṇa, Hanumān has strength enough to cross the ocean but uses it in service to someone else. That is why the city’s protector is a servant rather than a second king. Many versions tell that Hanumān stayed in Ayodhyā to guard Rāma’s capital after his return.

ZAPPY: Why does the guardian get visited first?

PROF. BOLT: It is a lesson in devotion: before approaching Rāma’s throne, meet the one who never asked for a throne himself.

KANAK BHAWAN AND THE ROYAL SHRINES

Rāma and Sītā at home

Kanak Bhawan, “the golden house,” is among Ayodhyā’s best-loved shrines. Local tradition calls it a gift from Kaikeyī to Sītā and Rāma after their wedding. Its present form owes much to later royal patronage; this is a temple of richly ornamented Rāma–Sītā images and household devotion, rather than a historical survival of an epic palace.

Daśaratha Mahal evokes Rāma’s father’s court and the royal domestic setting of the epic. Nāgeśvarnāth, dedicated to Śiva, is associated by local legend with Rāma’s son Kuśa. Treta Ke Thakur recalls the sacrificial and kingly Rāma of later tradition. Together they make the city both Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva.

Site Why pilgrims go
Kanak Bhawan Sītā–Rāma together; the royal household
Daśaratha Mahal The king and family setting
Nāgeśvarnāth Śiva worship within Rāma’s city
Hanumān Garhī Hanumān as guardian and ideal devotee
Rām Janmabhūmi Rāma Lallā and the birth-site tradition

THE SARAYŪ AND THE GHĀṬS

A river that receives the city’s prayers

The Sarayū is praised in Rāma-related literature and loved as a sacred river. Rām Ki Paidi is a developed series of broad riverfront steps and bathing spaces. Nayā Ghāṭ is a busy point of arrival; Guptār Ghāṭ, farther west, is traditionally associated with Rāma’s final departure from the world.

A morning river visit is different from the crowded temple experience: flowing water, ancestral remembrance, bathing, lamps, recitation and the informal religious life of the ghāṭs. River levels vary seasonally, and current safety instructions always take priority. Do not enter strong currents; use designated bathing places.

Guptār is a good example of how a sacred city holds more than its birth story. In the tradition, Rāma’s life does not end with coronation. The Lord’s return from the earth is also remembered by a place.

THE PARIKRAMĀS

Walking around sacred Ayodhyā

Pilgrimage is measured not only in temples visited but in ground walked. Ayodhyā is known for Pañcakośī, Caturdaśakośī and larger Caurāsīkośī circuits in the wider sacred geography; exact modern routes, distances and permitted days vary. A kos is a traditional unit and is not a fixed modern kilometre in every account.

Circuit Traditional idea Practical note
Pañcakośī Circumambulation of the inner sacred city Often treated as the shorter city circuit
Caturdaśakośī Outer pilgrimage boundary Large festival crowds during the annual yātrā
Caurāsīkośī Wider Ayodhyā/Kosala sacred region Multi-day route; requires advance local planning

Walking the boundary makes the town itself a shrine. The route links scattered shrines that a hurried visitor, driving directly to the Janmabhūmi temple, may never see.

ZAPPY: Isn’t parikramā just walking in a big circle?

PROF. BOLT: Physically, yes. Devotion turns that circle into a way of saying: the entire place matters.

FESTIVALS AND THE RITUAL YEAR

A city that celebrates a return

Festival When What it means here
Rāma Navamī Caitra śukla navamī (Mar–Apr) Celebration of Rāma’s birth; heavy pilgrim turnout
Dīpotsav / Dīpāvalī Kārtika (Oct–Nov) Lamps for the return of Rāma; citywide illuminations

Śrāvaṇa observances Monsoon months River worship and varied local rituals

On an ordinary day, darśan, recitation of the Rāmāyaṇa or Rāmacaritamānas, bhajan and river worship quietly continue. Festivals change the scale, not the underlying grammar of the place. Dates are lunar and should be checked each year.

AYODHYĀ IN LITERATURE AND CULTURE

From Vālmīki to Tulasīdāsa

Vālmīki’s Sanskrit Rāmāyaṇa provides the foundational narrative of the prince of Ayodhyā. Tulasīdāsa’s Rāmacaritamānas, composed in Awadhi in the sixteenth century, brings the story into the language and imagination of North India. Its opening devotion, the role of Hanumān, and its singing traditions remain visible in Ayodhyā’s lanes.

Kamba Rāmāyaṇam in Tamil, Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇa in Bengali, Jain tellings and Southeast Asian performance traditions show that this is not a story held by one language or region. They disagree on episodes and emphasis. That variety is part of Rāma’s cultural reach.

The Rāmlīlā tradition stages episodes of Rāma’s life, often culminating in Daśaharā and Dīpāvalī celebrations. Household Rām-nām, public processions, miniature painting and contemporary devotional music all make the epic present-tense.

ZAPPY: Which Rāmāyaṇa is the real one?

PROF. BOLT: Vālmīki is the oldest major surviving Sanskrit telling. Many communities have retold it as their own; understanding the differences is more illuminating than pretending they do not exist.

A PILGRIM’S ROUTE

One thoughtful day; two is better

When A sensible sequence
Early morning Sarayū ghāṭs and quiet prayer, subject to weather and safety
Morning Hanumān Garhī; then Kanak Bhawan
Midday Rāma Janmabhūmi darśan, according to current security and queue arrangements
Afternoon Daśaratha Mahal, Nāgeśvarnāth and old-town lanes
Evening Return toward the river and see the illuminated ghāṭs
Second day Guptār Ghāṭ and deeper local shrines; a guided short parikramā

Practical: Carry identity documents if requested by current authorities; leave prohibited items at authorised facilities; expect restricted photography and security screening around the temple. Festival days demand patience. Dress modestly, respect working worship spaces, and use official directions rather than unofficial queue promises.

AYODHYĀ ON ONE PAGE

The city in five lines

The city. Ayodhyā, on the Sarayū, is the epic capital of Kosala and first of the Saptapurī.

The story. Rāma accepts exile, returns after fourteen years, and becomes the model of dharma-governed kingship.

The sacred centre. Rāma Lallā at the Janmabhūmi temple, consecrated on 22 January 2024.

The circuit. Hanumān Garhī, Kanak Bhawan, Daśaratha Mahal, the Sarayū and pilgrim parikramās.

The lesson. A kingdom is holy not because it is rich, but because power is asked to answer to duty.

THE MANY RĀMĀYAṆAS

A story told in many languages

The Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa is the most influential Sanskrit narrative, but it is not the only Rāmāyaṇa. Kampaṉ's Tamil Irāmāvatāram, Tulsīdās's Awadhi Rāmcaritmānas, Kṛttivāsa's Bengali retelling, Jain narratives and Southeast Asian adaptations bring different emphases to the familiar story.

These texts do not always agree on plot details. Sītā's trials, the portrayal of Rāvaṇa, Hanumān's exploits and the treatment of Rāma as king or deity vary from tradition to tradition. Comparing them is a way to understand the scale of the story's cultural life, not to rank one community's devotion above another.

The famous public Rāmlīlā performances give this literary inheritance a stage, often culminating in Dussehra. Ayodhyā's temples and festivals thus sit within a network of storytelling that extends far beyond the city.

KampaṉTamil poetic retelling; distinctive aesthetic and theological voice

WHAT RĀMA RĀJYA MEANS

The ideal behind the royal city

Rāma-rājya is often translated as the ideal kingdom of Rāma. In devotional language it means a ruler who treats office as duty, protects the vulnerable and attempts to place public welfare before private comfort. It is a moral image rather than a recoverable constitution or a verified description of an ancient administrative system.

The Rāmāyaṇa does not make ideal rule effortless. It repeatedly shows conflicts between competing duties: the father's promise, the son's obedience, the wife's dignity, the king's public obligations and the cost of separation. That tension is why the epic remains a text for discussion and not merely praise.

A pilgrim may visit the temples as places of darśan, while a reader approaches the city as a question about ethics: what obligations come with the power to rule?

IdealPublic welfare, truthfulness, restraint and responsibility
DifficultyConflicts arise when personal loyalty and public duty collide
