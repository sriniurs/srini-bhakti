The Tamil Temple

How a Tamil temple town is built · explained and illustrated

Gateways, halls and the god at the centre

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt read the Tamil temple from Pallava caves to Chola towers and Nāyaka gateways, and learn the parts, the rituals and the hymns that every temple in this series shares.

WHERE WE ARE

Tamil Nadu has more great temples than almost any region on earth. Many are still in full worship after a thousand years, and some are the size of small towns.

They share one language of building: a sanctum under a stepped tower, enclosures one inside another, pillared halls, a tank, and tall gateway towers, gopurams, that you can see from miles away.

This overview teaches that language once, so that each temple in the series can concentrate on its own god, story and festivals.

ZAPPY: Is every Tamil temple this big?

PROF. BOLT: No. Most are village temples with one shrine and a porch. The big temples simply repeat the same parts many times over.

A THOUSAND YEARS IN THREE STEPS

Caves, towers, gateways

The story

Pallava caves. In the 7th century the Pallava kings of Kāñcīpuram cut shrines into rock and carved whole temples from boulders at Māmallapuram, now a UNESCO World Heritage site.
The first built temples. Around 700 the Pallavas built in stone: the Shore Temple at Māmallapuram and the Kailāsanātha at Kāñcīpuram, with stepped towers and walls full of sculpture.
Chola towers. From the 9th to the 13th centuries the Cholas built hundreds of temples. Under Rājarāja I the Big Temple at Thanjavur raised its vimāna to about 66 m (Part 3).
Gateways grow. From the 12th century gateways began to outgrow shrines. Under Vijayanagara and the Nāyakas (14th–17th centuries) temples became walled cities with towering gopurams, as at Madurai and Srirangam.
Still building. Renovation never stopped. Srirangam’s great southern gopuram was finished in 1987, and new temples are still built by traditional sthapatis today.

ZAPPY: So the tall gateways are the newest parts?

PROF. BOLT: Usually. In most great Tamil temples the small sanctum at the centre is the oldest part, wrapped in later rings.

Pallava caves → Chola towers → Nāyaka gateways.

THE PLAN: RINGS ROUND A CENTRE

From the street to the god

Rings. The plan is a set of walled rings, the prākārams, one inside another, with the sanctum at the very centre. Srirangam has seven; most big temples have two to five.

Gates on the axes. Each ring has gateways, usually on all four sides, the main one on the axis facing the deity. The outer gopurams are the tallest.

What fills the rings. Halls, shrines for the goddess and other deities, the tank, the temple kitchen, store-rooms, the flower garden, the vehicle hall and the chariot shed.

Out into the town. Beyond the outer wall the streets of the town often run in squares round the temple: the car streets (see How to Read Part 2).

Reading direction. From street to sanctum you go from loud to quiet, light to dark, newer to older. Each gateway is a step inward.

ZAPPY: Why so many walls?

PROF. BOLT: Each was built by a later patron, to enlarge and protect the temple. The walls also mark degrees of sacredness: the nearer the centre, the holier.

THE VIMĀNA

The tower over the god

Over the sanctum. The vimāna is the sanctum and its tower together. It is always over the god, never over a passage.

Storeys. It rises in storeys, each edged with a parapet of miniature shrines. Count the storeys: one or two for a small shrine, thirteen at Thanjavur.

Crown and finial. On top sits a dome-like crown stone, the śikharam: square, eight-sided or round. Above it, one or more metal kalaśams.

Golden roofs. In many great temples the vimāna is covered in gilded copper: the Ānanda Nilayam at Tirumala, the golden roof of Chidambaram’s hall, the golden vimāna of Srirangam.

Read the niches. On a Chola Śiva temple, the wall niches usually hold Dakṣiṇāmūrti on the south, Liṅgodbhava on the west and Brahmā on the north (How to Read Part 4).

ZAPPY: Why is the vimāna often smaller than the gopuram?

PROF. BOLT: Because the shrine is old and holy and is not rebuilt bigger; gateways could be added freely. The god’s house stays; the doors grow.

THE GOPURAM

Reading a gateway tower

Stone base. The lower part, with the passage through it, is granite, carved with pilasters and figures. The door frame is often very tall, for festival vehicles to pass.

Brick above. The storeys above are brick and timber, covered in lime stucco figures: gods, demons, guardians, scenes from the purāṇas, painted in bright colours.

Odd numbers. Gopurams rise in odd numbers of storeys: five, seven, nine, eleven or more. The roof is a long barrel vault with a row of pot finials.

Tallest. Srirangam’s Rājagopuram is about 73 m. Tamil Nadu’s state emblem shows a gopuram, that of the Āṇḍāḷ temple at Srivilliputhur.

Read the colour. The bright paint is renewed at each kumbhābhiṣekam, the renewal ceremony. The paint is new; the habit is old.

ZAPPY: Are the figures just decoration?

PROF. BOLT: They are a catalogue of the temple’s world: its gods, its stories, its guardians. Spend five minutes on one face and you will find whole purāṇas.

THE HALLS

Maṇḍapams for every purpose

Before the sanctum. Halls in front of the sanctum hold worshippers during the rituals: the mukha-maṇḍapam and the mahā-maṇḍapam.

Festival halls. Out in the enclosures stand halls for festivals: the kalyāṇa maṇḍapam for the god’s wedding, the vasanta maṇḍapam for spring, the ūñjal maṇḍapam with a swing.

Thousand pillars. The Nāyaka-period “thousand-pillar halls” at Madurai, Srirangam and elsewhere are among the largest stone halls in India.

Pillars that tell the date. Square Chola pillars, slender Vijayanagara clusters, and Nāyaka pillars with rearing horses and yāḷis, lion-like beasts, carved from one block.

Portraits. Some halls have life-size portrait sculptures of the patrons, kings and queens with folded hands, facing the god.

ZAPPY: What is a yāḷi?

PROF. BOLT: A mythical beast with a lion’s body and sometimes an elephant’s trunk, a favourite guardian on Tamil temple pillars.

TANK, TREE AND TĪRTHA

Each temple’s own water and tree

Three things. Tamil tradition says a great temple has three sacred things: the deity (mūrti), the place (sthalam) and the water (tīrtham).

The tank. Most big temples have a stepped tank in or beside the enclosure, named and with its own story, used for bathing and the float festival.

The tree. Each temple also has its sthala vṛkṣam, a sacred tree linked to its legend: a mango at Kāñcī Ekāmbareśvara, a jackfruit, a vilvam, a punnai.

The story. The sthala purāṇam, the temple’s own legend, explains how the deity came there, who worshipped, and why the place is holy.

Read the three. In each article of this series, look for the deity, the tank and the tree. Together they make the temple’s identity.

ZAPPY: Why is a tree so important?

PROF. BOLT: Many temples began as a shrine under a tree. The tree is often older in legend than the stone, and it is still worshipped.

HOW WORSHIP IS DONE

Āgamas, priests and the daily round

Rule books. Temple worship follows the Āgamas: in Śiva temples the Śaiva Āgamas; in Viṣṇu temples the Pāñcarātra or the Vaikhānasa tradition. These texts govern building, images, rituals and festivals.

Priests. Each tradition has its hereditary priests: Śivācāryas in many Śiva temples; Bhaṭṭācāryas in Viṣṇu temples. At Chidambaram the Dīkṣitars serve.

Six times a day. Many big temples hold six daily pūjās, from the early morning awakening to the night ceremony when the god retires. Times vary by temple.

Abhiṣekam and alaṅkāram. Bathing the deity, dressing and decorating it, offering food, waving lamps: these are the heart of every pūjā.

Festivals. The year turns on festivals: Brahmotsavam, Kārttikai Dīpam, Ārudrā Darśanam, Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, Panguni Uttiram, Skanda Ṣaṣṭhī.

ZAPPY: Can anyone watch?

PROF. BOLT: Devotees watch the pūjās from the hall. Rules about who may enter where differ by temple; follow the boards and the staff.

TEMPLES THAT WERE SUNG

The Tēvāram and the Divya Prabandham

Saint-poets. Between roughly the 6th and 9th centuries, Tamil saint-poets travelled from temple to temple singing in Tamil: the Nāyanmārs for Śiva and the Āḻvārs for Viṣṇu.

Tēvāram. The hymns of Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar, collected as the Tēvāram, sing of 276 Śiva temples, called the Pāṭal Peṟṟa Talams, “places that received songs”.

Divya Prabandham. The four thousand verses of the twelve Āḻvārs, the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham, sing of 108 Viṣṇu temples, the Divya Deśams.

Still sung. Ōduvārs sing the Tēvāram in Śiva temples, and the Prabandham is recited in Viṣṇu temples, often in the god’s procession.

Why it matters. For many Tamil temples, being sung by a saint matters more than size or age. The hymns are part of the temple’s identity.

ZAPPY: Are all the temples in this series “sung”?

PROF. BOLT: Many are. Thanjavur’s Big Temple came later, but it was built by a king who loved the Tēvāram and had it sung there.

THE GOD WHO GOES OUT

Bronzes and processions

Festival bronzes. Beside the fixed stone image, temples keep bronze festival images, utsava mūrtis, which go out in procession. Chola bronzes are among the world’s great sculptures.

Vāhanams. Each festival day the god rides a different vehicle: Garuḍa, Hanumān, the serpent, the swan for Viṣṇu; Nandi, the bull, for Śiva; the peacock for Murugan.

The tēr. The temple chariot, the tēr, runs round the car streets on the great festival day, pulled by thousands.

The tank. The float festival, teppam, takes the god round the temple tank on a lit raft.

More in the series. How to Read a Hindu Temple, Part 2, tells this story in detail. Each temple article here names its own great festivals.

ZAPPY: Why do bronzes travel and not the stone image?

PROF. BOLT: The stone image is installed for ever. The bronze is the same god in a form that can come out to meet everyone.

STUCCO, PAINT AND KUMBHĀBHIṢEKAM

A temple kept alive

Renewal. Tamil temples are renewed and reconsecrated in a kumbhābhiṣekam, when sanctified water from many pots is poured over the finials. Many temples aim to hold one about every twelve years.

Before it. Towers are repaired and repainted, stucco figures remade, floors relaid, and the whole temple cleaned.

Living fabric. So what you see is both old and new: a Chola sanctum, a Nāyaka hall, a gopuram painted last year. Reading the layers is part of the visit (How to Read Part 11).

Care. Tamil Nadu’s Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments (HR&CE) Department administers most of the state’s big temples; some, like Thanjavur, are also protected monuments of the Archaeological Survey of India.

Balance. The best renewal keeps worship alive while protecting old inscriptions, paintings and stone.

ZAPPY: Why is paint allowed on such old temples?

PROF. BOLT: Because they are living temples, not ruins. The colour is part of worship. Conservators and temple boards try to keep both the colour and the history.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical and respectful
Dress. Many big temples ask for traditional dress: dhoti or trousers and shirt for men, sari, salwar or long skirt for women. Check the board at the gate.
Footwear. Leave footwear outside, usually at a counter. Carry a cloth bag for small things.
Timings. Temples close in the afternoon, typically for a few hours. Check timings, especially for darśana tickets and special pūjās.
Photography. Usually allowed in outer areas, never in the sanctum. Phones may have to be deposited in some temples.
Order. Follow the queue and the local custom: often the main god first, then the goddess and other shrines, then the circumambulation.

ZAPPY: Is there a right order to see things?

PROF. BOLT: Each temple has its own custom. Ask the staff or follow the regulars, and give way to people at worship.
