TEMPLES OF TAMIL NADU · PART 7 OF 10 · THE ELEPHANT HILL

Kanchipuram Varadarāja

Viṣṇu who grants boons · explained and illustrated

Varadar on Attigiri, the Elephant Hill

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt climb the Elephant Hill at Kanchipuram to meet Varadarāja, hear about Rāmānuja, look for the golden lizards, and learn why a wooden god rises from the tank once in forty years.

WHERE WE ARE

Kanchipuram, an hour or two from Chennai, is called the city of a thousand temples. It is one of the seven sacred cities of India, the Sapta Purī, and was the capital of the Pallavas.

Tradition divides it into Śiva Kāñcī, around the Ekāmbareśvara temple, and Viṣṇu Kāñcī, around the temple of Varadarāja, “the king who grants boons”, on a small rise called Attigiri or Hastigiri, the Elephant Hill.

It is one of the most important of the 108 Divya Deśams, and the city of Rāmānuja’s early life.

PROF. BOLT: One town, many temples. The goddess Kāmākṣī and Śiva Ekāmbareśvara have their own guides on this site; this one is for Viṣṇu.

ZAPPY: Why “Elephant Hill”?

PROF. BOLT: Hasti means elephant. In the legend, the elephant Airāvata or the elephant-form of a sage worshipped here; the little hill took the name.

THE GOD WHO ROSE FROM THE FIRE

The temple’s story

The story

Brahmā’s sacrifice. The temple’s story says that Brahmā performed a great fire sacrifice at Kāñcī to see Viṣṇu.
Sarasvatī’s river. His consort Sarasvatī, offended, came as a flooding river to wash away the sacrifice; Viṣṇu lay across its path and stopped it, a story told at several Kāñcī temples.
Rising from the flames. At the climax Viṣṇu appeared from the sacrificial fire as Varadarāja, the king of boon-givers, and agreed to stay.
On the hill. He stands on the hill’s top in the sanctum, facing west, his right hand raised in the gesture of granting boons, the varada mudrā.
Read the name. Every name tells the story: Varada, giver of boons; Devarāja, king of gods; in Tamil, Pēraruḷāḷan, “the one of great grace”.

ZAPPY: Facing west?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, unlike most temples. Temple tradition links it to the direction of Brahmā’s fire altar.

Varadarāja: Viṣṇu who rose from Brahmā’s fire to grant boons.

CLIMBING THE HILL

From the gopuram to the sanctum

Gateways. Tall gopurams stand on the east and west. Through them you enter a large outer court with halls, shrines and the tank.

The hundred-pillar hall. Near the entrance stands a Vijayanagara-period hall famous for its carved pillars (page “Stone chains”).

Up the hill. At the centre rises the hill, Attigiri. Inside the inner enclosure a flight of steps leads up to the sanctum. Tradition counts 24 steps, linked to the 24 syllables of the Gāyatrī.

Two levels. On the lower level is a shrine of Narasiṃha in a cave-like chamber; on the upper level stands Varadarāja. Devotees visit both.

Goddess. Perundēvi Tāyār has her own shrine in the court; devotees visit her as part of the round.

ZAPPY: A temple inside a hill?

PROF. BOLT: The hill is small, more like a raised platform of rock built up into a sanctum. But it gives the climb a sense of ascent.

THE GOLDEN LIZARDS

A ceiling everyone touches

Look up. In a chamber of the temple, on the ceiling, are two lizards, one covered in gold and one in silver.

Why lizards?. In Indian folk belief, a lizard falling on a person is an omen. Touching these lizards is believed to remove the effect of such omens.

A legend. The temple’s story links them to two disciples of a sage who were cursed into lizards and freed by Varadarāja’s grace.

Queues. There is often a queue and a small fee. Devotees climb a few steps, touch both lizards, and move on.

Read it as. A small, homely piece of worship inside a grand temple: people bring their everyday fears to the god.

ZAPPY: Does it work?

PROF. BOLT: That is for each devotee to decide. What is certain is that people have been touching them for generations.

ATTI VARADAR

The god under the water

A second image. Beside the stone Varadarāja on the hill, the temple has an ancient image of the god carved from the wood of the fig tree, the atti. It is called Atti Varadar.

Under the tank. For most of the time this wooden image rests in a chamber beneath the waters of the temple tank, the Anantasaras.

Every forty years. Once every forty years the tank is drained and Atti Varadar is brought out and shown to devotees for about forty-eight days, first reclining and then standing.

2019. The last showing was in 2019, when millions of pilgrims came to Kanchipuram. The next is expected around 2059.

Why the water?. Tradition says the wooden image was placed in the tank long ago, perhaps to protect it, and the stone image became the main object of worship.

ZAPPY: Forty years is a long wait!

PROF. BOLT: For many, once in a lifetime. That is part of the power of the event: whole families plan around it.

RĀMĀNUJA AT KĀÑCĪ

The teacher and the temple

Rāmānuja. Rāmānuja, the great teacher of Viśiṣṭādvaita and the Śrī Vaiṣṇava tradition, spent his early years at Kāñcī, studying and serving at this temple.

Tirukkacci Nambi. His guide here was Tirukkacci Nambi, a devotee who fanned the deity. Tradition says Varadarāja spoke to him and through him gave Rāmānuja six teachings.

Sannyāsa. Tradition holds that Rāmānuja took sannyāsa, renunciation, at Kāñcī before going to Srirangam, which became his centre.

Vedānta Deśika. The poet-philosopher Vedānta Deśika, born near Kāñcī in the 13th century, wrote many hymns on Varadarāja.

Two traditions. Kāñcī is important to both Vaḍakalai and Teṉkalai Śrī Vaiṣṇavas; the temple’s customs reflect both. We describe, not decide, between them.

ZAPPY: Is Rāmānuja shown in the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, there are shrines and images of the Āḻvārs and ācāryas, as in all Śrī Vaiṣṇava temples, including Rāmānuja.

STONE CHAINS

The hundred-pillar hall

Vijayanagara work. The hall near the entrance, often called the hundred-pillar hall, is a masterpiece of 16th-century Vijayanagara sculpture.

Pillars. Its pillars are carved with rearing horses and riders, yāḷis, hunters, dancers and scenes from the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata.

Stone chains. At the corners hang chains of stone links, each link carved from the same block, free to move. Look for them; guides love to show them.

Festival use. The hall is used during festivals, including the god’s procession and rest, and for the Garuḍa Sevai preparations.

Read it. Compare these pillars with the plain Chola ones at Thanjavur (Part 3): same tradition, four centuries apart.

ZAPPY: How do you carve a chain from one stone?

PROF. BOLT: Patiently! The sculptor cuts away everything between the links, leaving each one free. One mistake and the chain breaks.

GARUḌA SEVAI

The god on the eagle

Brahmotsavam. The temple’s great festival is the Brahmotsavam in the Tamil month of Vaikāsi (May–June), when Varadarāja goes out on a different vehicle each day.

Garuḍa Sevai. The most famous morning is the Garuḍa Sevai, when the god rides Garuḍa, the eagle, through the streets. Huge crowds gather from before dawn.

A brief glimpse. Many devotees wait for the moment the god emerges from the gateway, the Gopura Darśanam, when the procession pauses under the tower.

The chariot. Later in the festival the god rides the temple chariot round the streets.

Through the year. Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, Navarātri, and other festivals fill the year. Many households in the city still time their lives by them.

ZAPPY: Why is Garuḍa Sevai so loved?

PROF. BOLT: Garuḍa is Viṣṇu’s own vehicle, his closest devotee. Seeing the god on Garuḍa feels like seeing him as he truly travels.

THE CITY OF TEMPLES

Kanchipuram beyond Varadarāja

Ekāmbareśvara. The great Śiva temple of the earth element, with its ancient mango tree (guide on this site, Pañca Bhūta series).

Kāmākṣī. The goddess temple at the heart of the city (guide on this site, Śrī Vidyā series).

Kailāsanātha. The Pallava temple of about 700, built by Rājasiṃha, small and exquisitely carved, a landmark in Tamil temple architecture.

Vaikuṇṭha Perumāḷ. An 8th-century Pallava Viṣṇu temple with panels telling the history of the Pallava kings, and a sanctum on three levels.

Silk and learning. Kanchipuram is also famous for its silk saris, for the Kāñcī Maṭha, and as an old centre of learning, the “ghaṭikā”.

ZAPPY: How many temples can I see in a day?

PROF. BOLT: Four or five comfortably, if you start early and allow for the afternoon closing.

A LONG HISTORY

Pallava, Chola, Vijayanagara

Old roots. Worship of Varadarāja at Kāñcī goes back to the Āḻvārs, who sang of the god here; Bhūtattāḻvār and Pēyāḻvār are among those linked with Kāñcī.

Chola building. Much of the present temple was built or rebuilt from the 11th century under the Cholas, with many Chola inscriptions on its walls.

Later rings. Later rulers, especially the Vijayanagara kings and their governors, added halls, gateways and enclosures.

Inscriptions. The temple has hundreds of inscriptions recording gifts from kings, queens, merchants and devotees across these centuries.

Read it. Walk outwards from the hill to the gates and you walk forward through time: a good place to practise How to Read Part 11.

ZAPPY: Were there foreign donors?

PROF. BOLT: Popular tradition remembers gifts even from British officers of the 18th century. Treat such stories as tradition unless a record is cited.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Kanchipuram is about 70 km from Chennai by road; trains and buses run often.
Timings. The temple opens early and closes in the afternoon for some hours. Check before going, especially on festival days.
Dress. Traditional or modest dress; footwear outside.
Inside. Climb to the sanctum, visit Narasiṃha below, the goddess, and the golden lizards. Photography only in outer areas.
Combine. Ekāmbareśvara, Kāmākṣī and Kailāsanātha make a full day; leave time for the silk shops if you like.

ZAPPY: Best time to go?

PROF. BOLT: Early morning on a weekday for quiet darśana; Garuḍa Sevai in Vaikāsi for the spectacle.
