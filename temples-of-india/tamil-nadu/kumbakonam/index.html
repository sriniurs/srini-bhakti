Kumbakonam

A town around the Mahāmaham tank · explained and illustrated

The town of the pot

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt explore Kumbakonam, the town named after a pot of nectar: Śiva’s Kumbeśvara, Viṣṇu’s Sārṅgapāṇi chariot, the great Mahāmaham tank, and the temples all around.

WHERE WE ARE

In the Kāverī delta, between the Kāverī and Arasalār rivers, lies Kumbakonam, a town so full of temples that you can hardly walk a street without passing one.

Its name comes from a kumbha, a pot, which in the town’s great legend carried the seed of creation through the cosmic flood and came to rest here.

This article is different from the others in the series: it is about a whole temple town. We visit its main Śiva and Viṣṇu temples, its great tank, and the circle of famous temples around it.

ZAPPY: A town as a temple?

PROF. BOLT: In a way. Kumbakonam’s temples, streets and tank work together, especially every twelve years at the Mahāmaham.

PROF. BOLT: Dozens in the town alone, and many more in the villages around. Locals say you could visit a different one every day for months.

THE POT OF NECTAR

How Kumbakonam got its name

The story

The flood. At the end of an age, the town’s legend says, a great flood, the pralaya, was coming. Brahmā asked Śiva how creation could begin again.
The pot. Śiva told him to fill a pot, a kumbha, with nectar, sacred earth and the seeds of creation, and set it afloat.
The arrow. When the flood receded, the pot came to rest here. Śiva, as a hunter, shot an arrow that broke the pot, and the nectar flowed out over the land.
The tank. Where the nectar collected became the Mahāmaham tank and the Potramarai tank, sacred waters of the town.
The liṅga. From the sand and nectar Śiva shaped a liṅga: Ādi Kumbeśvara, the “first lord of the pot”, said to be slightly pot-shaped.

ZAPPY: So the whole town is the broken pot?

PROF. BOLT: In the legend, yes: the pieces of the pot are linked with sacred places around the town. Kumbakonam is where the world began again.

Kumbha + koṇam: the corner where the pot came to rest.

ĀDI KUMBEŚVARA

Śiva of the pot

The temple. Ādi Kumbeśvara is the main Śiva temple of Kumbakonam, at the heart of the town, with a tall gopuram on the east.

The liṅga. The sanctum holds the liṅga of the legend. Devotees see it as Śiva in the form of the pot of creation.

Maṅgalāmbikā. The goddess is Maṅgalāmbikā, with her own shrine; she is one of the important goddess shrines of the Tamil country.

Halls. The temple has long pillared corridors and a Nāyaka-period hall with carved pillars, including one with the 27 star constellations and 12 zodiac signs carved on a single stone, as guides point out.

Sung. Ādi Kumbeśvara is a Pāṭal Peṟṟa Talam, sung in the Tēvāram by Sambandar and Appar.

ZAPPY: Why is the goddess so important here?

PROF. BOLT: Maṅgalāmbikā, “the auspicious mother”, is the town’s protector. Many devotees visit her first.

SĀRṄGAPĀṆI

Viṣṇu with the bow, in a chariot

The god. Sārṅgapāṇi, “he who holds the bow Sārṅga”, is Viṣṇu reclining, worshipped here as Āravamudan, “the insatiable nectar”. The goddess is Komalavalli Tāyār.

A Divya Deśam. It is one of the 108 Divya Deśams, one of the most important after Srirangam and Tirupati, sung by several Āḻvārs.

A chariot sanctum. The central shrine is built as a chariot with stone wheels, drawn by horses and elephants: Viṣṇu arriving from heaven (How to Read Part 10).

Tall gateway. Its rājagopuram, of many storeys, is one of the tallest in the town.

Two doors. The sanctum has two entrances, the Uttarāyaṇa and Dakṣiṇāyana doors, opened in turn for each half of the year.

ZAPPY: What does the chariot mean?

PROF. BOLT: The story says Viṣṇu came down from Vaikuṇṭha in a chariot to marry Lakṣmī, born here as the daughter of the sage Hema. The shrine is that chariot.

TEN VERSES AND FOUR THOUSAND

How the Divya Prabandham was recovered

Lost songs. By the 10th century, tradition says, most of the Āḻvārs’ Tamil hymns had been forgotten.

Ten verses. The scholar Nāthamuni heard visiting pilgrims at his home town, Kāṭṭumannārkoil, sing ten verses of Nammāḻvār addressed to Āravamudan of Kumbakonam. The last verse mentioned a thousand.

The search. He set out to find the rest. Tradition says he meditated on Nammāḻvār at his birthplace and received all four thousand verses.

The Prabandham. Nāthamuni arranged them as the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham and set them to music for temple recital.

Why it matters. So Sārṅgapāṇi holds a special place: its hymn led to the recovery of the Tamil Vaiṣṇava scripture.

ZAPPY: Is that history or legend?

PROF. BOLT: It is the Śrī Vaiṣṇava tradition. Historians agree Nāthamuni compiled the Prabandham; the details of the story are tradition.

MORE TEMPLES IN TOWN

Nāgeśvara, Chakrapāṇi, Rāmasvāmī

Nāgeśvara. The Nāgeśvara temple is an early Chola stone temple with exceptionally fine sculptures on its sanctum walls. Tradition notes that in the month of Chittirai sunlight falls on the liṅga.

Chakrapāṇi. The Chakrapāṇi temple honours Viṣṇu as the discus, Sudarśana, shown with eight arms. The temple is popular with devotees seeking protection.

Rāmasvāmī. The Rāmasvāmī temple, built in the Nāyaka period, has a hall of finely carved pillars and walls painted with the whole Rāmāyaṇa.

Kāśī Viśvanātha. By the Mahāmaham tank, the Kāśī Viśvanātha temple has shrines of the nine sacred rivers as maidens, linked with the Mahāmaham legend.

And more. Small Chola and Nāyaka temples are everywhere; even a short walk shows how deeply the town lives with its gods.

ZAPPY: How do I choose?

PROF. BOLT: For a single day: Kumbeśvara, Sārṅgapāṇi, Nāgeśvara and the Mahāmaham tank. Add Rāmasvāmī for its paintings.

THE MAHĀMAHAM TANK

Sixteen shrines and twenty wells

The tank. The Mahāmaham tank is a large tank in the middle of the town, covering several acres, with steps on all sides.

Sixteen shrines. Around its edges stand sixteen small shrines, the Ṣoḍaśa Mahāliṅga shrines, built in the 16th century by Govinda Dīkṣitar, minister of the Nāyakas of Thanjavur.

Twenty wells. Within the tank are wells or springs named as tīrthas, linked to sacred rivers. Pilgrims bathe at them in turn during the festival.

The rivers come. The legend says that at the Mahāmaham the nine sacred rivers, such as the Gaṅgā, Yamunā and Narmadā, come to bathe here to wash away the sins people left in them.

Every day. Most of the time the tank is a quiet public space where people walk, sit and pray; its revival and cleaning is a matter of town pride.

ZAPPY: The Gaṅgā comes to Kumbakonam?

PROF. BOLT: That is the beautiful idea: the rivers themselves need purifying, and they come here. So this tank is called the place where even the Gaṅgā bathes.

THE MAHĀMAHAM FESTIVAL

Once in twelve years

When. The Mahāmaham takes place in the Tamil month of Māsi (February–March), when Jupiter is in Leo and the moon is in the star Makham. This happens about once every twelve years.

2016. The last Mahāmaham was held in February 2016, drawing millions of pilgrims. The next is expected around 2028.

The bath. At the auspicious time, pilgrims bathe in the tank, then go on to bathe in the Potramarai tank and the Kāverī.

Gods come too. Festival images from the town’s temples are brought to the tank for the tīrthavāri, the sacred bath.

Every year. A smaller festival, Māsimaham, is held every year at the same season.

ZAPPY: Why does a twelve-year festival feel like the Kumbh?

PROF. BOLT: They follow a similar logic: Jupiter’s cycle and a sacred bath. Some call the Mahāmaham the Kumbh Melā of the South.

TEMPLES AROUND KUMBAKONAM

A circle of famous shrines

Swamimalai. About 8 km away, one of Murugan’s six abodes: Murugan as teacher of his father Śiva. The village is also famous for bronze casting, with a GI tag for its icons.

Dārāsuram. The Airāvateśvara temple, a UNESCO-listed Chola masterpiece with its chariot hall (Part 3 and How to Read Part 10).

Uppiliappan. A Divya Deśam of Viṣṇu, where food offerings are made without salt.

Patteeswaram. A Śiva temple linked with Durgā and the Chola kings.

Navagraha temples. The nine temples of the planetary deities, from Suryanar Koil to Thirunallar, are spread around Kumbakonam, which is the usual base for the circuit.

ZAPPY: How many days do I need?

PROF. BOLT: Two or three for Kumbakonam and its neighbours; more if you do the Navagraha circuit.

THE TEMPLE TOWN’S LIFE

Brass, silk, coffee and scholars

Bronze. Swamimalai’s sthapatis still cast temple bronzes by the lost-wax method, the same craft as the Chola bronzes.

Silk and brass. The region is known for silk weaving (Thirubuvanam), brass vessels and betel leaves.

Coffee. Kumbakonam is also famous across Tamil Nadu for its filter coffee, a modern pleasure in an old town.

Scholars. The town has long been a centre of learning, with old maṭhas, Sanskrit and Tamil scholars, and colleges.

Living together. Temples, crafts and households form one fabric. The festival calendar shapes the town’s year.

ZAPPY: Is the coffee really that good?

PROF. BOLT: Many Tamils say so loudly! Try it after your morning darśana and decide for yourself.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Kumbakonam is about 40 km from Thanjavur by road and rail; Tiruchirappalli has the nearest major airport.
Timings. Temples close in the afternoon, usually for a few hours. Plan mornings and evenings.
Walk. The main temples are close together; walk or take an auto between them.
Festivals. Māsimaham every year; the Mahāmaham about every twelve years. Expect enormous crowds then.
Dress and footwear. Modest dress; leave footwear outside each temple.

ZAPPY: Base for the Chola temples?

PROF. BOLT: Kumbakonam is a good base for Dārāsuram and Gangaikondacholapuram; Thanjavur is an hour away.
