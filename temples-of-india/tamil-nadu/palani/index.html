TEMPLES OF TAMIL NADU · PART 8 OF 10 · MURUGAN’S HILL

Palani Daṇḍāyudhapāṇi

The renouncer with the staff · explained and illustrated

The god who left home for a hill

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt climb Palani hill to meet Murugan as the renouncer, hear the story of the fruit, carry Idumban’s kāvaḍi, and taste the famous pañcāmṛtam.

WHERE WE ARE

In the Dindigul district, where the plains meet the Western Ghats, a rocky hill rises above the town of Palani. On its top is one of the most visited temples in Tamil Nadu.

Here Murugan, the son of Śiva and Pārvatī, stands not as a prince but as a young renouncer: head shaven, a loincloth, a staff in hand. He is called Daṇḍāyudhapāṇi, “he who holds the staff as his weapon”.

Palani is one of the six sacred abodes of Murugan, the Āṟupaḍai Vīḍu, and the goal of huge foot pilgrimages at Thaipusam.

ZAPPY: Six abodes?

PROF. BOLT: Six famous Murugan temples sung in old Tamil poetry: Tiruparankundram, Tiruchendur, Palani, Swamimalai, Tiruttani and Pazhamudircholai.

THE STORY OF THE FRUIT

Why Murugan came to Palani

The story

A fruit. The sage Nārada brought a single fruit of wisdom, the gñāna paḻam, to Śiva and Pārvatī. Their two sons both wanted it.
A race. Śiva set a contest: whoever went round the world first would win. Murugan leapt on his peacock and flew round the earth.
Gaṇeśa’s answer. Gaṇeśa simply walked round his parents, saying they were his whole world, and was given the fruit.
Murugan leaves. Hurt, Murugan gave up his ornaments and went away to the hill, as a renouncer. His parents followed to console him.
“You are the fruit”. Śiva told him, in a Tamil play on words, “Paḻam nī”, “you yourself are the fruit”. Tradition says the hill took the name Paḻani from these words.

ZAPPY: So Gaṇeśa cheated?

PROF. BOLT: The story says he was wise. And Murugan’s anger turned into renunciation, which made him the teacher of wisdom himself.

Paḻam nī: “you are the fruit”.

THE IMAGE OF NINE MINERALS

Bhogar’s navapāṣāṇam

Bhogar. Temple tradition says the image of Daṇḍāyudhapāṇi was made by Bhogar, one of the eighteen Tamil siddhas, master yogis and alchemists.

Nine minerals. It is said to be made of navapāṣāṇam, a compound of nine minerals, which tradition holds to have healing power.

Abhiṣekam. The image is bathed many times a day. Devotees receive the abhiṣekam substances, such as pañcāmṛtam and sandal, as prasādam.

Bhogar’s shrine. On the hill, in the outer passage, is a shrine of Bhogar, believed to be his place of samādhi.

Care. The image is old and delicate. In recent decades the temple has limited some abhiṣekams to protect it.

ZAPPY: Is the navapāṣāṇam story history?

PROF. BOLT: It is the temple’s tradition and deeply believed. Scientists have not been allowed to test it, and that is how the temple wishes it.

THE CLIMB

Steps, winch and rope car

By foot. Most pilgrims climb the stone steps, about 690 of them, with shelters and small shrines along the way. Many climb barefoot, some chanting “Vel Vel”.

By winch. A winch railway, a small cable-hauled car on rails, carries pilgrims up the hill and has run for decades.

By rope car. A rope car, opened in 2004, lifts pilgrims in minutes over the north side of the hill.

The elephant path. A gentler path with a longer slope, the yāṉai pātai, also winds up the hill.

At the top. The temple stands within a walled enclosure with a gopuram; inside, the sanctum faces west.

ZAPPY: Which way should I go up?

PROF. BOLT: If you are fit, walk up early in the morning and take the winch or rope car down. The view rewards the climb.

IDUMBAN AND THE KĀVAḌI

How the pilgrimage began

Idumban. The temple story says the sage Agastya asked his disciple Idumban, an asura, to carry two hills, Śivagiri and Śaktigiri, from the north to the south.

Two hills on a pole. Idumban slung them from a pole across his shoulders. At Palani he set them down to rest and could not lift them again: Murugan, as a boy, was standing on one.

Defeated and blessed. After a fight, Idumban was defeated and blessed, and asked that whoever carried a load like his to Murugan should be blessed too.

The kāvaḍi. So devotees carry a kāvaḍi: a decorated arch on a pole, carrying milk, flowers or other offerings, dancing to the hill. It is one of Tamil Nadu’s great devotional sights.

Idumban’s shrine. On the smaller hill nearby is Idumban’s shrine. Many pilgrims visit him first.

ZAPPY: Is the kāvaḍi always so elaborate?

PROF. BOLT: From a simple pot of milk on a pole to towering peacock-feather arches. Size matters less than the vow behind it.

THAIPUSAM

The great walk to Palani

When. Thaipusam falls on the day of the Pūsam star in the Tamil month of Thai (January–February), usually near the full moon.

Walking. In the weeks before, devotees from across Tamil Nadu and beyond walk to Palani, some for many days, in saffron or green, many carrying kāvaḍis.

Vows. Many keep a period of fasting and discipline beforehand. Some carry milk pots, some shave their heads as an offering.

At Palani. The town and the hill are packed for days. The festival image goes out in procession, and the temple chariot runs in the streets below.

Abroad. Thaipusam is also celebrated by Tamil communities in Malaysia, Singapore and elsewhere, with huge processions.

ZAPPY: Why walk when there are buses?

PROF. BOLT: The walk is the offering. The days on the road, the heat and the songs make the darśana at the end mean more.

PAÑCĀMṚTAM

The taste of Palani

Five nectars. Pañcāmṛtam, “five nectars”, is Palani’s famous prasādam: a thick sweet made of bananas, jaggery, ghee, honey and cardamom, with dates or sugar candy in some versions.

A special banana. It traditionally uses the hill banana of the region, which gives it a special taste.

GI tag. Palani pañcāmṛtam received a Geographical Indication tag in 2019, the first temple prasādam in Tamil Nadu to receive one.

In the abhiṣekam. Pañcāmṛtam is also used to bathe the image, and devotees value the blessed portion.

Take it home. It is sold in tins and jars at the temple counters and carried home as a gift for family.

ZAPPY: Does it keep?

PROF. BOLT: The sealed tins keep for a while. Check the date on the pack; it is a fresh food, not a sweet that lasts for ever.

PALANI BELOW THE HILL

Tiru Āviṉaṉkuḍi and the town

The old abode. At the foot of the hill is the temple of Tiru Āviṉaṉkuḍi. In the old poem Tirumurukāṟṟuppaḍai, this is the name of the third abode of Murugan, so many devotees visit it first.

Murugan with his peacock. Here Murugan is seen as a child on his peacock, a gentle contrast to the renouncer on the hill.

Periyanāyakī. In the town, the Periyanāyakī Amman temple is linked with Palani’s festivals; some processions start there.

Golden chariot. On the hill, a small golden chariot carries the festival image round the outer passage in the evenings, sponsored by devotees.

A pilgrim town. Shops, lodges, barbers for tonsure, and the smell of pañcāmṛtam fill the streets: a town built around one hill.

ZAPPY: So Palani has two Murugans?

PROF. BOLT: The same god in two moods: a child on a peacock below, a renouncer on the hill above. Pilgrims meet both.

WORSHIP ON THE HILL

A day at the temple

Many pūjās. The temple holds many pūjās from early morning to night, each with its own name and offering.

Alaṅkāram. The image is dressed in different ways at different times: as a renouncer, as a king, as a child. Some devotees come for a particular alaṅkāram.

Tonsure. Many devotees offer their hair, shaving their heads in fulfilment of a vow, at halls at the foot of the hill.

Night. The last pūjā at night, when the god retires, is attended by devotees who stay on the hill late.

Rules. The temple is administered by the Tamil Nadu HR&CE Department. Darśana may require tickets for special queues; follow the posted rules.

ZAPPY: Why shave the head?

PROF. BOLT: It is an offering of pride and beauty, and an imitation of the god himself, who stands here with a shaven head.

THE SIX ABODES

Āṟupaḍai Vīḍu

Six abodes. The Tamil poem Tirumurukāṟṟuppaḍai and later tradition name six sacred abodes of Murugan, together the Āṟupaḍai Vīḍu.

The list. Tiruparankundram (his wedding), Tiruchendur (his victory), Palani (renunciation), Swamimalai (teacher of his father), Tiruttani (peace after battle), Pazhamudircholai (the grove).

In this series. Palani and Tiruchendur (Part 9) are the two best known and are in this series.

A pilgrimage. Some devotees visit all six in one journey; many families visit one each year.

Murugan’s land. Murugan is often called the god of the Tamils; these six shrines are the heart of his worship.

ZAPPY: Will all six get articles?

PROF. BOLT: A dedicated Murugan series could cover all six. For now, Palani and Tiruchendur represent them here.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Palani is on the rail line from Dindigul and has good bus links from Madurai, Coimbatore and Dindigul.
Going up. Steps, winch or rope car. Queues for winch and rope car can be long on festival days.
Darśana. Free and paid queues; special darśana tickets may be available. Allow several hours on busy days.
Dress. Modest dress; footwear left at the foot of the hill. The steps can be hot by midday.
Avoid. The Thaipusam week if you dislike crowds; go then if you want to see the pilgrimage at its height.

ZAPPY: Can I carry water?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and you should. Respect the temple’s rules on plastic and keep the hill clean.
