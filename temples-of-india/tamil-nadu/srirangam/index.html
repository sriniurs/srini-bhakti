BHŪLOKA VAIKUṆṬHA · 21 GOPURAS · 7 ENCLOSURES

Śrīraṅgam

The Temple of Raṅganātha · explained and illustrated

Heaven on earth, on an island

HOW THIS WORKS: A full guide to Śrīraṅgam: the island between the Kāverī and Kollidam, the reclining Raṅganātha and his legend from Laṅkā, the seven enclosures and 21 towers, Raṅganāyakī and Tulukka Nācciyār, Rāmānuja, the Āḻvārs and the Divya Prabandham, the 1323 invasion and return, and the great Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī.

WHERE WE ARE

On an island in the Kāverī near Tiruchirāppaḷḷi stands one of the largest functioning temple complexes in the world: Śrīraṅgam, home of Raṅganātha, Viṣṇu reclining on the serpent Ādiśeṣa. Śrīvaiṣṇavas call it simply Kōil, “the temple”, and Bhūloka Vaikuṇṭha, heaven on earth.

This full guide covers its legend, layout, deity and goddess, its saints and poets, its near-destruction and return, its architecture and its great festival. It is the last of the three Raṅgas of the Kāverī; the other two, and a short visit to this one, are in our Triraṅga series.

THE ISLAND

Between the Kāverī and the Kollidam

The setting. Just north of Tiruchirāppaḷḷi the Kāverī divides into two branches, the Kāverī and the Kollidam (Coleroon), enclosing a long island. On it stands Śrīraṅgam.

A temple-town. The outer enclosures are streets of houses, shops and maṭhas; the inner ones hold the shrines. Life and worship are wrapped in the same walls.

The Raṅga islands. Upstream, the Kāverī makes two more islands with Raṅganātha temples: Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa near Mysuru (the first Raṅga) and Śivanasamudra (the middle). Śrīraṅgam is the last.

Pilgrimage. Pilgrims bathe in the Kāverī at the Amma Maṇḍapam ghāṭ before entering. See Triraṅga for the three Raṅgas together.

ZAPPY: Same river as Śrīraṅgapaṭṭaṇa!

PROF. BOLT: The same Kāverī, Zappy, holding Raṅganātha three times on her way to the sea.

Held by the river.

RAṄGANĀTHA FROM LAṄKĀ

Why the Lord faces south

The story

Temple tradition (Śrīraṅga Māhātmya); details vary.

The image of Raṅganātha in a golden vimāna was worshipped, tradition says, by the kings of Ayodhyā, Rāma’s line.
After his coronation, Rāma gave it to Vibhīṣaṇa, Rāvaṇa’s brother who had joined him, to take to Laṅkā.
On the way Vibhīṣaṇa set the vimāna down on the island to perform his evening rites. When he tried to lift it again, it would not move.
The Lord told him he wished to stay by the Kāverī; the local Cōḻa king, Dharmavarma, would care for him. To comfort Vibhīṣaṇa, Raṅganātha agreed to face south, toward Laṅkā.
So Raṅganātha reclines facing south, his gaze on his devotee’s island, and Vibhīṣaṇa is honoured in the temple.

ZAPPY: He faces south for a friend?

PROF. BOLT: For a devotee who had to leave without him, Zappy. That’s the kindness the story remembers.

Facing south, for Vibhīṣaṇa.

PERIYA PERUMĀḶ

The great Lord reclining

The image. Raṅganātha, called Periya Perumāḷ, “the great Lord”, reclines on the coils of Ādiśeṣa beneath its five hoods, his head resting on his right hand. The image is large, made of stucco and painted, so it is not bathed; abhiṣeka is offered to a smaller image.

Namperumāḷ. The bronze processional image, “our Lord”, standing with his consorts, goes out for every festival; the name itself is affectionate.

The golden vimāna. Above the sanctum rises the golden Praṇavākāra vimāna, shaped like the syllable Oṃ, with an image of Paravāsudeva on it.

Garuḍa. A huge seated Garuḍa in the Garuḍa Maṇḍapam faces the sanctum. See Garuḍa and Nandi.

ZAPPY: Why not bathe the big image?

PROF. BOLT: It’s stucco, Zappy: water would harm it. So a little image takes the baths for him.

The Lord at rest.

SEVEN WALLS, TWENTY-ONE TOWERS

How the temple is laid out

Seven enclosures. The temple is planned as seven concentric rectangles (prākāras), said to stand for the seven worlds or the seven layers around the self. The outer two are town; the inner five hold the monuments. (Interpretation of the number.)

Twenty-one gopuras. Gateways in the walls, many on the south axis leading to the Lord. The Rājagopuram, about 73 metres, begun in Vijayanagara times and left unfinished for centuries, was completed in 1987 under the Ahobila Maṭha.

About 155 acres. One of the largest functioning Hindu temples in the world.

Reading the walls. Inscriptions in Tamil, Sanskrit, Kannada and Telugu cover the walls, recording gifts from Cōḻa, Pāṇḍya, Hoysaḷa and Vijayanagara rulers. See What a Temple Inscription Can Tell Us.

ZAPPY: A tower finished in 1987?

PROF. BOLT: Started centuries ago, Zappy, and finally completed in living memory. Temples can be patient.

Seven walls, one centre.

THE GODDESSES

Raṅganāyakī and Tulukka Nācciyār

🪷 Raṅganāyakī

Lakṣmī as the temple’s goddess never leaves her own shrine. Even the Lord goes to her: her processions stay within her enclosure.

💞 Serthi Sevai

Once a year, on Paṅguni Uttiram, the Lord and Raṅganāyakī are seen together, a great reconciliation festival; tradition says she keeps her distance because of his other marriages.

🫓 Tulukka Nācciyār

A Muslim princess of Delhi, the story goes, fell in love with the captured processional image during the invasions and followed it south. She is honoured with a shrine, and the Lord is offered roti and butter for her.

🌺 Āṇḍāḷ

The poet-saint who married Raṅganātha in legend, merging into him at Śrīraṅgam; her Tiruppāvai is sung here every Mārgaḻi. See Āṇḍāḷ’s Tiruppāvai.

ZAPPY: A Muslim princess in the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Honoured every day, Zappy. Love crossed every line in this story.

Love across every line.

THE ĀḺVĀRS AND THE PRABANDHAM

First of the 108

The Āḻvārs. Twelve Tamil poet-saints (sixth to ninth centuries) whose songs of love for Viṣṇu were gathered as the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham, “the four thousand divine verses”.

The first Divya Deśam. Śrīraṅgam is the first of the 108 shrines praised in those songs, and the most praised of all: eleven of the twelve Āḻvārs sang of it.

Araiyar Sevai. Hereditary performers called Araiyars still sing and enact the Prabandham in the temple, with gesture and music, especially during Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī.

Kamban. Tradition holds that Kamban’s Tamil Rāmāyaṇa was first presented and approved in this temple, in a hall still pointed out.

ZAPPY: Tamil songs in the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Sung alongside the Vedas, Zappy. Śrīvaiṣṇavas call the Prabandham the Tamil Veda.

The Tamil Veda sung here.

RĀMĀNUJA AT ŚRĪRAṄGAM

The teacher who never left

The story

Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition; Wikipedia, “Ranganathaswamy Temple, Srirangam”.

Rāmānuja (traditionally 1017–1137), the great teacher of Viśiṣṭādvaita, came to Śrīraṅgam as a young sannyāsī and became the head of its worship.
He reorganised the temple’s administration and rituals, assigning duties to many communities and setting the order of daily worship that, in large part, continues.
During persecution by a Cōḻa king, he spent years in Karnataka under Hoysaḷa protection, much of it at Mēlukōṭe; see Mēlukōṭe. He returned to Śrīraṅgam in his last years.
He died here, and his body was preserved and enshrined within the temple, in a seated posture; devotees call it Tānāna Tirumēni, “the body that remained by itself”.
From here his teaching spread across India: Śrīraṅgam is the heart of the Śrīvaiṣṇava world.

ZAPPY: His body is still in the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Seated in his own shrine, Zappy, tended for nine centuries. Devotees see him as still present.

The teacher who stayed.

LOSS AND RETURN

1310 to 1371

The story

Wikipedia, “Ranganathaswamy Temple, Srirangam”; temple chronicle (Kōil Oḻugu).

In 1310–11, the army of Malik Kafur raided the south and plundered the temple.
In 1323 a second invasion, under the Tughluq forces, approached. Devotees fled with Namperumāḷ, the processional image, and kept it hidden for decades, moving through the south; accounts say it rested for a time near Tirumala. Many defenders and servants of the temple died.
The scholar Vedānta Deśika, tradition says, hid the manuscripts of a great commentary among corpses to save it.
In 1371, the Vijayanagara prince Kumāra Kampaṇa, with the general Gopaṇārya, defeated the Madurai sultanate and reconsecrated the temple; the image came home.
The years of exile are remembered in the temple’s rituals and in the honour paid to Tulukka Nācciyār.

ZAPPY: The image was away for decades?

PROF. BOLT: Carried and hidden by devotees, Zappy, until it could come home. Some of them never did.

Carried away, brought home.

THE GREAT HALLS

Pillars, horses and the Garuḍa

🏛️ Thousand-Pillared Hall

A vast Vijayanagara-period hall of granite pillars, used for festivals including Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī’s night of the Paramapada.

🐎 Śeṣarāya Maṇḍapam

A Nāyaka-period hall whose pillars are carved as rearing horses with riders hunting, among the finest stone carving of South India.

🦅 Garuḍa Maṇḍapam

A free-standing giant Garuḍa faces the sanctum, hands folded.

🏅 Care for heritage

In 2017 the temple received a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Award of Merit for its conservation work.

ZAPPY: Horses carved from pillars?

PROF. BOLT: Rearing up out of the stone, Zappy, three hundred years ago and still galloping.

Stone that gallops.

VAIKUṆṬHA EKĀDAŚĪ

The gate of heaven opens

Twenty days. In Mārgaḻi (December–January) Śrīraṅgam keeps its greatest festival: ten days called Pagal Pattu (the day ten) and ten called Rā Pattu (the night ten), with the Divya Prabandham sung in full.

The gate. On the dawn of Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī, the first of the Rā Pattu, Namperumāḷ passes through the Paramapada Vāsal, the “gate of heaven”, followed by hundreds of thousands of devotees.

The Mohinī form. On the evening before, Namperumāḷ appears as Mohinī, the enchantress.

Araiyars. Every day of the festival the Araiyars sing and enact the Āḻvārs’ verses before the Lord.

ZAPPY: Everyone walks through heaven’s gate?

PROF. BOLT: Behind the Lord, Zappy. It’s the temple’s way of saying the door is open to all.

The gate opens for all.

WHAT ŚRĪRAṄGAM TEACHES

Rest, return and belonging

God at rest. The reclining Lord suggests a God who need not strive; it is we who come and go. (Interpretation.)

Return. Images carried away, a teacher in exile, a princess who followed: the temple’s stories are about coming home.

A home for many. Āḻvārs of every background, Tamil and Sanskrit, a Muslim princess, Kannada kings: all are woven into its worship.

Patience. A tower finished after centuries; a festival kept for a thousand years. Some things are worth waiting for.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Raṅganātha?

PROF. BOLT: Come home to someone you’ve been away from, Zappy. That’s the story Śrīraṅgam tells.

Every story comes home.
