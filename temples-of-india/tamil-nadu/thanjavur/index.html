TEMPLES OF TAMIL NADU · PART 3 OF 10 · THE BIG TEMPLE

Thanjavur Bṛhadīśvara

Rājarāja’s temple of 1010 · explained and illustrated

The Big Temple, Periya Kōvil

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt visit the Big Temple of the Cholas: the king who built it, the great liṅga and tower, the words on its walls, its hidden paintings, worship today and how to visit.

WHERE WE ARE

In the town of Thanjavur, on the Kāverī delta, stands the temple Tamils simply call Periya Kōvil, the Big Temple. Its builder, the Chola king Rājarāja I, called it Rājarājeśvaram.

Consecrated in 1010, it was the largest temple of its time in India, and its tower over the sanctum, about 66 m high, still dominates the town.

It is a UNESCO World Heritage site, a monument of the Chola empire, and above all a living temple of Śiva where worship has gone on for a thousand years.

ZAPPY: Is it a museum or a temple?

PROF. BOLT: Both. Visitors come for the history, devotees come for darśana of the great liṅga. The two crowds walk the same courtyard.

ZAPPY: Why is it called the Big Temple?

PROF. BOLT: In Tamil, Periya Kōvil: the Big Temple. For a thousand years nothing in the region stood taller.

THE KING WHO BUILT IT

Rājarāja and his temple

The story

The king. Rājarāja I ruled the Chola kingdom from 985. In a reign of conquests across south India and Sri Lanka, he chose to build a temple to Śiva on a scale never seen before.
The stone. It is built of granite brought from quarries some distance away, laid course by course without mortar. A well-known tradition speaks of a long earth ramp for raising the top stone; treat it as tradition.
The day. The temple’s inscriptions record the gift of the gilded copper finial on the 275th day of his 25th regnal year, in 1010.
The records. Rājarāja had his gifts, and those of his family and officers, cut into the walls: gold, jewels, lamps, land, and the names of hundreds of temple staff.
A thousand years. Worship has gone on ever since. In 2010 the temple’s millennium was celebrated in Thanjavur.

ZAPPY: Did he build it alone?

PROF. BOLT: Of course not. The inscriptions name his chief architect and many officers, and thousands of workers built it. But the vision was the king’s.

Rājarāja’s temple: granite, 66 m, consecrated 1010.

THE GREAT LORD

Bṛhadīśvara and Bṛhannāyakī

Bṛhadīśvara. The deity is Śiva as Bṛhadīśvara, the “great lord”, called in the inscriptions Rājarājeśvaram Uḍaiyār or Peruvuḍaiyār. He is worshipped as a liṅga.

The liṅga. The liṅga in the sanctum is one of the largest in any temple, several metres tall. The priests climb steps to perform its abhiṣekam.

Two storeys of sanctum. The sanctum is surrounded by a two-storeyed passage, an inner ambulatory; its walls carry the famous Chola paintings and dance sculptures.

The goddess. Bṛhannāyakī, the goddess, has her own shrine in the court, a later addition to Rājarāja’s plan, like several other shrines (see “Layers”).

Darśana. Devotees see the liṅga from the hall before the sanctum. Abhiṣekam is performed on special days, such as Pradoṣam, with milk, curd, sandal and more.

ZAPPY: How big is the liṅga?

PROF. BOLT: Very big: you look up at it. Sources give different heights, so we won’t print a number. Go and feel the scale yourself.

WALKING IN

From the gates to the sanctum

Two gateways. You enter from the east through two gopurams on the axis: the outer Keralāntakan gateway and the inner Rājarājan gateway, both named in Rājarāja’s honour. Both are lower than the vimāna: here the god’s tower still rules.

The court. Inside is a vast rectangular court lined with a two-storeyed colonnade. Along it stand small shrines and a long row of liṅgas.

Nandi. On the axis, facing the sanctum, sits Nandi in his pavilion (next page).

Up the steps. Steps lead up into a chain of halls, then the vestibule and the sanctum. The halls are often crowded at pūjā times.

Round the court. Walk round clockwise: the shrines of the goddess, of Murugan and of the siddha Karuvūrār, and the vimāna’s carved walls on every side.

ZAPPY: Is there a right time to visit?

PROF. BOLT: Early morning or late afternoon: cooler stone, softer light on the tower, and the evening lamps.

THE TOWER

Dakṣiṇa Meru

Its name. The inscriptions call the vimāna Dakṣiṇa Meru, the “Southern Meru”, after the cosmic mountain at the centre of the world.

Thirteen storeys. Above a tall two-storeyed base, the tower rises in thirteen storeys, each edged with miniature shrines, to a dome-shaped crown stone.

Hollow. Above the sanctum the tower is a hollow shaft; the weight is carried down through thick walls. That is how such a height was possible in dry granite.

The crown. The single crown stone at the top is very large. A popular figure is about 80 tonnes; treat it as an estimate.

A myth. A popular claim says the tower’s shadow never falls on the ground. It does, like every tower’s; but the story shows how amazed people have always been.

ZAPPY: Why is the tower straight-sided, not curved?

PROF. BOLT: That was the style of Rājarāja’s time. His son’s temple at Gangaikondacholapuram has a gently curving tower. Compare them on page “Sister temples”.

THE NANDI

Śiva’s bull on the axis

On the axis. In the court, facing the sanctum, Nandi, the bull, sits in an open pillared pavilion. Devotees look at the liṅga across him.

A later Nandi. The large Nandi you see and its pavilion belong to the Nāyaka period, centuries after Rājarāja. The original Chola Nandi is kept elsewhere in the court.

A monolith. The Nandi is carved from a single stone and is one of the largest in India.

Painted ceiling. Look up inside the pavilion: its ceiling is painted with Nāyaka-period designs.

Pradoṣam. On Pradoṣam evenings Nandi receives abhiṣekam with milk and other offerings, and large crowds gather around the pavilion.

ZAPPY: Why does Nandi always face Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: He is Śiva’s mount and devotee, always gazing at his lord. Many devotees whisper their wishes into his ear.

THE WORDS ON THE WALLS

Lamps, gold and four hundred dancers

A library in stone. The base of the vimāna and the walls around carry long inscriptions in Tamil and Sanskrit, many in Rājarāja’s own name. They are among the richest temple records anywhere.

Gold and jewels. They list ornaments given to the deities and bronzes, with weights and stones, as carefully as a jeweller’s ledger.

Perpetual lamps. They record lamp endowments: each perpetual lamp funded by 96 ewes, or 48 cows, or 16 she-buffaloes, given to shepherds who supplied ghee daily.

People. One long inscription lists about four hundred dancing women brought from other temples and many male servants: musicians, drummers, lamp-lighters, tailors, goldsmiths, accountants, each with a share of land.

Read more. How to Read a Hindu Temple, Part 3, explains how to read such inscriptions.

ZAPPY: Can I read them?

PROF. BOLT: Unless you read old Tamil and Grantha, not directly. But translations were published long ago, in the series South Indian Inscriptions.

THE HIDDEN PAINTINGS

Chola murals under a later layer

In the dark passage. The walls of the passage round the sanctum were painted around 1010. About six centuries later, in the Nāyaka period, new paintings were laid over them.

Found in 1931. In 1931 S. K. Govindasami of Annamalai University noticed the older Chola paintings showing through and identified them.

What they show. Śiva as Tripurāntaka destroying the three flying cities; celestial dancers; the saint Sundarar and the Chēra king journeying to Kailāsa; and a scene widely identified as Rājarāja with his teacher.

Two layers. Conservators have carefully separated the layers in places so both can be studied. The passage is usually closed to visitors to protect the paintings.

Read more. How to Read a Hindu Temple, Part 9, tells the story of these paintings in more detail.

ZAPPY: Why paint over such fine paintings?

PROF. BOLT: The later rulers renewed the temple in the style of their day. They did not know the Chola paintings would one day be so treasured.

THE DANCE POSES

Karaṇas carved in stone

Dance in the shrine. The Nāṭyaśāstra, the ancient text on drama and dance, describes 108 karaṇas, basic units of dance movement.

Carved poses. On the walls of the upper floor of the passage round the sanctum, many of these karaṇas are carved as panels of Śiva or a dancer in each pose.

Unfinished. The series was not completed; some panels were left blank. It is one of the earliest sculptural records of the karaṇas.

A dancing temple. With four hundred dancers recorded in the inscriptions and the karaṇas carved in stone, the Big Temple was a great centre of dance.

Today. Bharatanāṭyam dancers perform in the temple courtyard on festival occasions, and the karaṇas are studied by dancers and scholars.

ZAPPY: Can visitors see the karaṇas?

PROF. BOLT: The upper floor is not usually open to the public. Photographs in books and exhibitions show them well.

LAYERS OVER A THOUSAND YEARS

What Rājarāja built and what came later

Chola core. The vimāna, the halls, the inner gateway and the enclosure were Rājarāja’s. The outer gateway also belongs to his time.

Goddess. The shrine of Bṛhannāyakī was added later, as in many Chola temples where the goddess gained her own shrine after the original building.

Nāyaka additions. In the 16th–17th centuries the Nāyakas of Thanjavur added the Nandi and its pavilion and the richly carved Murugan shrine, and repainted the passage.

Maratha period. The Maratha rulers of Thanjavur, from the late 17th century, renovated shrines and added paintings and records of their own.

Read it. So the courtyard is a meeting of three dynasties: Chola, Nāyaka and Maratha. How to Read a Hindu Temple, Part 11, shows how to tell them apart.

ZAPPY: And the Karuvūrār shrine?

PROF. BOLT: It honours Karuvūrār, a siddha remembered in tradition as Rājarāja’s guru, connected with the installation of the liṅga.

WORSHIP AND FESTIVALS

A living temple

Daily. The temple follows the Śaiva Āgamas, with pūjās through the day from early morning to night.

Pradoṣam. On Pradoṣam evenings, twice a month, Nandi and the liṅga receive abhiṣekam and crowds fill the court.

Sadaya Vizhā. Every year, in the Tamil month of Aippasi, the town celebrates Rājarāja’s birth star, Sadayam, with processions, music and dance in the king’s honour.

Śivarātri and more. Mahāśivarātri, Ārudrā Darśanam and Chittirai festivals bring their own rituals and processions.

Music and dance. Concerts and Bharatanāṭyam performances are held in the courtyard on festival occasions, continuing the temple’s old role.

ZAPPY: Do people really celebrate a king’s birthday in a temple?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, Sadaya Vizhā is a much-loved festival. Rājarāja is remembered as a devotee of Śiva as much as a ruler.

SISTER TEMPLES

Gangaikondacholapuram and Dārāsuram

Three together. UNESCO lists the Big Temple with two later Chola temples as the “Great Living Chola Temples”.

Gangaikondacholapuram. Rājarāja’s son Rājendra I built a new capital and temple, finished around 1035. Its vimāna, about 51 m, has gently curving sides.

Dārāsuram. The Airāvateśvara temple near Kumbakonam, built in the 12th century by Rājarāja II, is smaller but extremely richly carved; its front hall is built as a chariot.

A day trip. The three can be seen in a long day from Thanjavur or Kumbakonam (Part 10 of this series).

Compare. Stand before each tower and compare: straight or curved, tall or small, plain or carved. You will see a dynasty’s taste change over a century.

ZAPPY: Which is the most beautiful?

PROF. BOLT: Many people fall for Gangaikondacholapuram’s curve or Dārāsuram’s carving. Thanjavur wins on sheer power.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Thanjavur is well connected by rail and road; Tiruchirappalli has the nearest major airport.
Timings. The temple is open from early morning to night, closing for some hours in the afternoon. Check the current timings.
Dress and footwear. Modest clothes; leave footwear at the counter by the gate. The stone can be very hot at midday.
Photography. Allowed in the courtyard; not inside the sanctum.
Nearby. The Thanjavur Palace with its art gallery and the Sarasvatī Mahal library; Gangaikondacholapuram and Dārāsuram on a day trip.

ZAPPY: How long should I stay?

PROF. BOLT: At least two hours, ideally a morning and an evening. The tower changes colour with the light.
