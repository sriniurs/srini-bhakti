TEMPLES OF TAMIL NADU · PART 9 OF 10 · BY THE SEA

Tiruchendur Murugan

The shrine of victory · explained and illustrated

Where the waves touch the temple

HOW THIS WORKS: Zappy and Prof. Bolt go to the shore where Murugan defeated the demon Sūrapadman, watch the battle re-enacted on the beach at Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī, bathe in the sea and drink from a sweet well beside it.

WHERE WE ARE

On the coast of the Gulf of Mannar, south of Thoothukudi, stands the only one of Murugan’s six abodes that is not on a hill: Tiruchendur, built right beside the sea.

Here, the story says, Murugan camped before and after his battle with the demon Sūrapadman, and here he worshipped his father Śiva after the victory.

Every year, at Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī, the battle is re-enacted on the beach before vast crowds. It is one of the great festivals of Tamil Nadu.

ZAPPY: A temple on the beach?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, with the waves close enough to hear from the sanctum. Pilgrims bathe in the sea before darśana.

ZAPPY: Why is it the abode of victory?

PROF. BOLT: Because it is where Murugan rested with his army and celebrated his victory over Sūrapadman. Jaya means victory.

THE WAR WITH SŪRAPADMAN

The story of Tiruchendur

The story

The demon. The asura Sūrapadman, made almost invincible by boons, oppressed the gods. Only a son of Śiva could defeat him.
Murugan’s birth. Murugan was born from sparks of Śiva’s third eye and raised by the six Kṛttikās; he became the commander of the gods’ army.
The battle. The Kandha Purāṇam tells of a long war ending at the sea. Murugan hurled his spear, the vēl, given by his mother Pārvatī.
Split in two. Sūrapadman took the form of a great mango tree in the sea. The spear split it in two: one half became Murugan’s peacock mount, the other the rooster on his banner.
Victory and worship. After the victory Murugan worshipped Śiva at Tiruchendur. So in the sanctum he is shown in worship, with Śiva beside him.

ZAPPY: The demon became his vehicle?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. In the story, defeat is also grace: the enemy is not destroyed but transformed and kept close.

The vēl split Sūrapadman: peacock and rooster.

KANDHA ṢAṢṬHĪ

The battle on the beach

Six days. Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī is a six-day festival in the Tamil month of Aippasi (October–November), ending on the sixth day of the bright fortnight. Many devotees fast for all six days.

Sūrasaṃhāram. On the sixth day, in the evening, the battle is re-enacted on the beach: the festival image of Murugan faces effigies of Sūrapadman, who changes form again and again, and defeats him each time.

The crowd. Hundreds of thousands gather on the sand. The cry of “Vetrivēl Muruganukku arōgarā!” rises as each form falls.

The wedding. The next day, Murugan’s wedding to Deivānai is celebrated, the Tirukkalyāṇam.

Everywhere. Sūrasaṃhāram is enacted at Murugan temples across Tamil Nadu and abroad, but Tiruchendur’s by the sea is the most famous.

ZAPPY: Why does the demon keep changing form?

PROF. BOLT: In the story Sūrapadman uses his powers to escape. Each new form is defeated. The repetition is the drama.

THE SEA AND THE SWEET WELL

Bathing before darśana

The sea bath. Most pilgrims first bathe in the sea in front of the temple, then wash off the salt before darśana.

Nāḻikkiṇaṟu. Near the shore is a small well, the Nāḻikkiṇaṟu, whose water is fresh, though it lies just steps from the sea. Pilgrims bathe in its water after the sea.

The story. Temple tradition says Murugan made the well with his spear to give his army fresh water.

Care at the shore. The sea here can be rough. Bathe where others do, follow the lifeguards and police, and keep children close.

Waves and temple. In recent years the sea has eroded parts of the shore; protective work goes on. The temple is closer to the waves than most visitors expect.

ZAPPY: How can a well by the sea be sweet?

PROF. BOLT: Fresh groundwater can sit above salt water near the shore. To pilgrims it is also Murugan’s gift.

THE SANCTUM

Murugan in worship

Bālasubrahmaṇya. In the sanctum Murugan stands as a young god, holding a flower and a rosary, in an attitude of worship. He is sometimes called Senthil Āṇḍavar, Senthil Nāthan.

Śiva beside him. Beside him is a liṅga: Murugan worshipping his father after victory. Devotees see both together.

Facing east. The sanctum faces east, towards the sea and the sunrise.

Other shrines. The temple has shrines of Valli and Deivānai, Murugan’s two consorts, of Naṭarāja, of Viṣṇu and of others, as well as a cave shrine linked with Murugan’s tapas.

Festival images. The festival image, Shanmukha with six faces, rides in processions on vehicles and in the chariot.

ZAPPY: Two consorts?

PROF. BOLT: Deivānai, daughter of Indra, given in marriage after the victory; and Valli, a hunter’s daughter, won by love. Together they show the god’s grace to heaven and earth.

THE WEST GOPURAM

A gateway facing the land

An unusual position. The temple’s tall rājagopuram stands on the west, landward side, not in front of the sanctum’s east-facing door, which looks towards the sea.

Nine storeys. It rises in nine storeys and was completed in the 17th century, by tradition through the efforts of a devotee, Desikamoorthi Swamigal.

Most pilgrims. Most pilgrims arriving from the town see this gopuram first; the beach side has its own entrances.

Tall on a flat shore. On the flat coast, the gopuram is visible from far away, a landmark for fishermen and travellers.

Painted. Like other Tamil gopurams, it is covered in painted stucco figures, renewed at the temple’s kumbhābhiṣekams.

ZAPPY: Why not build the gate on the sea side?

PROF. BOLT: There is too little room between the temple and the waves, and the sea is the god’s own front door.

STORIES OF THE TEMPLE

Poets, raiders and devotees

In Sangam poetry. Tiruchendur, called Tiruchīralaivāy, “the sacred shore of waves”, is praised in the old poem Tirumurukāṟṟuppaḍai as the second abode.

Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī Kavacam. The famous protective hymn Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī Kavacam, by Dēvarāya Swāmigaḷ, recited daily in many Tamil homes, is linked with Tiruchendur.

The Dutch story. A well-known local account says that in the 17th century Dutch sailors carried off the image, ran into a storm, threw it into the sea, and it was later recovered and restored. Treat it as tradition; details vary.

Kings and devotees. Pāṇḍya, Nāyaka and later patrons built and enlarged the temple, and local devotees have rebuilt parts over the centuries.

Today. The temple is administered by the Tamil Nadu HR&CE Department and is one of its busiest.

ZAPPY: Is the Dutch story true?

PROF. BOLT: It is told widely and is part of the temple’s memory. Historians discuss the details. We give it as tradition.

THE FESTIVAL YEAR

More than Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī

Māsi and Āvaṇi. Two twelve-day festivals in the Tamil months of Māsi (February–March) and Āvaṇi (August–September) bring processions on vehicles and the chariot.

Vaikāsi Viśākham. Murugan’s birth star in Vaikāsi (May–June) is celebrated with special worship.

Thaipusam and Panguni Uttiram. As at Palani, these days draw pilgrims, some carrying kāvaḍis.

Every month. The Kṛttikai star and Ṣaṣṭhī days each month are special for Murugan devotees.

Pilgrims on foot. Pilgrims walk from towns across the south to Tiruchendur, especially before Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī.

ZAPPY: Which is the best time to go?

PROF. BOLT: For quiet darśana, an ordinary weekday morning. For the experience of a lifetime, Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī, with patience for the crowds.

VALLI’S CAVE AND THE SHORE

Small places around the temple

Valli’s cave. Near the temple is a small cave associated with Valli, Murugan’s consort, which pilgrims visit.

Shore shrines. Small shrines and maṇḍapams line the approach to the beach.

Red sand dunes. The coastal dunes and red sand hills (teri) near Tiruchendur are part of the landscape of this area.

Fishermen. Fishing communities of the coast have their own deep ties to the temple and its festivals.

Walk. Walk along the shore at sunrise: the temple, the sea and the light make one of the most beautiful temple scenes in India.

ZAPPY: Is the cave old?

PROF. BOLT: Its age is not well recorded. Its importance is in the story and in the pilgrims who visit it.

HOW TO VISIT

Practical notes
Getting there. Tiruchendur has a railway station; it is about 40 km from Thoothukudi and 60 km from Tirunelveli. Thoothukudi has the nearest airport.
Timings. The temple opens very early and closes late, with closures for special rituals. Check timings for festival days.
Sea bath. Bathe only in the marked area; follow the lifeguards. Carry a change of clothes.
Darśana. Free and paid queues. Expect very long waits at Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī.
Dress. Traditional or modest dress; men may be asked to remove shirts in the inner shrine, as in some Tamil temples. Follow the boards.

ZAPPY: Where should I stay for Kandha Ṣaṣṭhī?

PROF. BOLT: Book well ahead, or stay in Thoothukudi or Tirunelveli and travel in. The town fills completely.
