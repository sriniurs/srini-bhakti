Durgā Namaskāra Pūjā

The Goddess Worshipped in a Lamp · explained and illustrated

The Mother invited into the lamp

HOW THIS WORKS: The rite step by step, the stories behind the name Durgā, the Vedic Durgā Sūkta to the fire-coloured Goddess, the maṇḍala and the lamp, and how the coast of Karnataka keeps it.

WHERE WE ARE

On the coast of Karnataka, as night falls in Navarātri, a family clears a floor, draws a lotus in red, yellow and white powders, and sets a tall brass lamp at its centre. The priest lights it and calls the Mother into the flame. For the next hours she is worshipped there, in the light itself. This is Durgā Namaskāra Pūjā.

This guide takes it apart piece by piece: what the rite is and why it is called namaskāra; how the Goddess came to be called Durgā; the Vedic Durgā Sūkta that hails her as the colour of fire; the maṇḍala, the lamp and the letters of mātṛkā-nyāsa; the order of worship; when it is done; and how Udupi, Kaṭīl and Mysuru keep it.

Her battles in full are in our Devī Māhātmya series; the nine nights in Navarātri; the island Goddess of the coast in Kaṭīl Durgāparameśvarī.

With the blessings of the Devī,

ZAPPY: Is this the same as the Durgā Pūjā of Bengal?

PROF. BOLT: Same Goddess, different rite. Bengal shapes her in clay and worships the image for days. Here there is no image at all: a lotus drawn on the floor and a flame at its heart.

WHAT IS DURGĀ NAMASKĀRA?

The Mother invited into a lamp

The rite. Durgā Namaskāra Pūjā is a worship of the Goddess in a lamp. The floor is cleaned, a maṇḍala is drawn in coloured powders, and a tall standing lamp is set at its centre and lit. The priest then invokes Durgā into the flame, and from that moment the lamp is treated as the Goddess herself.

Three in one. She is called in as Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī, the three forms praised in the three movements of the Devī Māhātmya. The guardians of the eight directions are worshipped around the edge of the maṇḍala, as the court around a queen.

Built of sound. The central act is mātṛkā-nyāsa: the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet are placed, one by one, on the parts of her form. The Goddess in the lamp is invoked, limb by limb, out of the sounds of speech. (This is described here, not given as a practice; it is done by a trained priest.)

The salutations. Then come the offerings and the long recitations: the Vedic Durgā Sūkta, the Devī Māhātmya, and in many homes the Lalitā Sahasranāma. Each offering ends in namaḥ, a bow. The name says plainly what the rite is: a pūjā of salutations, namaskāra, to Durgā in the lamp.

On the coast. In Udupi and Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa it is a familiar household rite. A family may hold a Gaṇahoma in the morning, a Satyanārāyaṇa Pūjā in the afternoon and close the day with Durgā Namaskāra at night; maṭhas hold it in Navarātri.

ZAPPY: Why a flame and not a picture of her?

PROF. BOLT: A picture shows one form. A flame shows none and all of them at once: it moves, it lights everything around it, and you cannot hold it. Many say that is the nearest the eye can get to Śakti.

No image: the flame is the Goddess.

THE NAME

Durgā · dīpa · namaskāra

Durgā. The usual explanation of the word is dur-gā, “hard to go to, hard to approach”: a fortress (durga) that no enemy can enter. The Goddess is that fortress for those who take refuge in her, and impassable for evil. The Devī Māhātmya adds a story: she earned the name by slaying the asura Durgama (told on its own page).

Dīpa. Many priests and families say Durgā Dīpa Namaskāra or Durgādīpa Namaskāra, putting the lamp into the name. The Goddess worshipped is “Dīpa Durgā”, Durgā in the lamp.

Namaskāra. Namas is the bow, the giving up of “I” before the deity; namaskāra is the act of bowing. One pūjā-service account says it directly: the salutation of Dīpa Durgā with these mantras is what is called Durgā Dīpa Namaskāra.

Other names. In some places on the coast and in Kerala the same kind of lamp-and-maṇḍala worship of the Devī is called Bhagavatī Sevā or Bhagavatī Pūjā. Traditions differ on how far these are one rite or cousins.

Not Durgā Pūjā. The name sounds close to Bengal’s Durgā Pūjā, but the two are different: no clay image, no immersion; a lamp, a drawn lotus and a night of salutations.

ZAPPY: So the whole name means “bowing to Durgā in the lamp”?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly that. Three words, and each is a thing you can see on the floor: the fortress-Mother, the flame, and the people bowing.

Durgā, in the lamp, saluted.

STORY · HOW SHE BECAME DURGĀ

The demon Durgama, the hundred-eyed Mother and the greens

The story

Foretold by the Goddess herself in the Devī Māhātmya (11.49–50), and told in full in the Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa (book 7, chapter 28).

The asura Durgama, “the hard to reach”, struck at the gods’ root: he made the Vedas vanish. The sages forgot them, the sacrifices stopped, and with them went the gods’ strength and the rain.
A drought of a hundred years followed. Rivers dried, fields cracked, people and animals starved.
The sages called on the Devī. She came with a hundred eyes to see them all at once, and wept at their misery; her tears ran as rivers. She is Śatākṣī, the hundred-eyed.
From her own body she brought out greens, fruits and roots and fed the world till the rains came. She is Śākambharī, “she who bears the greens”.
Durgama came with his army; she slew him and gave the Vedas back. She had foretold it in the Devī Māhātmya: she would slay the asura Durgama, and be famed as Durgā Devī.

ZAPPY: So “Durgā” means the one who killed Durgama?

PROF. BOLT: That is her own reading. The word gives another: durgā, “hard to approach”, a fortress no evil enters. Together: what was impassable for us, she makes passable.

She ended the drought and took the name of what she defeated.

STORY · THE LIGHT OF ALL THE GODS

How Durgā was born, and Mahiṣāsura

The story

From the Devī Māhātmya, chapters 2–3, the middle movement of the text recited at Durgā Namaskāra.

The buffalo-demon Mahiṣa defeated the gods in a hundred-year war and took heaven for himself.
The gods went to Viṣṇu and Śiva. As they told their story, a great light of anger poured out of every god, and the lights came together into one blazing form: the Devī.
Each god gave her his weapon: Śiva the trident, Viṣṇu the discus, Varuṇa the conch, Indra the thunderbolt; the mountain Himavān gave her the lion.
She rode into Mahiṣa’s armies. He changed shape, buffalo, lion, man with a sword, elephant, and back to buffalo; she saw through every form.
She pinned him under her foot and struck him down with her trident and sword: she is Mahiṣāsuramardinī. Read it in full in Devī Māhātmya, Part 8.

ZAPPY: She was made of all the gods’ light?

PROF. BOLT: That is why a lamp suits her so well. A flame lit from many flames is still one flame.

All the gods’ light in one flame.

THREE MOTHERS IN ONE LAMP

Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī, Mahāsarasvatī

Why three. At Durgā Namaskāra the Goddess in the lamp is invoked in three forms: Mahākālī, Mahālakṣmī and Mahāsarasvatī. They are the presiding Goddesses of the three movements (caritas) of the Devī Māhātmya.

Mahākālī, the first movement. As Yoganidrā, the sleep of Viṣṇu, she releases him to slay the demons Madhu and Kaiṭabha who threatened Brahmā. The dark power of the cosmic night. (Part 6)

Mahālakṣmī, the middle movement. Born of the gods’ light, she slays Mahiṣāsura. The power of action and splendour. (Parts 7 and 8)

Mahāsarasvatī, the last movement. As Kauśikī, Kālī and Cāmuṇḍā she destroys Śumbha and Niśumbha, Caṇḍa, Muṇḍa and Raktabīja. The power of clear knowing. (Parts 12–15)

One flame. The tradition is firm that they are not three goddesses but one Devī seen three ways, as dark, as red and as white. Many read them as the three qualities of nature, tamas, rajas and sattva, all held in the Mother. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: Three names, one lamp?

PROF. BOLT: One flame. Look at it at night and it is dark around the wick, red in the middle, white at the tip. The lamp itself teaches the doctrine.

Dark, red, white: one Mother.

THE MAṆḌALA

A seat for the Goddess, drawn on the floor

A seat drawn on the floor. Before anything else the ground is cleaned, traditionally with a wash that includes cow dung, and a maṇḍala is drawn in coloured powders: raṅgavalli, the same art as the rangoli at every Karnataka doorstep, raised here to a sacred plan. Many call it simply the padma, the lotus.

Its parts. A common form has an outer square with a gate on each side for the four directions, a circle within it, an eight-petalled lotus, and at the heart a six-pointed star or a Devī yantra. Each family of priests has its own design, colours and proportions; there is no single correct drawing.

The centre. At the very centre, the point called the bindu, stands the big lamp. Fresh red and maroon flowers are laid around it as her seat (āsana); smaller lamps are often set on the rim.

The colours. Red, yellow, white, green and black powders are usual. Red, the Devī’s own colour, usually dominates.

What it means. Many teachers read a maṇḍala as a map of the whole world folded to a point: the square of the earth, the lotus of unfolding life, the star where Śiva and Śakti meet, and the Goddess at the still centre. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: All that drawing, and it gets swept up afterwards?

PROF. BOLT: That is part of the lesson. The seat is made with care, used with love and not clung to. What stays is what happened in you.

The world drawn as a lotus, the Goddess at its centre.

THE LAMP

The tall standing lamp at the centre

A standing lamp. The lamp at the centre is a tall brass or silver lamp on a stem, the kind kept in every coastal and Kannada home for festivals: in Kannada a kālu-dīpa, a footed lamp. A Kannada explainer describes exactly this: a kālu-dīpa set at the centre of the maṇḍala and decorated with flowers around it.

Wicks and oil. It is filled with ghee or sesame oil and lit with one or several wicks; five-wicked lamps are common. It must burn steadily through the whole worship, so someone keeps watch to top it up.

From lamp to Goddess. Before the invocation it is a lamp. After the āvāhana (invitation), the prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā (the gift of life) and the nyāsa, it is treated as Durgā herself: bathed with flowers, dressed, offered food, waved with lights and bowed to.

Why a lamp at all. Light is the oldest image of the divine in Indian worship; the Durgā Sūkta calls her fire-coloured and blazing. A lamp also carries no single face, so all three forms can live in it at once. (Interpretation.)

Other lamps. Smaller lamps often ring the maṇḍala, and in Kaṭīl and other coastal temples a related night worship, raṅgapūjā, uses rows of lamps on drawn patterns. See Kaṭīl Durgāparameśvarī.

ZAPPY: What if the lamp goes out in the middle?

PROF. BOLT: That is why someone watches it the whole night. Most priests say: relight it quietly with a prayer and carry on. Fear is not the point of this worship; care is.

A household lamp becomes the Mother.

WHAT IS NEEDED

The things laid out before the worship

A typical list, gathered from priests’ and pūjā-service lists in Karnataka. Families and priests differ; the priest usually brings the ritual items.

🪔 The lamp

The tall standing lamp, ghee or sesame oil, cotton wicks; small lamps for the rim.

🎨 The maṇḍala

Coloured powders (red, yellow, white, green, black) for the raṅgavalli; a cleaned floor.

🌺 Flowers and leaves

Fresh red and maroon flowers for her seat; leaves, tulasī and garlands.

🟡 Turmeric, kuṅkuma, akṣata

Turmeric, kuṅkuma (vermilion), unbroken rice, sandal paste and perfume.

🥥 Kalaśa and coconut

A water pot with mango leaves and a coconut; betel leaves and nuts.

🍯 Naivedya

Fruits, sweets, the five sweet things (pañcakhādya), the household’s cooked offering.

🔥 Incense and camphor

Incense sticks, dhūpa, camphor for the closing ārati.

🔔 Bell and plates

A bell, ārati plate, small vessels for water and the offerings.

ZAPPY: That is a long list for a lamp.

PROF. BOLT: Most of it is simply what you would put before an honoured guest. Families who cannot gather everything keep the lamp, the flowers and the sincerity.

Everything you would offer an honoured guest.

THE GUARDIANS AROUND HER

Gaṇapati, the kalaśa and the eight directions

First, the intention. The worship opens like every formal pūjā: ācamana (sips of water), prāṇāyāma, and the saṅkalpa, the statement of who is worshipping, where, when, and for what. On the coast the family head usually makes it with the priest.

Gaṇapati and the kalaśa. Gaṇapati is worshipped first so that the rite runs without obstacles, and a water pot (kalaśa) is set up and worshipped as the seat of the sacred waters. See our simple home pūjā for what each of these steps means.

The court of the Goddess. Then the maṇḍala itself is honoured. Around its edge the aṣṭa dikpālas, the guardians of the eight directions, are invoked: Indra in the east, Agni in the south-east, Yama in the south, Nirṛti in the south-west, Varuṇa in the west, Vāyu in the north-west, Kubera in the north and Īśāna in the north-east.

Why the guardians. They mark out the space as a whole world in small, closed on every side, before the queen of that world is invited to its centre. (Interpretation.)

Order varies. Which deities are worshipped around the maṇḍala, and in what order, differs by priest and paddhati (ritual manual).

ZAPPY: Even Yama gets invited?

PROF. BOLT: Every direction, and every power that keeps it. Nothing in her world is left outside her protection, not even death.

A whole world drawn, guarded on all sides.

MĀTṚKĀ-NYĀSA

The Goddess built out of letters

The heart of the rite. Every account of Durgā Namaskāra names one act above the others: mātṛkā-nyāsa. Durgā is invoked in the big lamp by placing the letters of the alphabet, one by one, on the parts of her form, so that every limb is worshipped with a different letter.

Mātṛkā. The word means “little mother”, and it is the name for the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, fifty or so, from a to kṣa. Śākta teaching holds that the letters are the Goddess as sound: everything that can be named comes out of them.

Nyāsa. “Placing”. In nyāsa a syllable is set on a part of the body with a touch and a mantra. Here the priest places the letters on the Goddess in the lamp: the vowels on the head and face, the consonant groups on the limbs, in a fixed order. (A general description; the order differs between manuals.)

Not printed here. The exact formulas, with their seed syllables, are taught from teacher to student and recited by the priest; this article describes them and does not give them.

What it says. A Goddess made of the alphabet is a Goddess of speech, knowledge and all naming. Many read it this way: before we bow to her, we remember that every word we have ever spoken was hers. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: So the priest spells her out?

PROF. BOLT: In a way. He places the whole alphabet on her, from the first sound to the last. When he is done, there is nothing sayable that is not her.

Every letter a limb of the Mother.

THE OFFERINGS AND THE READINGS

Sixteen services and a night of recitation

Sixteen services. Once she is present in the lamp, Durgā is served as an honoured queen: water for the feet and hands, a bath (with flowers and sprinkled water), cloth, ornaments, turmeric and kuṅkuma, flowers, incense, lamp and food. This is the familiar ṣoḍaśopacāra, the sixteen services of any full pūjā.

Her names. Kuṅkuma or flowers are offered while her names are recited: the 108 names (aṣṭottara) of Durgā, and in many homes the Lalitā Sahasranāma or the Lalitā Triśatī.

The Saptaśatī. The Devī Māhātmya, “the seven hundred verses”, is recited in full or in part, often by several priests, with the Durgā Sūkta. Its three movements match the three forms invoked in the lamp.

Song. In maṭhas and big gatherings bhajana groups sing devotional songs between the formal parts, and a scholar may speak about the Goddess.

Naivedya. Food is offered last, with water sprinkled around it, before the closing rites.

ZAPPY: How long does all this take?

PROF. BOLT: A few hours on one evening, more for a vow over several days. It is a night’s work, not a quick pūjā.

Served like a queen, praised in her own scripture.

THE NAMASKĀRAS AND THE CLOSE

Bowing, ārati, prasāda and farewell

The bows. The rite is named for its salutations, and it ends in them. After the offerings the family and the gathered devotees bow before the lamp, many in full prostration, sāṣṭāṅga namaskāra (“with eight limbs” touching the ground). Women often make the five-limbed bow, kneeling.

Pradakṣiṇā. Circling clockwise around the maṇḍala or, where there is no room, turning in place.

Maṅgaḷārati. The lamp-Goddess is waved with lights, camphor burning to nothing on the plate, with bells and a closing song. Then the ārati is taken: palms passed over the flame and touched to the eyes.

Prasāda. Flowers, kuṅkuma and the offered food are given to all present as her grace. On the coast the evening usually ends with a meal.

Farewell. The Goddess is respectfully asked to withdraw (visarjana) and pardon is begged for any fault in the worship. The maṇḍala, its work done, is later cleared. (Reported practice; details differ.)

ZAPPY: Why bow at the end and not at the start?

PROF. BOLT: You bow at the start too. But by the end you have seen her served, sung and lit up for hours. The last bow is the one that comes by itself.

It ends as it is named: in a bow.

WHEN IT IS DONE

Nights, months and vows

Sources and priests differ on the best days; these are the ones named most often. It is usually done in the evening or at night.

🌙 Navarātri

Above all in the nine nights of Śarad Navarātri, when maṭhas and temples hold it, often on Durgāṣṭamī or Mahānavamī. See Navarātri.

🌧️ Āṣāḍha

The monsoon month of Āṣāḍha, Karkāṭaka (Āṭi) in the coastal solar calendar, is a favoured season.

📅 Tuesdays and Fridays

The Devī’s weekdays; many start a vow on one of them.

🌓 Tithis

Aṣṭamī, navamī, caturdaśī, pūrṇimā and amāvāsyā are named by different sources.

🏠 Family occasions

A new house, a wedding, a vow fulfilled, or a hard time; often on the same day as a Gaṇahoma.

🗓️ One night or many

Usually one evening; devoted families keep it as a vow of several days, up to twelve.

ZAPPY: So it can be done any time?

PROF. BOLT: On a good day chosen with the priest. Navarātri is the great season, but a family in trouble does not wait for the calendar.

Navarātri above all, and whenever the family needs her.

ON THE TUḶU COAST

Homes, maṭhas and the Durgā shrines

A household rite. In Uḍupi and Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa, Durgā Namaskāra is above all something families do at home. A big family occasion may run from a Gaṇahoma in the morning through a Satyanārāyaṇa Pūjā in the afternoon to Durgā Namaskāra at night.

Maṭhas. The Mādhva maṭhas of Uḍupi keep it as an annual Navarātri rite; the Pejavara Maṭha holds an annual Durgā Namaskāra even at its Mumbai branch. Uḍupi families who have moved to Bengaluru, Mysuru or Mumbai carry it with them.

A land of the Devī. The coast is thick with great Durgā shrines: Kaṭīl, Mandārthi, Bappanāḍu, Poḷali and Maṅgaḷādevī. Their night worships with lamps and drawn patterns are close cousins of the household rite.

Raṅgavalli and night worship. The coast’s love of drawn floor patterns, lamps and night-long devotion, seen also in Nāgamaṇḍala and Yakṣagāna, runs through Durgā Namaskāra too. (Our observation.)

Inland. Mysuru keeps the Devī as Cāmuṇḍeśvarī on her hill and in the Mysuru Dasarā; Durgā Namaskāra there is mostly the practice of coastal and Mādhva families. See Cāmuṇḍeśvarī.

ZAPPY: Why is it so strong on the coast?

PROF. BOLT: The coast is Devī country, with a great Durgā shrine every few miles and a priestly tradition in Uḍupi that keeps these rites alive in homes. Habits that live in homes last longest.

A home rite of the Devī’s own coast.

STORY · THE MOTHER OF BEES

Kaṭīl’s Bhrāmarī and Aruṇāsura

The story

The legend of Kaṭīl Durgāparameśvarī, the great Durgā of the Tuḷu coast.

When the Goddess destroyed Śumbha and Niśumbha, their minister Aruṇāsura escaped and raised a new army.
By penance he won from Brahmā a boon that no creature with two legs or four could kill him.
The Goddess came to him as a beautiful woman and vanished into a rock. In rage he struck the rock with his sword.
Out of the rock burst a swarm of black bees, bhramaras. Bees have six legs; the boon could not protect him, and they stung him to death. She is Bhrāmarī.
At the gods’ prayer she stayed in the middle of the river Nandinī as the rock-born Durgāparameśvarī of Kaṭīl.

ZAPPY: What does a bee story have to do with a lamp pūjā?

PROF. BOLT: Both are the coast’s Durgā: a Mother who comes in the smallest, plainest form, a bee, a flame, and is more than a match for any demon.

The smallest form, the surest victory.

WHAT IT MEANS

Readings of teachers and devotees

The Goddess as light. The simplest reading: the Mother is the light by which everything is seen. To worship her in a flame is to worship the seeing itself. (Interpretation.)

Crossing over. The Durgā Sūkta’s image, the boat over the sea of difficulties, is what families come with: illness, debt, quarrels, fear. The rite says: you do not have to swim; take refuge, and be carried. (Interpretation.)

The world in a lotus. The maṇḍala draws the whole world with the Goddess at its still centre, guarded on every side. Teachers read it as the shape of a centred life. (Interpretation.)

Made of speech. Mātṛkā-nyāsa makes her out of the alphabet. Every word is hers; speaking carefully is a form of worship. (Interpretation.)

The bow. The rite is named not for what is offered but for the bow. Whatever else is done, the essence is the surrender in “namaḥ”: not I, but you.

ZAPPY: Which reading is right?

PROF. BOLT: Take the one that carries you across. They all point at the same flame.

Light, refuge, a centre, a word, a bow.

WORDS TO KNOW

The vocabulary of this guide

🪔 Dīpa · kālu-dīpa

Lamp; a tall footed standing lamp.

🎨 Maṇḍala · raṅgavalli

The sacred diagram drawn in coloured powders.

🔤 Mātṛkā-nyāsa

Placing the letters of the alphabet on the Goddess’s form.

🙏 Namaskāra

Bow, salutation; sāṣṭāṅga is the full prostration.

🧭 Aṣṭa dikpālas

Guardians of the eight directions.

📜 Saptaśatī

The Devī Māhātmya, “seven hundred verses”.

📣 Saṅkalpa · āvāhana

The statement of intent; the invitation of the deity.

🔥 Visarjana

The respectful farewell at the end.

Words to keep.
