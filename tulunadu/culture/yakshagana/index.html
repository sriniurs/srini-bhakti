THE NIGHT THEATRE OF THE COAST · A REFERENCE GUIDE

YAKṢAGĀNA

Gods, kings and demons from dusk to dawn

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

At dusk the ceṇḍe starts to thunder. By the time the drumming stops, a field has become a court of the gods, and for the rest of the night Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Bhīma and demon kings walk among the coconut palms.

Yakṣagāna is the coast’s great theatre: sung, danced and argued, in towering crowns and painted faces, played to villagers and pilgrims alike, and still offered at temples as worship.

This guide covers how a night is built, who does what, what they wear, and how the two great styles differ.

With love for the āṭa,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is it a dance, a play or an opera?

PROF. BOLT: All three at once. One man sings the story, the actors dance it and then speak it in their own words. That last part is what makes it unlike anything else.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
WhatA dance-drama of song, dance and spoken dialogue, traditionally all night, in the open air
Name“Song of the yakṣas”; simply āṭa, “the play”, in Tuḷu and Kannaḍa; older names include bayalāṭa, kēḷike and daśāvatāra
WhereUḍupi, Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa, Uttara Kannaḍa, parts of Śivamogga and Kāsaragōḍ
LanguageKannaḍa, and since the 20th century also Tuḷu
MusiciansThe himmēḷa: singer, drums, a drone
ActorsThe mummēḷa, all men by tradition; women perform today
SeasonTouring troupes play November to May, about 180–200 nights each
ScriptA prasaṅga: songs in Kannaḍa metres; the speech between them is improvised

ZAPPY: Who were the yakṣas?

PROF. BOLT: Semi-divine beings of the old stories. The name probably points to the songs, “yakṣa-gāna”, rather than to who performs them.

ROOTS AND RECORDS

From ritual to theatre
Roots Scholars count the bhūta kōla and the Nāgamaṇḍala among its sources, along with temple music, Sanskrit drama and the Bhakti movement.
c. 1300 Tradition credits Narahari Tīrtha, a disciple of Madhvācārya, with a daśāvatāra troupe at Uḍupi.
1556 An inscription at the Lakṣmīnārāyaṇa temple, Kurugōḍu (Baḷḷāri), records land given to tāḷamaddaḷe performers: the earliest written evidence.
c. 1600 The poet Pārti Subba composes a Rāmāyaṇa for the stage; his father Veṅkaṭa is thought to have shaped the southern style.
1621 A palm-leaf manuscript describes the sabhālakṣaṇa, the fixed opening of a show.
1840s A troupe from Uttara Kannaḍa tours Maharashtra and inspires the first modern Marāṭhī mythological play.
19th–20th c. New prasaṅgas, ticketed “tent” troupes, gas and then electric light, and Tuḷu on stage.

INSIDE A TROUPE

The mēḷa and its two halves
BhāgavataLead singer and director; holds the cymbals
MaddaḷeTwo-headed barrel drum played with the hands
CeṇḍeLoud drum played with sticks
DroneFormerly the puṅgi pipe; now usually a harmonium or a śruti box
VeṣadhārisThe costumed actor-dancers
CaukiThe green room, where the troupe dresses

Who sits where. The first four make up the himmēḷa, “those behind”; the actors are the mummēḷa, “those in front”.

Green-room order. In the cauki, actors sit in rows by seniority. At the entrance stands the king’s crown, honoured as Gaṇeśa and as the troupe’s deity; the night begins with a worship before it, the cauki pūje.

ZAPPY: Is the drummer just keeping time?

PROF. BOLT: No. Yakṣagāna has its own system of tāḷas and rhythmic patterns, called maṭṭu. The drummers shape every entry, fight and exit.

THE BHĀGAVATA

The first actor who never dresses

🎤 Narrator

Sings the verses of the prasaṅga, including the characters’ lines, which the actors then dance and expand in speech.

🎛️ Director

Sets the pace, calls each entry, cuts or stretches scenes, and holds the pitch on which the actors speak.

🔔 Cymbals

Keeps the tāḷa on small cymbals of a five-metal alloy, carrying sets in different keys to suit his voice.

🌗 Time of night

Chooses rāgas to suit the hour, and sings high and bright as dawn approaches.

ZAPPY: He talks to the actors on stage?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. He asks them questions, they answer in character, and a whole scene can grow out of that exchange. He is called the modalane veṣa, the first role.

THE STAGE

Four poles and a held curtain

Size. A traditional stage is a rectangle roughly 12 by 15 feet, marked by four wooden poles dressed with mango and palm leaves, open on three sides to the crowd.

The tere. A single cloth, about four by eight feet, is held up by two helpers. A character waits behind it, is shown to the audience over its top, and makes his entry as it drops.

One prop. The ratha, a seat at the centre, is for the king in court, or for whoever ranks highest on stage.

Movement. Mostly circular; battles cut across the diagonals.

THE NIGHT BEGINS

The sabhālakṣaṇa, or pūrvaraṅga
Dusk Abbara, or pīṭhike: the ceṇḍe and maddaḷe play for up to an hour as the crowd gathers.
Cauki In the green room, worship of Gaṇeśa before the crown, with coconut, bananas and the lamp.
Bālagōpālas Two boys as young Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma dance the first dance. In the southern style, Ardhanārīśvara and Ṣaṇmukha may follow.
The clown The hāsyagāra, also Hanumanāyaka, enters with his boy-monkeys, the kōḍaṅgis, who wear mango leaves. He has licence to joke about anyone.
Pīṭhike strīveṣa Two actors in female roles dance a graceful piece of love and longing.
Oḍḍōlaga The court assembles: the king and his circle are presented in a slow, grand dance of about half an hour. The story is about to start.

ZAPPY: An hour of drums before anyone appears?

PROF. BOLT: It calls the village. On a still night the ceṇḍe carries for kilometres, and people walk over in the dark to the sound.

THE STORY UNFOLDS

Entry, song, speech, battle, blessing
Pravēśa. Each character enters from behind the tere with a dance proper to his kind: stately for kings, explosive for demons, light for women and boys.
Song, then speech. The bhāgavata sings a verse; the actor dances it; then the actor speaks in his own words, often at length, arguing, pleading, boasting or teaching.
Debate in character. Rāma and Vālī, or Karṇa and Kṛṣṇa, may argue duty and justice for long stretches, each actor drawing on his own learning.
Battle. Drums rise, fighters circle and leap; in the southern style, spinning jumps can go on for dozens of turns.
Toward dawn. The story reaches its end as the sky lightens.
Maṅgaḷa. The strīveṣa dances with the lamp; the bhāgavata asks the audience’s leave and sings the closing praises; in the cauki the crowns of Gōpāla and Balarāma are honoured, and the host gives the troupe its fee with betel, the vīḷya.

THE PLOTS

Where the prasaṅgas come from

🏹 Rāmāyaṇa

From Rāma’s youth to the war in Laṅkā; Pārti Subba’s cycle is among the oldest and best loved.

⚔️ Mahābhārata

The richest source: dicing, exile, embassy, Kurukṣētra. Episodes are named for their heart, such as Karṇārjuna or Gadāyuddha.

🪈 Bhāgavata and Purāṇas

Kṛṣṇa’s deeds, Devī’s battles, the stories of Śiva and Viṣṇu; also Jain tales.

🌾 Tuḷu stories

Since Tuḷu came on stage, the epics of the coast: Kōṭi–Cennaya, Siri, and the shrines of the region.

Classic texts. Among famous prasaṅgas are Muddaṇa’s Ratnāvatī Kalyāṇa and Mummaḍi Kṛṣṇarāja Woḍeyar’s Saugandhika Pariṇaya; King Kaṇṭhīrava Narasarāja Woḍeyar II is credited with fourteen. Newer troupes add social and invented stories alongside.

KINDS OF ROLE

Every character has a type
Rāja veṣaKings and great heroes: crown, dignity, measured dance
Puṇḍu veṣaYoung warriors: a heart-shaped headdress, quick, bright dance
Baṇṇada veṣa“Coloured roles”: demons and fierce beings, with huge headdresses and painted faces
StrīveṣaWomen: queens, goddesses, heroines, in sari and ornaments
HāsyagāraThe clown: servant, messenger, commentator on the day’s news
Ṛṣi and guruSages and teachers, sober and unpainted

ZAPPY: Are demons always the villains?

PROF. BOLT: Not simply. Karanth sorted them: destroyers like Rāvaṇa, but also demons with a moral code, like Vibhīṣaṇa and Atikāya. Some of the most admired performances are of “villains”.

COSTUME

Gold, mirrors and padding
Ornaments. Crown, breastplate (kavaca), winged armlets (bhujakīrti) and belts (ḍābu) are carved from light wood, covered in gold foil and set with mirror pieces and coloured stones to catch the light.
Headgear by role. Kings wear the tall kirīṭa; young heroes the round pagaḍe or the kēdage muṇḍāle, named for the screw-pine flower; women a small pagaḍe; demons enormous haloed headdresses.
Body. A kacche, a wrapped lower cloth in red, yellow and orange checks; a jacket beneath the ornaments; padding that broadens the frame into something larger than life.
Today. Some troupes use thermocol and synthetic parts to cut weight and cost; others keep to wood and foil.

MAKE-UP

Colour tells you who someone is
BaseWhite zinc and a red pigment mixed in coconut oil; a yellow powder in place of the old orpiment, aradāḷa
RedHeroism, war, temper
GreenPeace, gentleness, romance
BlackDeath, cruelty, the tragic
DemonsRed, green and black in layers of rice paste; white dots, the cuṭṭi; cotton balls on the nose; tusks; about three hours to apply
WomenA soft face, a red tilaka on the brow
Boys’ rolesBālagōpāla, Lava, Kuśa and Kṛṣṇa wear no moustache

ZAPPY: Three hours, every night?

PROF. BOLT: For the big demon roles, yes. The actor is often painting himself in the cauki while the early scenes are already running outside.

TEṄKU AND BAḌAGU

The southern and northern schools side by side
Teṅku tiṭṭu (south)Baḍagu tiṭṭu (north)
RegionDakṣiṇa Kannaḍa, Kāsaragōḍ, Sampāje and south UḍupiUḍupi to Kundāpura and Baindūru, and Uttara Kannaḍa
CeṇḍeClose to the Kerala ceṇḍe, played standingThe Karnataka ceṇḍe, built and tuned differently, played seated
MusicStronger Karnāṭaka influence in singing and maddaḷeIts own older melodic manner
DanceSpectacular: high spinning leaps, the dhīṅgiṇa; showpiece demon entriesFootwork and gesture suited to each role
StressVisual splendourFacial expression and dialogue
ColoursThree fixed sets: rājabaṇṇa, kāṭbaṇṇa, strībaṇṇaMake-up shaped more freely to the character

Further north. A third school, the Baḍabaḍagu or Uttara Kannaḍa tiṭṭu, is played in the far north. Scholars also group all coastal forms as paḍuvalapāya, the western way, against the inland mūḍalapāya.

TĀḶAMADDAḶE

Yakṣagāna with only the word

🪑 The form

No costume, no dance, no stage. The bhāgavata and drummers sing the same prasaṅga; seated speakers, the arthadhāris, take the roles in words alone.

🗣️ The art

Pure oratory: each speaker defends his character with argument, scripture and wit, often told his role just before starting.

📛 Other names

Kūṭa, “gathering”, as against āṭa, “play”; also baiṭhak, prasaṅga, oḍike and jāgara, “keeping awake”.

🎓 Its masters

An amateur, freelance art with no regional schools. Deraje Sītārāmayya (1914–1984) is among its remembered names.

TEMPLE TROUPES

When the show is an offering
KaṭīlDurgāparamēśvarī temple; Teṅku; first troupe 1867, a seventh added in 2025
DharmasthaḷaMañjunātha temple; Teṅku; founded in the 19th century
MandārtiDurgāparamēśvarī temple; Baḍagu; from the 1950s
IḍagunjiMahāgaṇapati temple; Baḍagu; founded 1934 by Keremane Śivarāma Hegaḍe
OthersKumbḷe, Hālāḍi, Amṛtēśvarī (Kōṭa), Kamalaśile, Hosanagara and more
TicketedCommercial troupes such as Sāligrāma and Perdūru sell seats in tents

Hiring the god’s troupe. At temple troupes, a devotee books a night as a sēvā, an offering; popular troupes are booked years in advance. See Kaṭīl and Iḍagunji.

Scale. One estimate puts the field at about 30 professional troupes, 200 amateur ones and 1,000 professional artists.

A CHANGING ART

What moved, and who moved it
Tuḷu Tuḷu enters the stage alongside Kannaḍa and brings in the coast’s own epics and huge new audiences.
Karanth K. Śivarāma Kāranta champions the Baḍagu style, writes a classic study of the art, and creates the shorter Yakṣaraṅga ballets at Sāligrāma; a later court case (2009) concerned the use of the Yakṣagāna name for them.
Short shows Many troupes now play a few hours instead of all night. At Kaṭīl the move to time-bound shows was challenged in court; the debate between the full night and the short show continues.
Women Women now perform, including all-women troupes that play male roles too.
Abroad Troupes such as Yakshaloka (USA, 1995) and Yakshamitra (Toronto, 2008) stage it overseas with live himmēḷa.
Honours Keremane Śivarāma Hegaḍe is the first Yakṣagāna artist to receive the Rāṣṭrapati award; Chiṭṭāṇi Rāmacandra Hegaḍe receives the Padma Śrī in 2012.
