THE RITE OF THE DAIVAS · TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

BHŪTA KŌLA

A night when the guardian comes to speak

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Every daiva in this series has a story. The kōla is how that story is kept alive: once a year, at night, by torchlight, a man paints his face, ties on the anklets, and for a few hours the guardian of the village stands among its people again.

A kōla is not theatre, though it has costume, music and dance. It is worship, and it is also a court: the daiva listens, settles quarrels and gives its word.

This guide walks through one night from start to finish: who performs, what they wear, what happens in what order, and what is said.

With respect to the daivas,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: So the performer is acting the daiva?

PROF. BOLT: Not in the eyes of those who gather. For them the man steps aside and the daiva arrives. That one belief changes how every step of the night is done.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
WhatA night rite in which a daiva is believed to come through a costumed performer, dance, receive offerings and speak
WhereDakṣiṇa Kannaḍa and Uḍupi districts, and the north of Kāsaragōḍ in Kerala
Also calledDaiva kōla, daiva nēma, bhūtārādhane, daivārādhane
PerformerAlways a man, from a hereditary family of the Nalike, Pambada or Parava communities
HostA temple, a shrine committee, a landholding house or a family
LanguageTuḷu, in an old form used in the epics
LengthUsually one night; a large festival can stretch over several
Heart of itThe nuḍi: the daiva’s spoken word to the village

ZAPPY: Does every village have one?

PROF. BOLT: Nearly every village and many old houses have at least one daiva of their own, and a daiva with a shrine expects its kōla.

BHŪTA, DAIVA, KŌLA

Three words to get right

👻 Bhūta

Not a ghost. In Tuḷunāḍu a bhūta is a guardian power bound to a place, a family or a field. Many were once people, some were animals, some came from the great gods.

🛡️ Daiva

The respectful word for the same beings. Many devotees today prefer daiva, and call the rite daivārādhane, worship of the daivas.

🎭 Kōla

Tuḷu for a form, a guise, a performance. The kōla is the daiva taking on a visible form for a night.

📜 Nēma

A larger rite where several daivas come one after another, in order of rank, usually hosted by a great house and spaced years apart.

ZAPPY: Why does “bhūta” sound scary to outsiders?

PROF. BOLT: In much of India it means a ghost. On the coast it is closer to “guardian”. That gap in meaning is why many people now say daiva instead.

THREE WORLDS

Field, forest and the world of the daivas

A map of the world. Scholars of the kōla, following the anthropologist Peter Claus, describe a three-part world: the cultivated village and field; the forest and wasteland around it; and, unseen behind both, the world of the daivas.

The go-between. On this reading the performer communities, who once lived at the edge of the village and worked in both field and forest, stood on the border between worlds. That made them fitting carriers for powers that cross borders.

The deal. The daiva guards fields, cattle, boundaries and fair dealing. In return the people keep its shrine and give it its kōla on time.

THE PERFORMER FAMILIES

Who may wear the daiva
NalikeThe largest group; the main performers across much of Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa.
PambadaA smaller community. By one long-held custom, Pambadas take the rājan daivas, the royal daivas such as Jumādi.
ParavaAnother hereditary community, strongest in some southern and Uḍupi-side villages, with its own repertoire.
OthersSources also name Kopāla and Pāṇara performers, and Billava darśana pātris, who take the daiva with sword and bell and no full costume.

Numbers. In 2022 a Pambada spokesman estimated that, among performers over sixty in the two coastal districts, about 20–25 were Pambadas, about 30 Paravas and about 1,000 Nalikes.

An old paradox. These are Scheduled Caste communities. For one night, the landholders who stand above them in daily life bow to the daiva in them and accept its rulings.

ZAPPY: Can anyone just learn it?

PROF. BOLT: No. Each family holds the right to particular daivas, at particular shrines. The right is inherited, not hired.

HOW THE ART IS PASSED ON

No school, no guru, only the family
Watching. Boys go with their fathers, uncles and grandfathers to kōlas and learn by watching and copying. There is no formal school.
Small parts first. A boy starts with a minor daiva or a helper’s role, and takes on larger daivas as he grows.
The family’s list. Each family has its own repertoire of daivas and performs only those. A performer may hold one daiva at one shrine and another daiva elsewhere.
The words. He must know the pāḍdana, the daiva’s epic in old Tuḷu, and the right steps, gestures and paint for each daiva.
A disciplined life. Performers keep rules of food and conduct in the season, so as to be fit to carry the daiva.

The women. Women of the performer families sing the pāḍdanas to the small tembere drum, and keep their own song-and-dance tradition, the mādira, sung from house to house in the rainy months when there are no kōlas.

THE BHAṆḌĀRA

The daiva’s treasure

What it is. The bhaṇḍāra is the daiva’s set of sacred things: metal masks, swords, a trident, bells, a cāmara fly-whisk, ornaments and sometimes a small idol. Through the year it rests in the shrine or in the daivakōṇe, a room set apart in an old landholding house, often on a hanging cot.

Its own procession. On the evening of the kōla the bhaṇḍāra is carried out with drums to the place of the rite. Its arrival starts the night.

Where the daivas come from. A Tuḷu saying holds that the daivas come up from the west, the side of the sea, which is why the daiva room of an old house was placed on its western side, facing east.

COSTUME, HEAD TO FOOT

What each piece is made of
FacePainted in patterns fixed for each daiva: bright yellow for some, red, black and white lines for others. Made from turmeric, lime, soot and coloured earths.
CrownA metal or palm-leaf headpiece, the muḍi; some daivas wear a mask over the face instead.
AṇiThe great halo tied to the back (next page)
BodyBreastplate, shoulder and ear pieces, armlets, bangles, a waist frame
SiriA skirt of tender coconut fronds, pale green-gold, cut fresh for the night
GaggaraHeavy hollow anklets of bell-metal or silver that ring with each step
In handSword and shield, a torch, a bell, a bow, or the daiva’s own weapon

Weight. Costume and ornaments together can weigh 60–70 kg for the grandest daivas. Some, such as Guḷiga in many villages, wear only paint, garlands and a light skirt.

Fire. The frond skirt burns easily, and daivas who dance with fire handle and leap across flames in it.

THE AṆI

The halo built for one night

🎋 Frame

Bamboo ribs fanned out from a hub and lashed to a wooden base that is tied firmly to the performer’s back.

🎨 Dressing

Bands of red and white cloth, silver or tin arcs, tender coconut leaf, shells and fresh flowers.

🧵 Making

Built by helpers on the day, from fresh materials, while the performer is painted. It is untied and taken apart after the rite.

📏 Size

Each daiva has its own shape. The largest aṇis reach about ten feet and frame the dancer like a rising sun.

THE MASKS

Bronze faces that are worshipped
Material. Most ritual masks are cast bronze or other copper alloy by the lost-wax method, up to about 30–40 cm tall and up to about 10 kg; some are brass, silver or gold, a few wood.
Waking the mask. A commissioned mask is consecrated when finished: the eyes are engraved last, after a cockerel is offered, and the mask is washed with coconut water or palm juice.
Always holy. A mask belongs to the bhaṇḍāra and is worshipped even when no one is wearing it; if damaged it is repaired, not discarded.
Rank shows. Banṭe, Jumādi’s attendant, has a boar mask that is carried in front of the dancer rather than worn, a sign that he serves.

THE NIGHT, PART ONE

From invitation to the anklets
Days before The host gives the performer the eṇṇe būḷya: coconut oil with betel leaf and areca, the formal invitation to carry the daiva.
Afternoon Helpers cut fronds for the siri and build the aṇi. The performer takes a ritual oil bath to cleanse and calm himself.
Dusk The bhaṇḍāra comes out of the shrine or the house in procession, with drums and torches, and is set in place at the kōla ground.
Evening Sitting before a lamp, the performer is painted. Beside him a family member sings the pāḍdana, the daiva’s life story, and the performer often takes up the song himself.
Late evening He is dressed and ornamented piece by piece. The aṇi is lifted and tied to his back.
The threshold The gaggara are tied at the ankles. The drums rise, his feet begin to tap, and from here the man is treated as the daiva.

ZAPPY: Why sing the whole life story first?

PROF. BOLT: The pāḍdana tells the daiva who it is and tells the people who is coming. When the song ends at the daiva’s arrival on earth, the performer is ready to become it.

THE NIGHT, PART TWO

From the dance to dawn
The dance. The daiva circles the ground with torch-bearers at its side. The steps are the daiva’s own: a boar’s rush for Pañjurli, a tiger’s crouch for Pilicāmuṇḍi, a warrior’s sword-play for the heroes.
Fire. Daivas of fire seize torches, walk on coals or dance through burning coir.
“Why was I called?” The daiva stops before the head of the house or the shrine and asks why it has been summoned.
Offerings. The host answers and offers: tender coconut, raw rice, puffed and beaten rice, bananas, sugarcane, betel; for many daivas a cockerel, whose blood is sprinkled on the ground.
The word. Pleased, the daiva sits, often fanning itself, and speaks: the nuḍi.
Prasāda. People come one by one; the daiva gives each a pinch of kuṅkuma or turmeric and an areca flower spray, and hears their troubles.
Leaving. At last the daiva circles the shrine, the anklets are untied, and an exhausted man is left sitting where the daiva stood.

THE NUḌI

The daiva’s word, and its court

🗣️ How it sounds

Rapid, formulaic, in old Tuḷu. Few outside the tradition can follow every word; elders and the head of the house can.

👁️ What it says

That the daiva watches over the village, sees good and bad, is pleased or angry with what has happened, and needs certain things done.

🤝 The answer

The head of the house or shrine replies on behalf of all and promises to carry out the daiva’s wishes.

⚖️ The court

Quarrels over land, boundaries, debts and family matters are put before the daiva. Its decision is accepted, and oaths sworn before it are kept.

Power and order. In the old landholding houses, the guttu, the daiva’s public respect to the head of the house, and his reply, renewed that house’s standing every year. Special rites, the dharma nēma and the gaḍi pradhāna, installed a new head before the daiva.

SEASON AND SOUND

When kōlas happen, and what you hear
SeasonFrom about Dīpāvaḷi to Pattanāje, the tenth day of the solar month Vṛṣabha (late May), when the rains close the season
BusiestDecember to May; a sought-after performer may take part in 75–100 kōlas a season
DateFixed by the shrine’s custom or chosen by the host; popular shrines are booked years ahead
StartsAfter dark, often around ten at night, ending near dawn
DrumsThe tembere, and larger drums for the dance
WindPipes; in many places today, the saxophone
BellsHand bells, and the gaggara at every step

ZAPPY: A saxophone, at an ancient rite?

PROF. BOLT: It is a regular sound in coastal ritual bands now. The rite holds its core steady and lets the band change around it.

THE KŌLA TODAY

Old rite, new attention
14th c. An inscription from Bārakūru is reported to mention a bhūta, Kundōdara, demanding an offering from a king: the earliest written trace cited for the cult.
1961–70s Karnataka’s land reforms break up the great estates whose houses had funded many kōlas; shrine committees and families take up more of the cost.
2022 The Kannaḍa film Kantara brings the kōla to audiences across India.
Oct 2022 The Karnataka government announces a monthly allowance of ₹2,000 for daiva performers aged sixty and over.
After Performer communities ask that the allowance also cover the wider ensemble: darśana pātris, the mukkālti who attend the daiva, torch-holders and musicians.

Two worries voiced by the community. Performers and many devotees object to kōlas being imitated on stage, in reels and in fancy dress, since for them a daiva cannot be acted. Some also worry about fewer young men taking up the hard, seasonal work.
