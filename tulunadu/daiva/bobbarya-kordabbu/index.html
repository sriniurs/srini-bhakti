DAIVAS OF TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

BOBBARYA AND KORDABBU

The sea daiva and the king of daivas

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Two daivas of the coast, two lives that began at the edges of society, and one quarrel that made one of them a king.

Bobbarya was a sailor of mixed birth, lost to the sea, who became the guardian of fishermen. Kordabbu, called Babbu Svāmi, was an orphan healer, betrayed and killed, whom the royal daivas themselves crowned Daivarāja, “king of the daivas”.

This guide tells both stories and the day they met.

Bobbarya, Babbu Svāmi,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why put two daivas in one guide?

PROF. BOLT: Because their stories meet. One stood in the road; the other moved him. You can't tell either tale fully without the other.

PROF. BOLT: Mūlūr keeps both, near each other. The quarrel is over; the neighbours remain.

WHERE THEY ARE WORSHIPPED

The fishing coast

The fishing coast. Bobbarya is honoured in the fishing villages along the shore from Maṅgaḷūru to beyond Malpe, wherever boats go out.

Mūlūr. The village of Mūlūr, between Uḍupi and Maṅgaḷūru on the coastal highway, takes its name from “mūla-ūru”, the village of origin: the first home of Bobbarya’s worship, with his pond and his shrine, and a shrine of Babbu Svāmi nearby.

BOBBA THE SAILOR

Born between two faiths
His birth. Bobbarya was once a man named Bobba, a sailor, the son of a Muslim father and a Jain mother.
The sea. He died at sea. One telling says he survived a shipwreck and, by his own mystic power, swam ashore at Kāpu, where he became a daiva.
His first shrine. It was a Billava toddy-tapper, a Hindu, who first built a shrine in his honour.

ZAPPY: A Muslim sailor’s son, a Jain mother, a Hindu shrine-builder?

PROF. BOLT: All three in one story. That's why scholars on the coast call Bobbarya a symbol of brotherhood.

DAIVA OF THE SEA

The fishermen’s guardian
A sea spirit. As a daiva, Bobbarya is the powerful spirit of the sea. The pāḍdanas say he longed to be recognised and honoured by the people of the land.
The Mogavīras’ daiva. He is the patron daiva of the Mogavīras, the fishing community of the coast, who have gone out to sea for centuries and know its tides and storms.
When boats are lost. When fishermen go missing at sea, their families turn to him. In January 2019, when a trawler with seven fishermen from Malpe vanished, the Mogavīras of Baḍanidiyūr held a daiva darśana at Bobbarya’s shrine to seek his word on them.

A DAIVA OF THREE PEOPLES

Mogavīra, Billava, Beary

🎣 Mogavīras

The fishermen, his own people, who keep his shrines and his kōla.

🌴 Billavas

The toddy-tappers, one of whom first built his shrine.

⛵ Bearys

The Muslim seafaring traders of the coast, his father’s people.

🪷 Jains

His mother’s faith, which once ruled much of the coast.

A question without an answer. A Kannaḍa scholar has asked: Muslims do not worship daivas, so in Muslim culture Bobbarya cannot be a daiva; among Hindus, if he is a god, what hymns would they sing? The answer the coast gives is its own: he is honoured in the daiva way, with pāḍdana and kōla, by people of more than one faith.

THE ORPHAN BABBU

Kordabbu’s beginning
Orphaned. A woman named Kacurmālti gave birth to a son, but she and her husband died soon after.
A spirit’s request. A neighbour, Koḍaṅge Bannār, heard the baby crying. The mother’s spirit asked him to look after the child.
Raised. The baby went to Bannār’s sister, who raised him as her own and named him Babbu.
A name earned. As a boy Babbu had strange powers. When hens stole the grain, he struck them dead with one stick and brought them back to life with another. From this he was called Kordabbu.

ZAPPY: Kill a hen and bring it back? Why bother?

PROF. BOLT: To show he held both death and life in his hands. That's the first sign of the healer he was going to be.

VAIDYANĀTHA

The master of medicines
Gifts. Babbu grew famous for prophecy, for divination and for finding water, the most valued skill on a dry hillside.
The cow. Rivals stole his cow, and a crocodile killed it. Babbu brought it back to life.
The title. From then on he was called Vaidyanātha, “lord of physicians”, master of medicines.

The same healing power returns at the climax of his story, when he heals the daiva he has just wounded.

THE WELL AND THE MAIDEN

Betrayal and a broken promise
The trap. A king at Kaṭpāḍi, who saw Babbu as a rival, lured him to his court and had him thrown to the bottom of a covered well.
The rescuer. A young woman in the forest, Tannimāniga, heard his cries. She lowered her sari for him to climb out, on his promise not to look at her body.
The broken promise. He broke it. Overcome with shame, he struck his own forehead sixteen times with a sword. She forgave him and blessed him.
A goddess? Some tellings say Tannimāniga was Pārvatī herself in human form.

THE GIANT IN THE ROAD

Bobbarya blocks the royal daivas
Pride. The Rājan Daivas, the royal daivas, held themselves high, and ignored Bobbarya’s wish to be recognised.
The giant. So Bobbarya grew enormous and stood with one foot on the Ghats and the other in the sea, across their path to a river.
The insult. “Move me if you can,” he said. To pass, the royal daivas would have had to go under his legs, a humiliation.
A champion. They asked Babbu Svāmi to move him. Babbu reasoned with Bobbarya, who would not budge; then Babbu’s calm turned fierce, and he struck and broke Bobbarya’s leg.

ZAPPY: One foot on the mountains and one in the sea?

PROF. BOLT: That’s Tuḷunāḍu itself, from the Ghats to the shore. For a moment Bobbarya made himself as big as the whole land.

DAIVARĀJA

King of the daivas
Forgiveness. Bobbarya repented his pride. Babbu Svāmi forgave him, and by his power as Vaidyanātha he healed the broken leg.
The crown. The royal daivas, pleased with Babbu’s strength and grace, gave him a place no other daiva holds: Daivarāja, “king of the daivas”.
A death and a return. Babbu’s human life ended unjustly, the tellings say. After death he became Babbu Svāmi, also called Kōṭeda Babbu.

The lowly crowned. One writer reads the story as a mirror of the coast’s social order: the orphan from a community long kept low is first betrayed by a king, then crowned by the gods themselves. The lesson, in his reading, is to see the worth of every being, however small they seem.

ZAPPY: Kordabbu and Tannimāniga as brother and sister, after all that?

PROF. BOLT: Forgiveness turned the story around. At the kōla she is no longer the woman he wronged but the sister who stands by him.
