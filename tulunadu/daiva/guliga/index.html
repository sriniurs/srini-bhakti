A DAIVA OF TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

GUḶIGA

Born of stone, hungry for justice

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Some gods are born of love. Guḷiga was born of a stone that Śiva threw away.

He came into the world furious and starving. He was too wild for Viṣṇu’s heaven, then too hungry for anything in it, until Viṣṇu fed him his own little finger. Tamed but never soft, he was sent to the coast to guard it.

Many on the coast call him the most widely worshipped of all the daivas, and often the most feared. This guide tells his story and how he is honoured.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A god who bit Viṣṇu? Is he a good guy or a bad guy?

PROF. BOLT: Neither, exactly. He’s fierce power with a job. Point him at injustice and you couldn’t ask for a better guard.

ZAPPY: “Guḷigajja”? Like Koragajja?

PROF. BOLT: Same affectionate “ajja”, grandfather. Even the fiercest daiva gets called granddad by the people he protects.

THE STONE IN THE ASH

A birth at Kailāsa
A strange stone. At Kailāsa, the pāḍdana says, Pārvatī was gathering sacred ash when she found a strange black stone hidden in it. She showed it to Śiva.
Thrown away. Śiva cast the stone away, into the water or onto the earth, as the tellings differ.
Born. From the stone sprang Guḷiga, dark and huge, carrying Śiva’s own fierce energy. He was counted among Śiva’s gaṇas.
Sent to serve. Śiva sent the newborn to Viṣṇu at Vaikuṇṭha, to serve him.

BORN AGAIN FROM A SERPENT

A curse in Vaikuṇṭha
Too wild for heaven. In Vaikuṇṭha Guḷiga was destructive and uncontrollable, and he greatly troubled Viṣṇu.
The curse. Viṣṇu cursed him to be born again from the womb of a serpent, called in the pāḍdana Neḷa-ulla Saṅke.
A violent birth. After nine months Guḷiga tore his way out of the serpent and came into the world a second time, wilder and hungrier than before.

ZAPPY: Born twice, and both times violently?

PROF. BOLT: Twice-born from stone and serpent: the two things the coast fears and honours most, the hard ground and the snake beneath it. Guḷiga carries both.

THE ENDLESS HUNGER

Nothing was enough
A lake drained. Starving from birth, Guḷiga drank dry Viṣṇu’s lake and ate every fish in it.
Blood and more. He was given the blood of horses and elephants to drink. Still his hunger would not end.
A danger to all. A hunger that nothing in creation could fill threatened to consume creation itself.

VIṢṆU’S LITTLE FINGER

The meal that tamed him
The offer. At last Viṣṇu held out his own little finger and let Guḷiga bite it.
Peace. That single bite of the divine satisfied the hunger nothing else could. Guḷiga grew calm.
A purpose. Viṣṇu told him that his birth had a purpose: to keep justice in the world. Then he sent him down to earth to protect its people.

ZAPPY: Why did a finger work when elephants didn’t?

PROF. BOLT: Because what he was hungry for was never food. A piece of God was the only thing big enough to fill him. After that, he worked for God.

TO THE COAST

Guardian of the land
With Lakṣmī. One telling says Guḷiga came to Tuḷunāḍu together with Lakṣmī, bringing prosperity with protection.
A kṣetrapāla. He took up his place as a kṣetrapāla, the guardian of a sacred place and its boundaries, honoured at the edge of fields, estates, temples and villages.
Everywhere. He is known by different names in different places, and is said to be the oldest of Tuḷunāḍu’s daivas.

Fear and trust. Guḷiga is approached with care. Devotees believe that he punishes wrongdoing swiftly, especially broken promises and stolen land, and protects fiercely those who keep faith with him.

RIVAL AND FRIEND

Pañjurli and Koragajja

🐗 Pañjurli

When Guḷiga reached Tuḷunāḍu he met the boar daiva Pañjurli. The two argued and fought. In many places today they are honoured side by side, the rivalry settled into partnership.

🧺 Koragajja

At Burdugōḷi Guḷiga made a sacred pact, an oḍambaḍike, with Koragajja. At Koragajja’s Ādisthaḷa at Kuttār he is worshipped beside him as Guḷigajja.

ZAPPY: So he fought one and befriended the other?

PROF. BOLT: The coast's gods have relationships like people do: rivals, partners, sworn brothers. Learn who stands beside whom and you can read a village's whole history.

WORSHIPPED AS A STONE

No image, no temple
A plain stone. Guḷiga is usually worshipped not as a carved image but as a rough stone, often set under the shade of a tree at the edge of a field or in a house compound.
Why a stone. He was born of a stone, and the stone is his body on earth: the oldest and simplest form of worship on the coast.
Daily light. A lamp, a smear of red, a flower: humble daily offerings, with the great honour reserved for his kōla.

GUḶIGA’S KŌLA

The fierce night
A dark guardian. At his kōla the performer becomes Guḷiga, dark-painted and fierce, carrying fire and moving with the restless power of the stone-born.
Hunger remembered. The rites recall his hunger, with offerings of food in plenty, so that the guardian is satisfied and stays calm.
The word. As every daiva does, he speaks through the performer, warns, blesses and judges.
Taken seriously. Of all the daivas, Guḷiga’s nights are among those approached with the greatest care, for he is held to punish carelessness as quickly as wrongdoing.

ZAPPY: Do people get scared at his kōla?

PROF. BOLT: Respectful, rather. Fear is what you feel for a danger; respect is what you feel for a guard. The village sleeps better because he’s fierce.

Where the facts come from

Not verified: the details of his kōla costume and offerings, which vary by place; his punishing of broken promises as a general belief. Diagrams are simplified. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
