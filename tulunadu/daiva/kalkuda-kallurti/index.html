DAIVAS OF TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

KALKUḌA AND KALLURṬI

The sculptor and the sister who avenged him

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

On a rock above Kārkaḷa stands a stone giant, the Bāhubali of 1432. The inscription names the king who ordered it. Tuḷu tradition names the man who carved it, and tells what the king did to him afterwards.

That man is Kalkuḍa. His sister is Kallurṭi. One was maimed so that he could never make another masterpiece. The other swore that the king would pay. Both became daivas.

This guide tells their story, and why the coast still honours them.

Kalkuḍa, Kallurṭi,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: The king punished him for doing a great job?

PROF. BOLT: For doing too great a job. The king wanted no other kingdom ever to have one like it. Jealousy dressed up as pride.

ZAPPY: “Kalkuḍa”. Does the name mean something?

PROF. BOLT: It comes from “kal”, stone: the one of stone. Fitting for a man whose whole life was rock and chisel.

WHERE IT HAPPENED

Kārkaḷa and Yēnūr

Jain country. Kārkaḷa, now in Udupi district, and Yēnūr, today called Veṇūr in Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa, were seats of Jain chiefs, who raised colossal monolithic statues of Bāhubali on their hills.

A story of two statues. The sculptor’s story runs from one to the other: from the king who maimed him to the king who took him in.

A FAMILY OF STONE-CUTTERS

Born to the chisel
The parents. One telling names the sculptor’s parents as Śambu Kalkuḍa and his wife Ervāḍi, of the stone-cutters’ community, poor but gifted.
A born master. Their son Bīru (or Bēre) Kalkuḍa took up his father’s craft and outgrew him, becoming famous for temples and statues.
The sister. His sister, Kallurṭi, loved him dearly. Her devotion is the second half of the story.

An older version. A 19th-century English account tells it differently: there Kalkuḍa is the father and the sculptor, and Kalkaṭṭi and Kallurṭi his son and daughter. The family changes between tellings; the injustice does not.

THE GOMMAṬA OF KĀRKAḶA

A giant raised by one hand
The commission. The king of Kārkaḷa, called Bairana Sūḍa in the pāḍdana, ordered a colossal statue of Gommaṭa, Bāhubali, the Jain saint who stood in meditation until creepers grew over him.
The carving. Kalkuḍa carved it from a single rock, perfect in every line.
The raising. When it came to setting the giant upright, the pāḍdana says Kalkuḍa put his left hand under it, lifted it, set it on its base and stood it up.
Fame. People came from everywhere to see it, and Kārkaḷa’s name spread.

THE KING’S REWARD

Honour, then the blade
A ceremony. The king summoned the sculptor to honour him, one telling says with a silver anklet for his leg and a gold bracelet for his hand.
A fear. But the king could not bear that such a master might carve a greater statue for another kingdom. “I will not let Kalkuḍa, who has worked in my kingdom, work in another country,” the pāḍdana has him say.
The maiming. He had Kalkuḍa’s left arm and right leg cut off.
Left with nothing. Maimed, the sculptor would not even stay to sit or drink water in the king’s land. He left Kārkaḷa.

ZAPPY: The very hand that raised the statue?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, the left. The pāḍdana makes that point on purpose: the king took the hand that made his fame.

TO YĒNŪR

A greater statue with one hand
A new king. Kalkuḍa went to Yēnūr, ruled by Timmaṇṇa Ajila. The king wanted him to work in his kingdom. “Master, I have no hand and no leg; how can I work?” he answered.
He works anyway. But he did. With one hand and one leg, the pāḍdana says, he made at Yēnūr a Gommaṭa two cubits taller than Kārkaḷa’s, and pillars, and an image of himself on a pillar.
Defiance in stone. The maimed artist outdid the work for which he was maimed: the king’s cruelty failed in the very thing it meant to prevent.

The real Veṇūr statue. The Bāhubali of Veṇūr was raised in 1604 under the Ajila chief Timmaṇṇa Ajila IV. It is in fact a little shorter than Kārkaḷa’s, but the pāḍdana’s point is the sculptor’s triumph, not the measurement.

THE SISTER’S OATH

Kallurṭi goes looking
The search. Kallurṭi heard what had happened and went looking for her brother. She learned he had gone to Yēnūr with a single hand and leg.
Her grief. “Rāma, Rāma, Bermetti! Alas my fate! Where shall I see my brother?” she cried.
His words. When she found him he told her: “This did not happen because of theft or falsehood. The king of Kārkaḷa cut off my hand and leg, so that I might not work elsewhere.”
The oath. She stood up, turned to face Kārkaḷa, and swore that she would not leave the king who had maimed her brother unpunished.

TWO DAIVAS

From injustice to power
Beyond death. The brother and sister left human life together. One telling says Kallurṭi made an offering to Śiva at the Mahaliṅgeśvara temple in Veṇūr and leapt into a well, returning in another form.
Śiva’s grant. By another telling, Kallurṭi prayed to Śiva to turn them into spirits, so that they could take their revenge.
Kārkaḷa pays. As daivas, they brought calamity on the king of Kārkaḷa and his land, until he was forced to acknowledge them and give them worship.
Feared and honoured. An early English observer listed them, with Pañjurli, among the most feared daivas of Kanara. Today they are honoured across Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa and Udupi as powers of justice.

ZAPPY: So the daivas are revenge spirits?

PROF. BOLT: Justice spirits. The difference matters on the coast: they don’t harm the innocent, they hold the powerful to account. That’s why ordinary people trust them.

LEGEND AND RECORD

What the stones say
Kārkaḷa BāhubaliAbout 42 ft, consecrated on 13 February 1432
Who ordered itVīra Pāṇḍya Bhairarasa, on the advice of his Jain teacher Lalitakīrti
The sculptorNot named in the inscription; Kalkuḍa’s name comes from tradition
Veṇūr BāhubaliAbout 35 ft, raised in 1604 under the Ajila chiefs
Written downThe pāḍdana was printed in the Indian Antiquary in the 1890s

Two kinds of truth. The inscriptions record who paid for the statues; the pāḍdana remembers who carved them and what it cost him. The pāḍdana’s king, “Bairana Sūḍa”, is the Bhairarasa of the inscriptions in the people’s own speech.

HOW THEY ARE WORSHIPPED

Kōla of the stone-cutters’ daivas
Together and apart. Kalkuḍa and Kallurṭi are often honoured together, but Kallurṭi is also widely paired with Pañjurli, as Kallurṭi–Pañjurli, and sometimes shares a sthāna with Jumādi and Guḷiga.
The kōla. At their kōla the performers sing the pāḍdana of the sculptor and his sister, and as daivas they hear the people’s grievances.
For the wronged. Devotees come to them above all when they have been wronged by those more powerful than themselves.
