KORAGA TANIYA · A DAIVA OF TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

KORAGAJJA

The grandfather who finds what is lost

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Lose your keys in Maṅgaḷūru and someone will tell you to pray to Koragajja. Stuck on a job that won’t finish, a case that won’t close, a child who won’t come home: the same advice.

He was once a boy called Taniya, orphaned, poor, of the Koraga community, kept outside a temple door. Today he is the most called-upon daiva of the coast, and the affectionate “Ajja”, grandfather, of millions.

This guide tells his story as the pāḍdanas and his shrines remember it, and how he is worshipped.

Svāmi Koragajja,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A boy kept outside the temple became the one everyone prays to?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the heart of his story. The coast remembers the one who was turned away, and makes him the one who turns no one away.

ZAPPY: Why so many names?

PROF. BOLT: Taniya was his name as a boy, Koraga his community, Ajja the love people have for him. Each name is a different part of the story.

WHERE HE IS WORSHIPPED

Shrines from Uḷḷāla to Kundāpura

Everywhere, in small. Koragajja rarely has a grand temple. He lives in thousands of small kaṭṭes, stone platforms under trees, in house compounds, at the edge of fields and markets, from Uḷḷāla in the south to Kundāpura in the north.

His first home. The most revered is the Ādisthaḷa, the original seat, at Kuttār, a short way south of Maṅgaḷūru across the Netrāvatī.

Beyond the coast. In recent decades devotees from Tuḷunāḍu have carried his worship to Bengaḷūru, Mumbai and abroad.

THE NAME

Koraga, Taniya, Ajja

🧺 Koraga

The name of his community, the Koragas, one of the oldest peoples of the coast, traditionally makers of baskets and mats from cane and bamboo.

👦 Taniya

His own name as a boy. The daiva is still formally called Koraga Taniya or Koraga Taniya Daiva.

👴 Ajja

Tuḷu for “grandfather”, an old man held in love. Though he became a daiva as a young man, people call him Ajja out of affection.

🙏 Svāmi

“Lord”. Devotees add it as a mark of reverence: Svāmi Koragajja.

ZAPPY: Grandfather, for someone who never grew old?

PROF. BOLT: “Ajja” isn’t about age here. It's what you call the one you can always go to: patient, close, on your side.

THE ORPHAN BOY

Taniya’s beginning
Born poor. The pāḍdana says Taniya was born at Koragara Koppa to his mother Maire (Ācu Maire) and a father from Paṇambūr.
Alone. His mother died when he was a baby, by one telling only thirty days old, and his father soon after. With no relatives left, the child was alone in the world.
Wandering. He wandered until he reached Kallāpu, near Kuttār. There, under a tree, half-mad with grief, he ate sand as if it were rice and a guava as if it were coconut, crying and laughing at once.

ZAPPY: That’s a sad way to start.

PROF. BOLT: It’s meant to be. His story begins at the very bottom, and that is why the people at the bottom have always trusted him most.

THE FOSTER MOTHER

Bairakke’s kindness
A stranger stops. A woman of the Billava community, Bairakke Baiḍedi (also told as Mairakke Baiḍeti), passed that way with her children, Mañjakke and Cenniya, carrying a pot of toddy. She saw the weeping boy and asked who he was.
A home. Hearing his story, she took him in as her own son, giving him food, clothing, shelter and dignity, in her house at Eñjūru Barke.
Luck in the house. Taniya worked for the family out of gratitude. He became a skilled maker of baskets and bamboo ware, and from the day he came, the household prospered.
The pot that never emptied. One day she sent him to market with a pot of toddy on his head. However much was poured from it, it never ran dry. Bairakke then revealed that she had made a harake, a vow, to the fierce goddess Lekkisiri, also called Raktēśvarī, to offer seven measures of coconut kernels and tender coconuts at her shrine.

The basket remembers. At his kōla the performer still carries a basket hung from his shoulder, the craft of his boyhood made into his emblem.

AT THE TEMPLE DOOR

Two tellings of one moment
A load for seven. When the vow fell due, the offering was so heavy it needed seven men to carry it, and no one would take it. Sixteen-year-old Taniya lifted it alone and carried it to the shrine.
Kept outside. At the temple the attendants would not let him in because of his caste, and made him wait outside the wall.
First telling. As he waited he climbed a tree beside the temple’s finial to pick a citrus fruit for his foster mother’s pickle. As he reached for it, the goddess Lekkisiri took him into the divine world.
Second telling. Another version says that, waiting for the festival to begin, he set foot on the temple’s kalaśa, its sacred finial, and the deity within, angered, made him vanish; and from that moment he became a daiva.

ZAPPY: Was it a punishment or a blessing?

PROF. BOLT: The two tellings disagree, and devotees keep both. Either way, the door that shut him out became the door into godhood.

TANIYA BECOMES A DAIVA

The journey of the new god
A daiva is born. Gone from human sight, Taniya appeared again as Koraga Taniya Daiva, a power to be honoured.
To the Goddess of the city. The pāḍdana sends him first to Maṅgaḷādevī in Maṅgaḷūru.
Across the river. He crossed the Netrāvatī at Aḍḍaṇḍa Sudhe and came again to Kallāpu, where he had once sat crying under the tree.
His seat. There, near Kuttār, his chief shrine, the Ādisthaḷa, was established, and his worship spread outward from it.

How a person becomes a daiva. In Tuḷu belief, a life ended by injustice or wonder can leave behind a power that must be honoured. Taniya joins Kōṭi and Cennaya, Siri and others who were once of this coast: people the land made divine. See the series introduction for the daiva tradition.

FRIENDS AND BATTLES

Guḷiga and the Pañjandāya daivas
The pact with Guḷiga. At Burdugōḷi, Koragajja made an oḍambaḍike, a sacred pact, with Guḷiga, the fierce stone-born daiva who once bit Viṣṇu’s finger. The two are honoured together, and Guḷiga is called Guḷigajja at his side.
A call for help. The Pañjandāya daivas of the Baṇṭ landlords were losing their lands to the royal Arasu daivas of Udyāvara. They asked Koragajja for help.
The chase. He confronted the Arasu daivas at Bardakaṭṭe and drove them to Kaṇṇīrtōṭa, where the god Vaidyanātha of Sōmanātha judged in the Pañjandāyas’ favour.
The reward. In return Koragajja received the right to his own kōla at seven places, and was declared the chief daiva of Sārlapaṭṭa.
The seven kōla sitesDekkāḍu, Bōḷya, Dēraḷakaṭṭe, Taḷa, Ujila, Mittagēl and Sōmeśvara

ZAPPY: So the poor boy ends up settling disputes between royal gods?

PROF. BOLT: That’s exactly why people bring him their own disputes today. If he could win the landlords’ case against the kings’ daivas, he can help with yours.

THE KUTTĀR ĀDISTHAḶA

The original seat
Where. At Kuttār, on the Rāṇipura road near Uḷḷāla, south of Maṅgaḷūru: the place the legend calls Kallāpu.
All his family. Here Koragajja is worshipped with Guḷigajja, and with Raktēśvarī, the goddess of the vow; Bermer, the lord of daivas; and the seven Siris. The shrine describes itself as the only place in Tuḷunāḍu where all of them are found together.
Sixteen families. The Ādisthaḷa is tied to sixteen hereditary family lineages, who keep its rites.
The lake. About two furlongs away lies Kannaḍakere, an ancient lake said to contain seven smaller ponds.

KORAGAJJA’S KŌLA

The night he comes
By night. Koragajja’s kōla and his kaṭṭe pūjā are held at night, in dim light, as befits a daiva who keeps watch in the dark.
The performer. The medium who becomes Koragajja carries the basket on his shoulder, with a staff or sword, and moves with the restless energy of the wandering boy.
Speaking. As at every kōla, the daiva speaks through him: hears complaints, gives assurance, and promises help.
Custom. Because the night rites involve offerings of toddy and liquor, by long custom women do not attend the kaṭṭe at night; they may take part in the day’s worship.
Sacred, not a costume. Devotees hold the daiva’s appearance and dress to be for the rite alone; imitating Koragajja outside it is regarded as sacrilege.

ZAPPY: Why only at night?

PROF. BOLT: Daivas are guardians, and guardians work when the village sleeps. His night rites belong to him the way the noon pūjā belongs to the great gods.

WHAT HE IS OFFERED

A boy’s favourite things
Toddy, liquorHis foster mother’s toddy; today often bottled liquor, poured three times
ChakkuliThe crisp spiral rice snack
BīḍaBetel leaf and areca nut, the tāmbūla
CoconutsBroken at the kaṭṭe
Flowers and sandalSandal-paste prasāda is given at three points at the Ādisthaḷa

Simple things. His offerings are the food and drink of an ordinary village boy, not the sweets of temple kitchens: the things Taniya knew and loved in Bairakke’s house.

The vow. Devotees make a harake to Koragajja, promising an offering or a kōla if a prayer is answered, the same kind of vow his foster mother once made.

THE FINDER OF LOST THINGS

Why everyone calls him

🔑 Lost things

A lost ring, a missing document, a strayed cow: the first prayer on the coast is to Koragajja.

⏳ Stuck work

Work that won’t finish, a sale that won’t close, a building that won’t get done.

⚖️ Disputes

Family quarrels, property cases, justice where human authorities fail.

🏠 Home and health

Illness, worries about children, troubles in the house.

Close by. Devotees say what sets Koragajja apart is his approachability. The great gods are distant and grand; Ajja is near, involved in ordinary life, and answers quickly. People of every community and faith on the coast pray to him.

ZAPPY: Is he really that fast?

PROF. BOLT: Ask anyone in Maṅgaḷūru and you'll hear a story. That's how a daiva grows: one answered prayer at a time, told and retold.
