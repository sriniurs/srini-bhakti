KŌṬI AND CENNAYA

The twins who fought for justice

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Twin boys, born on a roadside to a mother who died giving them life. Raised in a king’s house, trained as warriors, they stood up to a cruel minister, and then to the king himself.

Kōṭi and Cennaya fell in battle, but the coast would not let them go. Today they are worshipped in garaḍis all over Tuḷunāḍu, together with the lord of all daivas, Bermer.

This guide tells the twins’ epic and how they are honoured.

Brahma Baidarkaḷa,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Heroes who lost, and became gods?

PROF. BOLT: They lost the battle and won the argument. The coast remembers who was right, not who was left standing.

ZAPPY: What does “Baidarkaḷa” mean?

PROF. BOLT: “The Baidas”, the Billava heroes. Brahma Baidarkaḷa is Bermer, the lord, together with the twin Baidas he protects.

WHERE THEY LIVED

Inland Tuḷunāḍu

Inland country. The twins’ story is set in the hilly interior of Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa, in the old chiefdoms around Puttūr, where local lords, the Baḷḷāḷas, ruled from their manor houses.

From birth to death. They were born at Paḍumale near Puttūr and fell near Eṇmūr, where their great garaḍi now stands as their resting place.

In the city. Their best-known garaḍi in Maṅgaḷūru is at Kaṅkanāḍi.

DEYI BAIḌETI

The mother
A lost girl. The epic begins with a girl abandoned in the forest, her hands tied and her eyes closed. Toddy-tappers of the Billava community found her and gave her shelter.
A healer. She grew up to be Deyi Baiḍeti, a woman wise in herbs and healing, of the Kirodi lineage of the Billavas.
Married. She married and was expecting a child, the twins whose story this is.
Worshipped too. Deyi Baiḍeti is honoured alongside her sons in the garaḍis, the mother of the heroes.

ZAPPY: So the twins’ story is really her story first?

PROF. BOLT: The coast thinks so. Without the healer there are no heroes. In the Kaṅkanāḍi garaḍi she has her own place of worship.

THE HEALER AND THE KING

A thorn, a cure, a birth
A poisoned foot. The lord of Paḍumale, Perumāḷa Baḷḷāḷa, went hunting and stepped on a poisonous thorn. His foot swelled and festered, and no one could cure it.
The cure. Deyi Baiḍeti, heavily pregnant, was called. She treated him with her herbs until he was healed.
A roadside birth. On her way home she gave birth to twin boys, and died.
The king’s debt. Grateful to the woman who had saved him, the Baḷḷāḷa took the orphaned twins under his care.

SUN AND MOON

The twins grow up

☀️ Kōṭi

The elder, named for the sun in one telling. Bold and decisive, the leader of the pair.

🌙 Cennaya

The younger, named for the moon. Fierce and loyal, never apart from his brother.

Their uncle. Their mother’s brother, Sāyana Baida, took charge of them and gave them their education and training.
Warriors. They grew into skilled fighters, masters of sword and shield, and were known to help anyone who came to them for justice.
A threat. Their strength and fearlessness made the powerful uneasy. Many began to see the twins as a danger.

THE MINISTER BUDYANTA

Power that corrupts
A builder rises. A man named Mallaya built the Baḷḷāḷa a magnificent palace. Delighted, the lord gave him the title Budyanta, “the clever one”, and made him minister.
Absolute power. The Baḷḷāḷa withdrew from daily affairs and let Budyanta run the land. The minister grew arrogant and cruel, and oppressed the people.
The twins stand up. Kōṭi and Cennaya refused to bow to his injustice. The clash between the minister and the twins set the epic’s tragedy in motion.

ZAPPY: So the real villain is the minister, not the king?

PROF. BOLT: Budyanta is the wicked one, but the king let him rule. The epic blames both: the man who abused power and the man who handed it over.

EXILE AND SHELTER

Leaving Paḍumale
Driven out. After their quarrel with the minister, the twins could no longer stay in Paḍumale. They left the land of the lord who had raised them.
Wanderers. They travelled from one chiefdom to another, honoured by some, feared by others.
Shelter. At last they took refuge with the lord of Eyyanūr, who welcomed their service.

A map of loyalties. The epic moves through the small kingdoms of the interior, each with its Baḷḷāḷa and its feuds. To its first listeners these were real places with real families, which is part of why the story still matters so much there.

THE LAST BATTLE

Victory and death
War. War broke out between the kingdoms of Pañja and Eyyanūr. The twins fought for Eyyanūr and were winning against a whole army.
Treachery. Then Perumāḷa Baḷḷāḷa of Paḍumale, the lord who had raised them, struck Kōṭi down by a trick.
A promise of peace. Dying, Kōṭi made the three lords, of Eyyanūr, Pañja and Paḍumale, promise to live in peace.
Two deaths. Cennaya could not bear to live without his brother, and ended his own life. They died together near Eṇmūr.

THE GARAḌIS

Gymnasiums become shrines
The name. A garaḍi (also garoḍi) was a gymnasium, a place for training in wrestling and swordplay: the warriors’ own ground.
Their shrines. Memorial shrines for Kōṭi and Cennaya were built as garaḍis all over Tuḷunāḍu, so that the twins are worshipped where young men once trained.
The great ones. The Eṇmūr garaḍi marks their mahā samādhi, where they fell. The Kaṅkanāḍi garaḍi in Maṅgaḷūru is a major centre of the community’s worship.
A community’s pride. For the Billava community the twins are the first great heroes of their history, and the garaḍis are their own sacred places.

BERMER AND THE TWINS

Brahma Baidarkaḷa
Bermer. In the garaḍis the twins are not worshipped alone. They stand with Bermer, Tuḷunāḍu’s Brahmā, lord of the forest and of the daivas.
The twins’ god. Devotees believe the twins themselves brought Bermer’s image to the garaḍi, and worshipped him as their protector.
One name. Together they are called Brahma Baidarkaḷa: Bermer with the Baida heroes.

ZAPPY: So in a garaḍi you pray to Brahmā and the twins together?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. One of the very few places in India where Brahmā gets regular worship, and he shares it with two Billava warriors. That's Tuḷunāḍu.

THE TWINS’ NĒMA

When the heroes return
Two performers. At the twins’ nēma, two performers become Kōṭi and Cennaya, armed with swords and shields, in the costume of warriors.
The story sung. Parts of the epic are sung during the rite, including by the Parava singers.
The heroes speak. As daivas, the twins hear the troubles of those who come, and promise protection to all who have faith in them.
Swords in the garaḍi. The weapons of the nēma are kept in the garaḍi between festivals, honoured like the twins themselves.

THE EPIC

A long song of the coast
One of two. The Kōṭi–Cennaya pāḍdana is counted, with the epic of Siri, as one of the two truly long epics of the Tuḷu language.
Old. References to the twins in Tuḷu pāḍdanas go back some five hundred years; scholars commonly place their lives in the 16th century, though some tellings put them later.
Written down. A version was recorded by British scholars in the 19th century and printed in the Indian Antiquary; modern editions and retellings followed.
On stage and screen. The story has been played as folk drama, as the Tuḷu film Kōṭi Cennaya (a National Award winner, 2007), and as Deyi Baiḍeti (2019), about their mother.
