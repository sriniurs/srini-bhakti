THE SERPENT RITE OF TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

NĀGAMAṆḌALA

A night danced around a serpent drawn on the floor · explained and illustrated

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

HOW THIS WORKS: Nāgamaṇḍala, the all-night serpent rite of Tuḷunāḍu: why it is held, the five-colour floor drawing, the Vaidya as nāgakannikā with the ḍakke, the Brahmin pātri and the areca flower, the night hour by hour and the erasing at dawn.

FOREWORD

In Tuḷunāḍu the land belongs first to the nāgas. Every old house has its serpent grove, every village its serpent stones, and when things go wrong, the nāga is often the first to be asked.

The grandest answer is the Nāgamaṇḍala: a whole day spent drawing a great serpent on the ground in five colours, and a whole night spent dancing around it, until at dawn the drawing is swept away.

This guide follows it from the reason it is held to the last handful of coloured powder.

Nāgadēvarige namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A drawing made just to be destroyed?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the idea. The maṇḍala is a dwelling built for one night. Once the nāga has come and gone, it has done its work.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
WhatAn all-night rite of serpent worship around a great floor drawing, the maṇḍala
WhereDakṣiṇa Kannaḍa, Uḍupi and Kāsaragōḍ, at serpent groves, temples and old family houses
ForThe nāgas, serpent deities, often as Nāga Brahma or Nāga Subrahmaṇya
PerformersThe Vaidya (drawer, singer, drummer, dressed as the female serpent) and the pātri (the medium of the nāga)
InstrumentThe ḍakke, a small bronze hourglass drum
SeasonRoughly December to April, before the rains
LengthA day of drawing, then about six hours of rite through the night
Grandest formThe Aṣṭapavitra Nāgamaṇḍala

ZAPPY: Is it held every year?

PROF. BOLT: Rarely at the same place. Many shrines and families hold it once in years, when a reason arises. A few, like Uḍupi’s Kṛṣṇa Maṭha, keep a fixed cycle.

LAND OF THE NĀGAS

Why the serpent comes first here

The grove. Old houses and villages keep a nāgabana, a patch of uncut forest with carved serpent stones, the nāgakallu. It is not cleared, its trees are not felled, and its nāgas receive milk, turmeric and flowers.

A legend of the land. The coast is told as land won from the sea by Paraśurāma, and the nāgas as its first dwellers. Those who settled were expected to keep them content.

Harm to a serpent. Killing or hurting a snake, or clearing a grove, is believed to bring a nāga dōṣa, a fault that shows up as illness, childlessness or misfortune, until it is made good by ritual.

WHY ONE IS HELD

From a trouble to a date
A trouble or a vow. A family or temple faces a run of misfortune, or has made a vow to the nāga, or is restoring an old grove.
Asking. An astrologer is consulted through a praśna, a ritual enquiry, which may name a nāga dōṣa and prescribe a Nāgamaṇḍala as the remedy.
Consent. The village deity is asked for her nod before the plan goes ahead.
A date. An auspicious day is fixed, often under the star Āśleṣā, which is linked with serpents.
A pandal. A large shelter is built in a field or before the shrine, its pillars dressed with flowers and areca blossom, and the Vaidyas and pātri are engaged.

ZAPPY: Is it only to remove a fault?

PROF. BOLT: No. Many are held to thank the nāga, to bless a new temple or building, or simply for the welfare of the land. The fault is one reason among several.

THE TWO PERFORMERS

WearsRed sari and blouse, a man’s turban, metal anklets and wristletsRed silk dhōti, bare chest, loose hair, unpainted face

StateA performed role, under controlTrance
CommunityThe Vaidya familiesTraditionally Brahmin

Why a man as the female serpent? The mixed dress is read as Ardhanārīśvara, Śiva who is half Pārvatī, and the dance of the pair as the meeting of male and female serpents. Some scholars suggest that men took over a role once held by women.

THE VAIDYAS

Drawers, singers, drummers

🩺 The name

Vaidya means healer. The community was known for traditional cures, especially for the eyes, poisoning and childbirth.

🎨 The hand

Only Vaidyas draw the maṇḍala. The pattern, its proportions and its order of colours pass from father to son.

🥁 The ḍakke

Their small bronze hourglass drum is treated as sacred, offered milk and fruit, and played with the palm to call the nāga.

🙏 Their worship

Gaṇeśa, Viṣṇu as a black stone, the liṅga and the goddess as Ammanavaru; they follow Smārta practice.

ZAPPY: How many Vaidyas are there?

PROF. BOLT: Very few. One study in the 1980s counted fewer than a dozen working Vaidyas in the region, against more than a hundred pātris. A handful of Uḍupi families still carry the art.

THE PĀTRI

The man the nāga enters
A Brahmin medium. Unusually for the coast, where most mediums come from non-Brahmin communities, the nāga’s pātri is traditionally a Brahmin, the role passing from father to son.
Preparing. A successor is often initiated as a young man, around eighteen, and keeps a regular practice of meditation at a serpent shrine.
Out of the rite. Pātris are also consulted for advice, predictions and auspicious dates; some are well-known names whose presence is announced for each Nāgamaṇḍala.

FIVE COLOURS

Each from something that grows or lies close by
YellowTurmeric powder
RedTurmeric mixed with lime, which turns it red
WhiteRice flour, or powdered white stone or earth
GreenPowder of dried green leaves
BlackPaddy husk or rice straw, roasted and powdered

ZAPPY: Why natural colours?

PROF. BOLT: Tradition, and the fact that the drawing ends up in the hands of devotees who carry a pinch home. Everything in it comes from the house and the field.

THE DESIGN

A knot with no way in or out

Reading it. The pattern is two serpent bodies looping through each other in an endless knot, so the eye finds no entrance or exit and is drawn to the five-hooded nāga at the centre. Heads and tails are placed by strict rule.

Sizes of the rite. A smaller, four-sided form is common; a rarer full form has sixteen sides and multiplies the heads. Grand festivals are announced as Aṣṭapavitra Nāgamaṇḍalas, from aṣṭa, eight.

THE DAY OF DRAWING

Morning to dusk
Early morning In the pandal before the shrine, the Vaidya begins the drawing on a prepared floor, laying lines of powder by hand.
Through the day The serpents grow coil by coil. Around the edge the host’s offerings are arranged: heaps of tender areca blossom, tender coconuts, fruit, vegetables and oil lamps.
Day rites At the shrine, priests perform the worship of the nāgas.
By evening The maṇḍala is finished. The Vaidya withdraws to dress as the nāgakannikā.
Dark The lamps around the maṇḍala are lit. Traditionally the night was lit by lamps and torches; some hosts still avoid electric light for the rite.

ZAPPY: Can people walk over it?

PROF. BOLT: No one steps on it during the day. The only person who will walk into it is the Vaidya, at the very end.

THE NIGHT

From the first hymn to the serpent dance
Beginning. Two or three hours after sunset, the Vaidyas sing invocations: Gaṇeśa first, then Śiva, Viṣṇu and Brahmā, then long songs to the goddess as Kāḷī and Durgā, ending in praise of Nāga Subrahmaṇya.
The blossom. The pātri takes sprays of pingāra, areca blossom; water is poured over them, and he presses them to his face again and again. Their scent is said to bring on the trance.
Arrival. He trembles, his eyes widen, his head sways and darts like a startled snake. The nāga is held to have come.
The serpent dance. He moves around the maṇḍala, often backwards, past the lamps. His circling is said to “untie” the knot and bring the drawing to life.
The pair. The nāgakannikā dances with and against him in a game of challenge and praise, the ḍakke setting the pace.

The crowd. People of every caste come, and Muslims attend too. Many listen closely to the opening songs, talk and eat through the long middle, and gather again for the climaxes.

MIDNIGHT TO DAWN

The climax and the sweeping away
Midnight. A priest pours water and turmeric over the pātri. He shudders and hisses, the music swells to its first peak, and all draw back while the nāgakannikā dances alone.
The long song. A key song tells of the nāga’s home in the underworld and its journey, timed to end with the sunrise.
Near dawn. The pātri’s dance ends. Still in the nāga’s presence, he answers devotees, gives advice and blesses, especially those who come for children or for healing.
Erasing. The Vaidya walks into the maṇḍala and sweeps the colours together into a grey heap. Devotees take pinches home as prasāda.

SONGS AND SOUND

What carries the night
LeadThe Vaidya’s voice and his ḍakke
RhythmCymbals, the tāḷa, and the ceṇḍe drum
MelodyThe nāgasvara, a long double-reed pipe
PlayersUsually two or more adult musicians, sometimes with a boy learning beside them
LanguagePraise songs in Kannaḍa and Sanskrit
MethodFixed texts, with singing stretched or quickened to follow the pātri’s state
In printThe Vaidyas’ songs were collected as Vaidyara Hāḍugaḷu, edited by A. V. Navada (Uḍupi, 1985)

ZAPPY: So the Vaidya controls the pace?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The pātri is in trance; the Vaidya watches him and drives or eases the rhythm. Whatever the crowd sees, the drummer is steering the night.

RELATED SERPENT RITES

Smaller and nearby

⭐ Āśleṣā bali

A shorter offering to the nāgas on the day of the Āśleṣā star, with a drawn figure and fire offerings. Famous at Kukke Subrahmaṇya.

🥁 Ḍakke bali

A Vaidya, dressed as Ardhanārī, plays the ḍakke while a pātri, possessed, is led round the place of worship in circles. At the end he rubs bundles of areca blossom on his face to accept all the offerings.

🥛 Nāga Pañcamī

The yearly festival when every household feeds its serpent stones milk and turmeric. See Nāga Pañcamī.

🪷 In Kerala

Neighbouring traditions also draw serpents on the floor: the nāgakaḷam of the Puḷḷuvan singers and the sarpam tuḷḷal, the serpent dance.

BEYOND THE RITE

Where the Nāgamaṇḍala reaches
Theatre With the bhūta kōla, the Nāgamaṇḍala is counted among the ritual roots of Yakṣagāna, the coast’s night-long dance-drama.
1986 David E. R. George’s India: Three Ritual Dance-Dramas gives a full study of the rite and a translation of the Vaidyas’ songs.
Late 1980s Girish Karnad’s play Nāga-Maṇḍala borrows the name for a story of a wife, a husband and a serpent; it draws on folk tales, not on the rite itself.
Uḍupi The Kṛṣṇa Maṭha holds a Nāgamaṇḍala every two years before its Takṣaka bila shrine, the nāgakannikā dressed in an Uḍupi handloom sari.
Today Grand Aṣṭapavitra Nāgamaṇḍalas draw thousands, with a named pātri and a public feast, while small family ones grow rarer.
