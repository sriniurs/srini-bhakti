ĀDI ĀḶADE · A DAIVA OF TUḶUNĀḌU · A REFERENCE GUIDE

SIRI

The princess who walked out and became divine

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

She was born from a flower, married to a lord who insulted her, and cheated of her inheritance by bribed elders. So she cursed his house, set fire to the manor and walked away into the forest.

Siri is Tuḷunāḍu’s great heroine and goddess. Her story is the longest poem in the Tuḷu language, sung by women as they plant rice. On her festival nights, hundreds of women become Siri, her daughter and her granddaughters, and speak with their voices.

This guide tells her epic and her worship.

Ādi Āḷade Siri,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A goddess who walked out on her husband?

PROF. BOLT: In her story she refuses to live with his insult, and her people honour her for it. The coast’s women have sung her courage for centuries.

ZAPPY: Twenty-five hours of singing?

PROF. BOLT: Nobody sings it all at once! Women sing parts in the paddy fields, singers recite parts at the jātre. The whole epic lives spread across a community.

SIRI’S COUNTRY

Udupi and inland Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa

Five great centres. Siri’s worship is spread over many shrines, but five are counted greatest: Hiriyaḍka and Paṅgāla near Uḍupi, Nandaḷike near Kārkaḷa, Kavatār, and Nidgal in Beḷtaṅgaḍi taluk.

Nidgal first. Nidgal is where the story begins, with the ancestral temple her father restored.

BORN OF FLOWERS

A gift from Bermer
An old lord’s grief. Bermaṇṇa Āḷva, an ageing Baṇṭ lord of Satyanāpura, had lost his wife and his only daughter. With no heir, he prayed to Bermer.
A disguised god. Bermer came to him dressed as a Brahmin and told him that his sorrow came from neglecting the temple of his family god, lying in ruins at Nidgal.
The prasāda. Bermaṇṇa restored the temple and performed pūjā. He brought home its prasāda: areca flowers and sandal paste.
A baby. Overnight the flowers turned into a baby girl. He named her Siri, “prosperity”.

ZAPPY: Born from areca flowers?

PROF. BOLT: A gift-child, straight from Bermer’s temple. On this coast areca flowers are used in every rite; here, a whole goddess grows from them.

THE INSULT

A sari worn first by another
A marriage. Siri grew up beautiful. Kānta Pūñja, a minor lord of Basrūr, married her.
The sari. On his way home with a sari he had bought for his pregnant wife, he visited a courtesan, Siddu, who wore the sari first.
Siri refuses. At the ceremony for her pregnancy Siri recognised it and refused to wear a sari another woman had worn first. Kānta Pūñja never forgave her.
Kumāra. She gave birth to a son, Kumāra, at her father’s house. Her husband and his mother sent no word.

ZAPPY: She turned down a gift from her husband?

PROF. BOLT: She turned down an insult dressed as a gift. That one refusal is the turning point of the whole epic.

FIRE AND CURSE

Cheated of her inheritance
Her father dies. When Bermaṇṇa died, the question of who would succeed him came before the elders.
Bribed elders. Kānta Pūñja joined with Aṇṇu Śeṭṭi, and the elders, bribed, ruled against Siri.
The curse. Siri cursed her husband’s lands and clan. By her power she set fire to the manor, Majalūṭṭu Bīḍu, now in her rival’s hands.
Leaving. Then she walked away from Satyanāpura, with her baby Kumāra and her maid Dāru, toward the forest of Bola.

THE ROAD TO BOLA

Loss and a new life
Wonders on the way. On the road to Bola Siri performed many miracles, the epic says, showing her divine power.
Loss. On that journey she lost both Kumāra and Dāru. In some tellings Kumāra dies; in others he vanishes, after foretelling that his mother would marry again and have a daughter.
A foster sister. At Bola the twin lords of the land took her in as their sister and arranged her marriage to Kōḍsar Āḷva of Kotrāḍi.
Sonne. She bore a daughter, Sonne. Before she died, Siri promised blessing and healing to all who would worship her.

ZAPPY: So Siri dies too?

PROF. BOLT: She leaves human life, as every daiva of the coast does, and comes back as a power. Her last promise is why people still call on her.

SONNE AND THE TWINS

A broken vow
A childless daughter. Sonne married Guru Marla, but they had no children. They vowed that if children came they would be dedicated to Bermer.
Twin girls. Twin daughters were born: Abbaga and Dāraga. The family forgot their vow.
The warning. Bermer came again in disguise to remind them. They paid no heed.
The board game. While the parents were away, Bermer opened for the girls a game of cenne, the coast’s pit-and-seed board game. The sisters quarrelled over it, and one struck the other dead with the board; the other then threw herself into a well.
The boon withdrawn. Bermer declared that because the vow had been broken, the gift had been taken back.

The sisters are honoured together as daivas, and a carved pair called Abbaga–Dāraga is among the best-known images of the Siri cult.

SIRI’S DIVINE FAMILY

Four generations
BermerThe god who gave Siri and took back the twins
SiriThe princess, mother of the line
KumāraHer son, lost on the road
SonneHer daughter from her second marriage
Abbaga and DāragaSonne’s twin daughters
DāruSiri’s faithful maid

The whole line. At the Siri shrines it is not one goddess but the whole family who are honoured: Siri, Kumāra, Sonne, Abbaga, Dāraga and their companions. At Koragajja’s Ādisthaḷa they appear too, as the seven Siris.

Siri’s shrines

NandaḷikeKārkaḷa taluk
KavatārA centre with old carved images of the Siri family
NidgalBeḷtaṅgaḍi taluk, where Bermaṇṇa restored his family temple

Āḷade. Siri’s temples are called Ādi Āḷade, “the first Āḷade”, the original seats of her worship. Many smaller shrines across the coast derive from them.

THE SIRI JĀTRE

The night of possession
A full-moon night. Each Ādi Āḷade holds its yearly Siri jātre, a night-long festival.
Darśana. Women, often hundreds at a time, enter trance and are possessed by Siri, Sonne, Abbaga and Dāraga. This is called darśana; the possession festival is also called dayyol.
Kumāra. A man takes the part of Kumāra, Siri’s son, and acts as the mediator between the possessed women and the people, and as a healer.
The song. Parts of the Siri pāḍdana are sung through the night in a solemn ritual style.
Across lines. Women of many castes and communities take part. Siri’s cult is known for cutting across the usual divisions of the coast.

THE LONGEST TUḶU POEM

The Siri pāḍdana
Its size. The Siri pāḍdana, also called Siri Sandhi, runs to 15,683 lines, the longest poem in Tuḷu. A full recitation takes about 25 hours.
Sung in the fields. Women traditionally sang it while transplanting paddy, verse by verse, through the long days of the monsoon planting.
One singer’s version. The great recorded version was sung by Gōpāla Nāyka and published with an English translation by the Finnish folklorist Lauri Honko and colleagues in 1998.
A legendary singer. The pāḍdana singer Kargi, who herself received Siri’s darśana all her life, died on the full-moon night of the Hiriyaḍka Siri jātre in 1994. Newspapers called her “the queen of pāḍdana singers”.

WHY WOMEN SING SIRI

A heroine of the coast

✊ She refused

She would not wear a sari soiled by her husband’s betrayal, nor accept a ruling bought with bribes.

🚶 She left

She walked away from a marriage and a home that wronged her, carrying her child.

💐 She began again

She made a new life, a new marriage and a new family, and became a mother of goddesses.

🙏 She protects

She promised help and healing to all who call on her, and women above all trust her.

ZAPPY: Is that why women go into trance as Siri?

PROF. BOLT: Scholars and devotees both say so: for one night a year, a woman can speak with the voice of a goddess who stood up for herself.
