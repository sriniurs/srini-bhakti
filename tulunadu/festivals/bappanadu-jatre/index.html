Bappanāḍu Jātre

The Drums of the River Goddess · explained and illustrated

The drums of the river Goddess

HOW THIS WORKS: The Bappanāḍu Jātre: the seven Durgās and the coconut, Bappa’s red river, eight days of festival, prasāda at Bappa’s door, and the great ḍōlu.

WHERE WE ARE

On the bank of the Śāmbhavī at Mulki stands a Goddess who chose her devotees widely: she stayed for a toddy-tapper’s coconut, called a Muslim trader in a dream to build her house, and had a Jain king raise it. Every April her chariot festival fills the town, her palanquin stops at the trader’s descendants’ door with prasāda, and her great drum is beaten so loud the coast has a saying about it.

This is the Bappanāḍu Jātre. This guide covers the seven Durgās and their sandalwood boat, the tender coconut, Bappa Beary’s red river, the eight days of the festival, the chariots, the prasāda for Bappa’s family, and the Bappanāḍu ḍōlu.

The temple is in Bappanāḍu Durgāparameśvarī. Previous: Koḍi Habba.

ZAPPY: A Muslim trader and a Goddess?

PROF. BOLT: The coast’s favourite story about itself. Every year the festival acts it out again at his family’s door.

ZAPPY: Is the drum played every day?

PROF. BOLT: Its great days are the festival. The rest of the year it stands at the entrance like a guardian.

A Goddess, a trader, and a drum.

MULKI

Rivers, sea and a moving shore

The temple. On the bank of the Śāmbhavī at Bappanāḍu, in Mulki town, beside the highway from Maṅgaḷūru to Uḍupi.

Two rivers. Tradition places the Goddess’s first appearance at the meeting of the Śāmbhavī and the Nandinī; the Nandinī is the river of Kaṭīl.

A shifting shore. European travellers in 1516 and 1623 found the area of today’s Mulki under water; later shifts of the river made the land the town stands on.

The Sāvantas. A Jain family, the Sāvantas, ruled the Mulki region from the 14th to the 18th century; the family still lives at Paḍupaṇambūr.

Uḷḷāḍi. Tuḷuvas call the Goddess Uḷḷāḍi, “the Lady”.

ZAPPY: The town wasn’t always there?

PROF. BOLT: The rivers moved it into being. The Goddess was found in the water, and the land came later.

A Goddess found where the rivers meet.

STORY · THE SEVEN DURGĀS

Dārikāsura and Bhadrakāḷī

The story

The story behind the Goddess of Bappanāḍu, as in our temple guide.

The demon Dārikāsura, with a boon from Brahmā, defeated the gods and even Viṣṇu, and gave Viṣṇu’s weapons to his wife to keep.
The Goddess appeared as seven women, the Sapta Durgās, and promised to destroy him. With them went the daiva Guḷiga.
One sister, Bhagavatī, came to the palace as an old woman begging for food and won the weapons from the demon’s wife.
After seven days of battle Dārika fled to the underworld.
Bhadrakāḷī found him there at evening, worshipping Śiva, and killed him. The same myth is the great story of the Goddess temples of Kerala.

ZAPPY: Seven sisters, and one of them is Bappanāḍu’s?

PROF. BOLT: Bhagavatī, the one who stayed behind on this coast. The next page tells why.

Seven sisters against one demon.

STORY · THE BOAT AND THE COCONUT

Why she stayed at Mulki

The story

The voyage of the sisters, as told at Bappanāḍu.

After the victory, Bhagavatī, her sisters and Guḷiga asked Viṣṇu for a boat to go down to earth; he gave them a sandalwood tree, and they made their boat from it.
It sailed north up the coast, past Kāsaragōḍ, Kumbḷe, Mañjēśvara, Uḷḷāla and Kudroḷi, to Sasihitlu near Mulki. (Another telling has them walk north from Kanyākumārī.)
At Mulki a toddy-tapper offered Bhagavatī a tender coconut, and she drank it.
In one telling her sisters would not travel on with her after she accepted a drink from a man of low caste; they went on without her.
She declared the man her devotee, chose to stay, and took the form of a liṅga in the earth between the rivers, unseen, until she was found.

ZAPPY: So the Goddess stayed for the one who served her?

PROF. BOLT: That is the shape of the story. She stayed for a toddy-tapper’s coconut, and later chose a Muslim trader to build her house.

She stayed where she was welcomed.

STORY · BAPPA’S BOAT

The trader and the red river

The story

The founding of the temple, as tradition tells it.

Bappa Beary, a Muslim merchant, often said to be from Kerala, was carrying goods up the Śāmbhavī.
Near Mulki his boat would not move, and he saw the water around it turn red as blood.
He slept in the boat. The Goddess appeared in a dream, told him there is one God for the whole world, and asked him to have a temple built through the king of Mulki, with Baila Uḍupa as priest. She promised his name would be remembered for ever.
Bappa gave his wealth; the Jain Mulki Sāvanta built the temple; the liṅga was found under the water and installed.
Bappa settled nearby, and the place became Bappanāḍu, “Bappa’s land”.

ZAPPY: A Muslim, a Jain and a Brahmin build a Devī temple?

PROF. BOLT: Each with his part. That is why the temple is held up as a symbol of the coast living together.

Her promise: his name for ever.

THE EIGHT DAYS

From the flag to the great chariot

Day 1. Dhvajārōhaṇa, the flag raised, on Mīna śuddha caturdaśī. Day 2. Bali utsava and Āyana dīpōtsava.

Day 3. Pēṭe savāri, the Goddess’s procession to the market (pēṭe). Day 4. Koppaḷa savāri.

Day 5. Bakimāru dīpōtsava. Day 6. Kere dīpōtsava, lamps at the tank.

Day 7. A morning chariot; at night the Candramaṇḍala chariot and śayanōtsava, the Goddess put to rest.

Day 8. The doors opened (kavāṭa), and at night the great Brahma Rathōtsava; then the closing bath. The temple has several chariots for these days.

ZAPPY: What are Koppaḷa and Bakimāru?

PROF. BOLT: Names of places or rites of the festival whose meaning we could not confirm. We give them as the temple does.

Eight days that end in the great chariot.

PRASĀDA FOR BAPPA’S FAMILY

The palanquin at the trader’s door

The custom. During the festival, as the Goddess’s palanquin reaches the house of Bappa Beary’s descendants, the priest hands them gandha prasāda (sandal paste) and flowers before going on.

Since Bappa’s time. The family says the custom has run since Bappa’s own time; the family in turn offers flowers and fruit to the Goddess.

Kept in hard times. In 2022, amid tensions on the coast, the temple management kept the custom and placed no restrictions on non-Hindus at the fair.

“Our village’s fair.” Local residents of all communities call the jātre the fair of the whole village.

Her promise kept. The Goddess promised Bappa his name would be remembered for ever; the prasāda at his family’s door is that promise, kept every year. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: Is Bappa’s story history or legend?

PROF. BOLT: The records are thin. A 1607 copperplate names a Bappa; who he was is debated. The custom at the door is very real.

A promise kept at a door every year.

THE BAPPANĀḌU ḌŌLU

The drum the whole coast knows

The drum. An outsize ḍōlu stands by the temple’s main entrance; it has been shown as a heritage piece of Tuḷunāḍu at the Karāvaḷi Utsav in Maṅgaḷūru.

Known everywhere. The beating of the Bappanāḍu ḍōlu is famous all over Tuḷunāḍu.

The drummers. Festival drumming is the hereditary right of a particular community, to whom the temple pays an honorarium.

The saying. “Go and beat the ḍōlu at Bappanāḍu” is a Tuḷu taunt for someone good for nothing, sent off to do noisy, useless work.

A great rite. A scholar of Tuḷu ritual lists the Bappanāḍu ḍōlu among the seven great ritual events of Tuḷunāḍu.

ZAPPY: A drum famous enough to have its own insult?

PROF. BOLT: That is fame. Every child on the coast knows what you mean.

A drum loud enough to become a proverb.

THE CHARIOT NIGHT

Brahma Rathōtsava

The great night. On the last night the Goddess rides in the Brahma Ratha, the temple’s largest and most decorated chariot, drawn through the night by the crowds.

Other chariots. The temple keeps several chariots: smaller ones for the morning chariot and the Candramaṇḍala (“moon-disc”) chariot of the seventh night.

2025. During the Brahma Rathōtsava in April 2025, the decorated upper part of the great chariot toppled. People moved away in time and no one was seriously hurt; the procession continued in a smaller chariot and all rites were completed.

Avabhṛtha. The festival ends with the closing bath of the festival image.

The fair. Around it, a fair of about three weeks.

ZAPPY: Was the 2025 accident taken as a bad omen?

PROF. BOLT: The rites went on, in another chariot. What people remember is that no one was hurt.

The Goddess rides through the night.
