Kaṭīl Festivals

The Island Goddess Through the Year · explained and illustrated

The fire play of Kaṭīl

HOW THIS WORKS: Kaṭīl’s year: the jātre from Mēṣa saṅkramaṇa, the Tūtedhāra fire play, the holy months, and the Kaṭīl Mela with its vow shows.

WHERE WE ARE

The Goddess of Kaṭīl sits on a rock in the middle of a river. Once a year, at her spring festival, the villages on the two banks gather in her courtyard and pelt each other with burning palm fronds; when the fire is spent, they go home as friends. Through the rest of the year her troupes of actors travel the coast, playing her stories all night wherever a family has vowed a show to her.

This guide is the festival year of Kaṭīl Durgāparameśvarī: the eight-day jātre from Mēṣa saṅkramaṇa, the utsavabali and the chariots, the Tūtedhāra fire play of Attūr and Koḍettūr, the flower month of Siṃha, Navarātri, the Nandinī’s day, and the Kaṭīl Mela with its vow shows.

The temple and its legends are in Kaṭīl Durgāparameśvarī; her worship in a lamp in Durgā Namaskāra. Previous: Poḷali Jātre.

ZAPPY: Is the island reachable?

PROF. BOLT: By a footbridge from the bank. The river parts above the shrine and joins again below it.

A Goddess on a rock who loves fire and theatre.

THE ISLAND

The middle of the land

The river parts. The Nandinī divides into two streams just above the shrine and joins again below it. The sanctum stands on the rock between, reached by a footbridge.

The name. Kaṭi (“middle”) + iḷa (“land”): Kaṭīl, the land in the middle. Tradition says it marks the midpoint of the Nandinī’s course from Kanakagiri to the sea.

Two banks, two villages. The banks belong to Attūr and Koḍettūr. Once a year they meet in the courtyard in the fire play.

The Goddess. A self-manifest stone, shaped like a liṅga, dressed each day as the Mother.

Read more. The full guide: Kaṭīl Durgāparameśvarī.

ZAPPY: Why does the fire play need two villages?

PROF. BOLT: Because the river made two. The festival brings the two banks together, once a year, in her courtyard.

Two banks, one Goddess between them.

STORY · NANDINĪ

The cow’s daughter who became a river

The story

From the legend of Kaṭīl, as in our temple guide.

A terrible drought struck the land. The sage Jābāli went to heaven to ask for Nandinī, daughter of Kāmadhenu, the wish-giving cow, so that sacrifices could bring rain.
Nandinī refused to come down to an earth of sinners. Jābāli cursed her to go there as a river.
She prayed to the Devī for release. The Goddess promised that Nandinī would flow as a holy river, and that she herself would rise in its middle.
Nandinī burst out at Kanakagiri as a river and the rains returned.
In the middle of her stream, the Goddess rose from a rock: Kaṭīlamma. Kaṭīl keeps the river’s birthday on Māgha pūrṇimā.

ZAPPY: And the bees?

PROF. BOLT: That is her other story: Aruṇāsura, whose boon was proof against two- and four-footed creatures, killed by six-legged bees. It is told in our temple guide.

A curse that became a blessing for the land.

THE JĀTRE

Eight days from Mēṣa saṅkramaṇa

Timing. The annual jātre begins on the eve of Mēṣa saṅkramaṇa, the Tuḷu new year of Bisu in mid-April, and runs for about eight days.

Every day. The festival image is taken out for utsavabali (see next page), with drums and lamps.

Chariots. Kaṭīl has a silver chariot for the Goddess’s smaller processions and wooden chariots for the great rathōtsava.

Food. The kitchen feeds thousands daily; at Mēṣa saṅkramaṇa the festival image is worshipped in the dining hall before heaps of rice and coconuts.

The fire. Near the end comes the Tūtedhāra, the fire play, the festival’s best-known rite.

ZAPPY: The festival starts on the new year?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess opens the Tuḷu year with her own festival. The coast’s spring belongs to its goddesses.

The Tuḷu year opens with her festival.

UTSAVABALI

Offerings to the guardians

What it is. Utsavabali (or śrībhūtabali) is the festival form of a daily temple rite: offerings of cooked rice are placed on the balipīṭhas, small stones round the temple that seat the deity’s attendants and guardians.

Carried round. In coastal temples a priest carries the festival image on his head, often dancing to the drums, round the temple as the offerings are made.

Why. Before the Goddess celebrates, every power in her household is fed: the guardians of the directions, her attendants, the bhūtas.

Daily in the jātre. At Kaṭīl it is held every day of the festival.

A coastal style. The image-dance on the priest’s head is a striking feature of Tuḷu and Kerala temple festivals. (General description.)

ZAPPY: Why feed the guardians first?

PROF. BOLT: A good host feeds the household before the guests. The Goddess’s court eats first.

Every power in her house is fed first.

TŪTEDHĀRA

The fire play of Attūr and Koḍettūr

The story

The Tūtedhāra (agni kēḷi) of the Kaṭīl jātre, as described in our guide and in press reports.

After the Goddess’s abhiṣeka and the special sevās of the day, men of Attūr and Koḍettūr gather in the temple courtyard, bare-chested in white dhotis.
Bundles of dried palm fronds, tūte, are lit.
The two sides throw the burning bundles at each other across the courtyard, in front of the sanctum. The bundles are thrown, not held.
The players take the sparks on their bodies as her blessing. Participants say injuries are rare, and are treated not with medicine but with the temple’s kuṅkuma.
When the fire is spent, the two villages leave as friends. The rite is believed to cleanse sins and please the Goddess, who delights in an offering of fire.

ZAPPY: Throwing fire at your neighbours sounds dangerous!

PROF. BOLT: It is a rite, not a riot. Generations have played it the same way, and the villagers say the Mother herself keeps the burns away.

Fire given to the Goddess and to each other.

WHY FIRE

Readings of the Tūtedhāra

Fire for a fiery Goddess. Durgā is a fierce, protecting Goddess; many say she “delights” in fire. The play is an offering suited to her.

Purification. Participants describe the fire as cleansing: sins and ill will burn away. (Belief of participants.)

Rivalry made holy. Two neighbouring villages could easily have been rivals. The rite lets them fight once a year, under her eyes, by her rules, and part as friends. (Interpretation.)

Courage. Taking sparks on the body without flinching is a test of nerve and faith, like walking on coals in other festivals.

Kin across the coast. Fire rites at temple festivals are found elsewhere on the coast too; Kaṭīl’s is the best known.

ZAPPY: Could it ever be banned?

PROF. BOLT: It has been watched and filmed for years and goes on. The villages guard their right to it closely.

A rivalry burnt away each year.

SIṂHA MĀSA AND NAVARĀTRI

The Goddess’s holy months

Siṃha māsa. The solar month of Siṃha (August–September) is the Goddess’s holy month at Kaṭīl. From Siṃha saṅkramaṇa special flower worship is offered.

Navarātri. The nine nights of the Goddess draw huge crowds; the temple stays open late. See Navarātri · 16 articles.

Māgha pūrṇimā. The day the Nandinī first flowed: the river’s own festival.

Every Friday and Tuesday. The Goddess’s weekly days bring their own crowds.

Durgā Namaskāra. Families of the coast often vow the lamp worship of Durgā to her. See Durgā Namaskāra.

ZAPPY: Why a flower month in the monsoon?

PROF. BOLT: Because the rains make the coast bloom. She is offered what the season gives.

Flowers in the rains, lamps at Navarātri.

THE KAṬĪL MELA

The Goddess’s own theatre

The troupe. The Kaṭīl Śrī Durgāparameśvarī Prasādita Daśāvatāra Yakṣagāna Maṇḍaḷi, the Kaṭīl Mela, was founded in the mid-19th century and plays in the Teṅkutiṭṭu style of the south.

Seven troupes. One troupe for a century; more from the 1980s as demand grew. A seventh began on 16 November 2025, making Kaṭīl the only organisation with so many. Each troupe plays about 180 shows a year.

The tour. The troupes set out after the rains, around late November, and return at the end of the season before Pattanāje. On the first and last days the shows run overnight before the Goddess.

Her favourite play. The most requested story is Śrī Devī Mahātme, the Goddess’s own glory, from the Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts.

Children too. The Śrī Durgā Makkaḷa Mela trains children in every part of Yakṣagāna.

ZAPPY: Why does a temple run a theatre company?

PROF. BOLT: Because the coast offers plays the way others offer flowers. Kaṭīl simply needed more flowers than anyone.

The coast offers plays as others offer flowers.

THE VOW SHOW

Harake Yakṣagāna

The vow. When a family’s prayer to Kaṭīlamma is answered, it vows a night of Yakṣagāna to her: a harake (also called bayalāṭa sevā).

Where. The Mela comes to the family’s own village or town and plays without tickets for anyone who comes.

She comes too. It is believed the Goddess herself comes to the place where her Mela plays.

Booked for years. So many families vow shows that bookings run years ahead; in one report the six troupes were booked several seasons in advance.

All night or shorter. Shows were traditionally all-night; noise rules led to shorter formats for some years, and the Mela has since returned to all-night shows in season. (Practice varies.)

ZAPPY: A thank-you gift of a whole play?

PROF. BOLT: A whole night of her stories, for the whole village. It is the coast’s grandest way of saying thank you.

A thank-you the whole village shares.
