Koḍi Habba

The Sugarcane Chariot of Kōṭeśvara · explained and illustrated

The fair of the newly-weds

HOW THIS WORKS: The Koḍi Habba of Kōṭeśvara: King Vasu’s vow, the great tank, the newly-weds and their sugarcane, the chariot and the fair.

WHERE WE ARE

For a few days every November or December, the roads into a small town south of Kundāpura disappear behind walls of sugarcane. Newly married couples in their wedding silks bathe in a great tank, circle it, worship Śiva together, and go home carrying a pair of canes. A king once vowed a temple here for the gift of children; when his chariot was not ready, his people made one of bamboo and sugarcane.

This is the Koḍi Habba of Kōṭeśvara, “the fair of the newly-weds”. This guide covers the Lord of a crore, King Vasu’s vow, the great tank and its rites, the chariot, the couples and their sugarcane, and the fair.

The temple is in Kōṭeśvara Kōṭiliṅgeśvara. Previous: Kaṭīl festivals.

ZAPPY: A festival for newly-weds?

PROF. BOLT: For newly-weds, and for everyone who remembers being one. Every couple of the area seems to have a Koḍi Habba photo.

PROF. BOLT: No, the whole coast comes. But couples are its heart.

A Lord who blesses new families.

KŌṬEŚVARA

Near the meeting of river and sea

Where. Kōṭeśvara lies on the coastal plain a few kilometres south of Kundāpura, a town surrounded by water on three sides where the Pañcagaṅgāvaḷi reaches the sea.

Basrūr. Up the river lies the old port of Basrūr, whose rulers are linked with the temple by inscriptions and by the story of King Vasu.

A Muktisthala. Kōṭeśvara is one of the seven sacred places of Paraśurāma’s coast, with Kumbhāśi and Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa nearby. See The Seven Muktisthalas.

Old name. The town was once called Dhvajapura, “the town of the banner”.

The name of the fair. Explanations of “Koḍi” vary: some connect it with the flag (koḍi) hoisted to open the festival, recalling Dhvajapura. (Traditions differ.)

ZAPPY: Dhvajapura and a flag festival?

PROF. BOLT: Two old names that both speak of banners. Whatever the origin, banners have never been short at Koḍi Habba.

A town of banners by the river mouth.

THE LORD OF A CRORE

Kōṭiliṅgeśvara

The name. Kōṭi is a crore, ten million. Tradition says countless sages, a crore of them, did penance here, and Śiva appeared to every one: the place became Kōṭi-īśvara, Kōṭeśvara.

The liṅga. Śiva is worshipped as a liṅga dressed with a silver face of the Lord.

The hidden liṅgas. Below the pedestal, it is said, lies a well holding about a thousand small liṅgas under water.

Brahmā. Another tradition says Brahmā himself did penance here.

A blessing Lord. He is the Lord who answered King Vasu’s prayer for children; couples come to him for the same blessing.

ZAPPY: You can’t see the thousand liṅgas?

PROF. BOLT: No. You worship one, and by faith you worship them all.

One Lord who appears to everyone at once.

Shrines the couples visit

STORY · KING VASU’S VOW

The chariot of sugarcane

The story

The story behind the Koḍi Habba, as told at the temple.

Mahārāja Vasu, a ruler of Basrūr, had no children for many years.
He vowed to Kōṭiliṅgeśvara that if he were blessed with a child he would build the Lord a temple at Kōṭeśvara.
Children were born to him, and he built the temple as he had promised.
When the festival came, the temple chariot was not finished. Rather than let the Lord go without his procession, a chariot was made of bamboo and sugarcane, and the Lord was drawn round on it.
The Koḍi Habba remembers that sugarcane chariot, and couples still come to the Lord who answered a king’s prayer for children.

ZAPPY: Did the sugarcane chariot hold up?

PROF. BOLT: Long enough, says the story. Devotion was the real frame.

A king’s vow, a chariot of cane.

KŌṬI TĪRTHA

The great tank

The largest. Beside the temple lies Kōṭi Tīrtha, a tank of about four acres, described as the largest temple tank (puṣkariṇī) in Karnataka.

Its shape. Steps run down on all four sides to the water, with a small maṇḍapa in the middle. Fish swim in it.

Its power. Its water is believed to heal.

At the festival. Devotees, and especially couples, bathe in its waters and walk round it in the rite known locally as suttakki sēve before going into the temple.

A tunnel tale. Local legend says an underground passage runs from the tank to Vaṇḍāru Kambaḷa Gadde, a race field some twenty kilometres away.

ZAPPY: Why walk round the tank?

PROF. BOLT: Circling is how you honour anything sacred on this coast: a deity, a tree, a tank. You keep it on your right and go round.

A bath and a circle before the Lord.

THE NEWLY-WEDS

The heart of the fair

Who comes. Couples married in the past year come from all over the coast, often in their wedding silks, sometimes with both families.

What they do. They bathe at Kōṭi Tīrtha, circle it, worship Kōṭiliṅgeśvara together and watch the chariot.

The canes. They take home a pair of sugarcane stalks, believed to bless them with healthy children, as the Lord blessed King Vasu.

Not only couples. Many families buy canes too, as prasāda for the house.

A rite of the new household. For many coastal couples, the Koḍi Habba is the first pilgrimage of married life. (Our observation.)

ZAPPY: Why do couples take home two sugarcanes, not one?

PROF. BOLT: A pair for a pair! One for each of them, together, for the children they hope will come.

A pair of canes for a new pair.

SUGARCANE EVERYWHERE

Walls of cane on every road

The market. In the days of the fair the roads into Kōṭeśvara are lined with stacks of sugarcane, brought in by growers and sellers from the region.

Bought in pairs. Couples and families buy canes in pairs; the sight of thousands carrying cane home is the signature of the fair.

Why sugarcane. It recalls the sugarcane chariot of King Vasu. Sugarcane is also an old sign of sweetness, fertility and plenty in Indian rites. (Interpretation.)

Kāma’s bow. In the old poets, the god of love carries a bow of sugarcane; a fair of newly-weds with sugarcane fits well. (Interpretation.)

Season. Vṛścika (Nov–Dec) is also when sugarcane is harvested in much of Karnataka.

ZAPPY: What do people do with all that cane?

PROF. BOLT: Chew it, share it, offer it at the home shrine. Prasāda you can eat for a week.

The sweetest reminder of an answered prayer.

THE CHARIOT

The Brahmaratha on the car street

The festival. The Koḍi Habba opens with the hoisting of the temple flag and builds to the chariot day.

The Brahmaratha. The festival image of Kōṭiliṅgeśvara is drawn round the car street on the great chariot by thousands of devotees.

Its company. Folk dancers, drums, music and fireworks go with it.

Remembering Vasu. Today’s chariot is wood; the festival remembers the day it had to be bamboo and cane. The bamboo chariot is described as the hallmark of the festival.

Rathōtsava. On the coast a temple’s annual festival is often simply called its rathōtsava, its chariot festival. See also Uḍupi’s chariots.

ZAPPY: How many pull the chariot?

PROF. BOLT: As many as can reach a rope. Pulling is an offering; nobody wants to miss it.

Thousands of hands on one rope.

THE FAIR

A great fair of the coast

Lakhs. The Koḍi Habba draws lakhs of people from across coastal Karnataka and beyond; it is counted among the most eagerly awaited fairs of the coast.

Stalls. Sweets, toys, bangles, kitchenware, rides: the fair spreads through the town.

Seasonal. It falls early in the coast’s festival season, after Dīpāvali, when the jātres and kōlas have just reopened (see The Tuḷu Year).

Other festivals. Mahāśivarātri and Kārtika lamps are the temple’s other great days.

Around. Pilgrims often go on to Kumbhāśi’s Ānegudde Vināyaka and to Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa.

ZAPPY: Is it noisy?

PROF. BOLT: It’s a coastal fair. Very.

One of the great fairs of the coast.
