Poḷali Jātre

The Month-Long Fair and the Ball Game · explained and illustrated

The ball game of the Goddess

HOW THIS WORKS: The Poḷali Jātre: the clay Rājarājeśvarī and King Suratha, a month-long fair, and the Ceṇḍu, the ball game played as a rite.

WHERE WE ARE

For a month every spring, a village fifteen kilometres from Maṅgaḷūru turns into a fair. In the middle of it a goddess of clay, nine feet tall, receives the whole coast; and near the end, two villages play five days of football in front of her temple with a leather ball stitched by a cobbler family who have made it for generations.

This is the Poḷali Jātre. This guide covers the clay Rājarājeśvarī and King Suratha who is said to have made her, the month from Mīna saṅkramaṇa to the final bath, the Siṃhāsana Kaṭṭe, and above all the Poḷali Ceṇḍu: who makes the ball, who carries it, how the game goes and what it means.

The temple is in Poḷali Rājarājeśvarī; King Suratha’s story in Devī Māhātmya. Previous: Lakṣa Dīpotsava.

ZAPPY: Football as worship?

PROF. BOLT: The Goddess likes a good game. And every family that touches the ball has a role in her service.

ZAPPY: How big is the crowd?

PROF. BOLT: Up to about five hundred have joined the game; the watchers are many more.

A month-long fair, a five-day game.

POḶALI

Hills, paddy and the Phalguṇī

The setting. Poḷali lies in Kariyaṅgaḷa village of Baṇṭvāḷa tālūk, ringed by low hills and paddy fields.

The river. The Phalguṇī (Gurupura) flows past the north side of the temple and turns south behind it.

The field. Before the temple lies the open ground where the Ceṇḍu is played, with the platform called the Sultān Kaṭṭe at its edge.

Two villages. The game is played between the villages on either side of the temple.

Old names. The place was Pural; inscriptions call the goddess Poraḷa Dēvi. The game is also called Pural da Ceṇḍu, “the ball of Pural”.

ZAPPY: Is Poḷali a town?

PROF. BOLT: A village, with one of the oldest goddesses of the coast and, for a month, one of its biggest fairs.

A village goddess with a field before her.

RĀJARĀJEŚVARĪ OF CLAY

The queen of kings

The name. Rājarājeśvarī, “Sovereign of the King of Kings”, is a name of Lalitā Tripurasundarī after her coronation. See Śrīvidyā.

Of clay. She is a seated image about nine to ten feet tall, made of clay from different soils mixed with herbal ingredients. A clay image of this size is described as probably the only one of its kind in Karnataka.

Lēpāṣṭagandha. About every twelve years the images receive a fresh coat of earth mixed with eight fragrant and medicinal substances.

Her family. Beside her are clay images of Subrahmaṇya, Bhadrakāḷī and Mahāgaṇapati, with Durgāparameśvarī.

Old worship. An inscription near the temple is cited for the 8th century; the Āḷupa kings are credited with promoting her worship.

ZAPPY: Clay doesn’t last, does it?

PROF. BOLT: This clay has lasted centuries, with care. The re-coating every twelve years is that care made into a festival.

A queen made of the earth.

STORY · KING SURATHA

The king who made a goddess of earth

The story

From the frame story of the Devī Māhātmya, with the local tradition of Poḷali.

King Suratha lost a war, and his own ministers seized his treasury. He rode alone into the forest.
At the hermitage of the sage Medhas he met a merchant, Samādhi, cast out by his own family.
The sage told them the deeds of the Goddess: the slaying of Madhu and Kaiṭabha, of Mahiṣa, of Śumbha and Niśumbha.
The two men went to a riverbank, made an image of the Goddess from earth, and worshipped it for three years. She appeared: Suratha won back his kingdom; Samādhi asked for wisdom.
Poḷali’s tradition places that riverbank here: Suratha shaped this clay image, and set his own jewelled crown on her head.

ZAPPY: So the Devī Māhātmya’s clay image is this one?

PROF. BOLT: So Poḷali believes. The text names no place; the coast gave the story a home.

A king’s clay goddess by the Phalguṇī.

THE MONTH

Mīna saṅkramaṇa to the final bath

Day 1. The festival begins on Mīna saṅkramaṇa, the sun’s entry into Pisces, in mid-March.

Day 4. The festival image, set on a circular crown-frame called the prabhāvaḷi, is carried to the Siṃhāsana Kaṭṭe, a platform about 100 m from the temple, for a special worship.

The weeks. Nightly processions, music, Yakṣagāna and a large fair around the temple.

The Ceṇḍu. The five days of the ball game begin seven days before the final bath.

The end. The great chariot festival (rathōtsava), and then the avabhṛtha, the closing bath of the festival image, in mid-April, just before Bisu.

ZAPPY: A whole month?

PROF. BOLT: One of the longest festivals on the coast, and timed to end just before the Tuḷu new year.

A month of fair, ending in a bath.

THE FAIR AND THE NIGHTS

Processions, plays and stalls

Every night. The festival image is taken out in procession each night with drums and lamps.

Yakṣagāna. Troupes play through the nights; temple festivals like this are the backbone of the Yakṣagāna season. See Yakṣagāna.

The fair. Stalls of sweets, toys, bangles, household goods and rides fill the grounds: one of the great spring gatherings of Tuḷunāḍu.

From across the coast. Devotees come from all over Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa and beyond.

Before the rains. Like every jātre it must finish well before Pattanāje in late May (see The Tuḷu Year).

ZAPPY: Is the fair religious?

PROF. BOLT: On the coast the jātre is both: the Goddess at the centre, and the whole village celebrating around her.

A goddess at the centre of a village fair.

THE BALL

Made by one family, carried by another

Ceṇḍu. Ceṇḍu in Kannaḍa, cend in Tuḷu: a ball. The game is Poḷali Ceṇḍu or Pural da Ceṇḍu.

The makers. A hereditary cobbler family of Mijār stitches the leather ball each year; it is their right and their honour.

Worshipped first. Before it leaves the maker’s house, the ball is worshipped there before the family’s goddess.

The carriers. An oil-miller family of Kaḍapu Kariya fetches the ball from Mijār and announces the day the game will begin.

The first resting place. They set it down in the freshly cleaned front yard of the Maḷali Ballāḷ house before it goes on to the temple.

ZAPPY: Why so many families for one ball?

PROF. BOLT: Because the village is the team. Cobbler, oil-miller, landlord, priest: each has a part, and none can play without the others.

A ball that passes through many hands.

THE BALL ON THE GATEWAY

The first evening

Handed to the priest. On the day of the Ceṇḍu, the family brings the ball to the temple and hands it to the priest in charge.

On the gateway. On the first evening the cobbler family places the ball and a palm-leaf umbrella on top of the temple gateway.

Worship. The ball is worshipped there as an offering to the Goddess.

To the field. After the worship, the priest carries it in a ceremonial walk to the field in front of the temple.

A ball as prasāda. By the time it reaches the players, the ball has been blessed by the Goddess: it is her ball.

ZAPPY: Why an umbrella?

PROF. BOLT: An umbrella is a mark of honour, held over kings and gods. The ball is given royal honours.

The ball receives royal honours.

THE CEṆḌU

Two villages, one ball, five days

The story

The Poḷali Ceṇḍu, from temple and press accounts.

When. Five days, starting seven days before the final bath (avabhṛtha).
Start. The guttu families (old landed houses) wait at the Sultān Kaṭṭe; a man who holds the hereditary right throws the ball in.
Sides. Two villages, one on each side of the temple. There is no fixed number of players; up to about five hundred have joined.
Aim. To drive the ball to the opponents’ side. No referee, no fixed team size, no clock; trustees recall crowds so thick no one could see the ball once play began.
Meaning. The contest stands for dharma against adharma; the chariot festival that follows marks the victory.

ZAPPY: Is it like modern football?

PROF. BOLT: Only loosely. It is closer to the old village games of Europe than to a stadium match, and it is played for a Goddess.

A game played as a rite.

THE SULTĀN KAṬṬE

A platform with a story

The name. The platform at the edge of the field is called the Sultān Kaṭṭe, “the Sultan’s platform”.

The story. Local tradition says Tipu Sultān admired the Poḷali Ceṇḍu and had a platform built to watch the game. (Tradition; not independently checked.)

Its use. Today the guttu families gather there before the throw-in.

A many-sided past. The coast’s temples passed under many rulers: Āḷupas, Vijayanagara, Keḷadi, Mysore. Stories like this one survive alongside the rites.

Read more. On Tipu and the temples of Mysore, see Tipu and the Temples.

ZAPPY: Did Tipu really watch?

PROF. BOLT: We don’t know. The platform is real; the story is what the village tells.

A platform, and the story it carries.

THE CHARIOT AND THE BATH

Victory and the close

Rathōtsava. After the Ceṇḍu the Goddess rides in the great chariot, pulled by the crowd: the victory the game foretold.

Avabhṛtha. The festival closes with the avabhṛtha, the ritual bath of the festival image, in mid-April.

Why a bath. Avabhṛtha is the old Vedic name for the bath that ends a great sacrifice: the festival is treated as one long offering, now complete.

Flag down. With the bath the festival flag is lowered and the temple returns to its daily worship.

Then Bisu. A day or two later the Tuḷu year turns at Bisu.

ZAPPY: Why does a festival end with a bath?

PROF. BOLT: Every great rite ends by washing: you return to ordinary life clean.

Victory, then a cleansing bath.
