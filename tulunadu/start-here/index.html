TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea runs a narrow green strip that, tradition says, an axe won back from the ocean.

In one village here, several kinds of the sacred live side by side, each with its own priests, its own songs and its own night of the year.

This first guide is the doorway to the series. Read it once, and every temple that follows will make more sense.

Oṃ Śrī Paraśurāmāya Namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Deva, daiva... aren’t those the same word?

PROF. BOLT: Both grow from the same Sanskrit root, div, “to shine”. On this coast they part ways, and by page seven you’ll see exactly where.

ZAPPY: Why call it Tuḷunāḍu, and not just Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa?

PROF. BOLT: Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa is an office name for half of it. Tuḷunāḍu is what its own people call home, wherever the district lines fall.

WHERE IT IS

Between the Ghats and the sea

A thin land. The plain is narrow. The Western Ghats rise steeply a short way inland, so the rivers are short and fast, and the coast is cut into strips by their estuaries.

Rain. This is one of the wettest settled coasts in India: Udupi district alone records about 4,300 mm of rain in a normal year, most of it in four monsoon months.

River-mouth towns. The coast’s cities grew where rivers meet the sea: Maṅgaḷūru at the mouths of the Netrāvatī and the Phalguṇi (Gurupura), and the old Āḷupa ports on the Sītā and Swarṇā further north.

PARAŚURĀMA’S LAND

The axe and the sea
A sage with no land. Paraśurāma, the sixth avatāra of Viṣṇu, had given away every land he won in his wars. He was left with nowhere of his own to stand.
The throw. From the heights of the Sahyādri, the Western Ghats, he hurled his axe into the ocean. The sea drew back as far as the axe flew, and a new coast rose, said to run from Gokarṇa to Kanyākumārī.
Salt into soil. The new land was salty and barren. The serpent king Vāsuki spat his poison over it, and the soil turned fertile. The nāgas were then made guardians of the land.
Holy places. Paraśurāma is said to have settled the land and set up its sacred places, chief among them the seven Muktisthalas.

Where the story is told. The Malayāḷam chronicle Kēraḷōlpatti (17th century) gives the axe and Vāsuki’s poison. The Sahyādri Khaṇḍa, which presents itself as part of the Skanda Purāṇa, is a gathering of texts from about the 5th to the 13th century and tells of Paraśurāma’s coast and its settlers. See also Paraśurāma in the Daśāvatāra.

What “mukti” means here. A muktisthala is a place of liberation: a pilgrimage to it is held to free the soul from rebirth. Devotees compare the seven with the Saptapurī · 8 articles, the seven liberating cities of the north, and call this coast their southern counterpart.

Kumbhāśi’s name. Local tradition says Paraśurāma installed Hari and Hara here in the Kṛta Yuga, and that the town takes its name from the demon Kumbhāsura, killed here by Bhīma in the Dvāpara Yuga.

ZAPPY: Only one of the seven is in Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa district?

PROF. BOLT: Just Kukke. Five sit in Udupi and one far north. The temples are older than the district lines, so this series follows the old South Canara, not today’s map.

TUḶU AND THE TUḶUVAS

Language, letters, family and the first god

🗣️ The language

Tuḷu belongs to the Dravidian family, alongside Kannaḍa, Tamiḻ, Telugu and Malayāḷam. The 2011 census counted about 18.5 lakh native speakers; other estimates run as high as 30 lakh.

✍️ The letters

Tuḷu had its own script, Tuḷu–Tigaḷāri, grown from the old Grantha script and long used by priests for Sanskrit texts. Over time the Kannaḍa script took its place for everyday writing.

👪 The mother’s line

Most Tuḷu communities followed aliyasantāna: a family belonged to its women, and a man’s estate passed not to his sons but to his sister’s children. The lineage name, the bali, also comes through the mother.

🌳 Bermeru

In Tuḷu tradition Bermeru, identified with Brahmā, is the lord of the forest and of the daivas, the first god of the land.

A spoken literature. Tuḷu’s great literature was never written down by its makers. The pāḍdanas (also called sandi) were sung: by daiva performers at the shrine, and by women in the paddy fields while transplanting seedlings. The first collectors to put them on paper were 19th-century missionaries of the Basel Mission, among them Hermann Mögling.

ZAPPY: Brahmā has hardly any temples in India. And here he’s the boss?

PROF. BOLT: Here he is Bermeru, and he never lost his place. Tuḷunāḍu kept a Brahmā the rest of India mostly forgot.

HISTORY

From Āḷvakheḍa to two districts
Saṅgam era The Tamiḻ anthology Akanāṉūṟu names a “Tuḷu Nāṭu”.
2nd c. The geographer Ptolemy names “Olokhoira”, read as Āḷvakheḍa.
c. 450 The Halmiḍi inscription mentions the Āḷupas (“Alapa”).
early 7th c. The Vaḍḍarase inscription, the first full Āḷupa record, in Kannaḍa.
to 1444 The Āḷupas rule, at times on their own, at times under the Kadambas, Cāḷukyas, Rāṣṭrakūṭas and Hoysaḷas.
14th–16th c. Vijayanagara governs the coast as the Maṅgaḷūru and Bārakūru rājyas; local chiefs rule under it.
17th c. The Keḷadi Nāyakas of Ikkēri hold most of the coast.
1760s Hyder Ali takes the coast; then Tipu Sultan.
1799 After Tipu’s fall the British form South Canara, a district of the Madras Presidency.
1956 South Canara joins Mysore State (later Karnataka); Kasaragod goes to Kerala.
1997 Udupi district is carved out of Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa on 25 August.

The serpent kings. The Āḷupas ruled from Maṅgaḷūru, Udyāvara and Bārakūru, and one effaced Āḷupa inscription bears a hooded serpent. In the 14th century King Soyideva was followed on the throne by his nephew Kulaśekhara Baṅkideva, an early sign of the mother’s-line rule in the royal house itself.

FOUR WORLDS OF WORSHIP

Deva, daiva, nāga and Jina

🛕 Deva

The great gods of the Purāṇas, Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī, Subrahmaṇya and Gaṇapati, in stone temples. Brahmin priests serve them every day by the Āgama and Tantra texts.

🔥 Daiva

Powers of a particular place, family or village. They have shrines but no daily pūjā: they are honoured on fixed nights of the year, when they come to speak through a performer.

🐍 Nāga

The serpents beneath the earth, owners of the soil and givers of children and health. Each lineage has its original nāga and its own grove.

🪷 Jina

The Tīrthaṅkaras of Jainism, in basadis. Jain chiefs ruled much of the coast for centuries, and Mūḍabidri, Kārkaḷa and Veṇūr remain great Jain centres.

One family, four calendars. A single landholding family might draw a temple chariot in spring, sponsor a daiva’s night in winter, renew its nāga grove after a bad dream and give alms at the basadi of a Jain neighbour. To a Tuḷuva these are not rival religions but different doors in the same house.

ZAPPY: So who’s more powerful, the deva or the daiva?

PROF. BOLT: Wrong question! People say the deva rules the world and the daiva rules the village. You pray to Śiva for mokṣa; you go to the daiva when your neighbour moves the boundary stone.

WHAT IS A DAIVA?

Not a ghost, not quite a god
The word. Daivas are also called bhūtas. Here the word does not mean a ghost: it keeps its old Sanskrit sense, “a being”. Many devotees now prefer “daiva”, so as not to be misunderstood.
Two kinds of origin. Some daivas came down from heaven: gaṇas of Śiva or forms of the Goddess, sent to earth for a fault or a task. Others were once men and women of this coast who died wronged or heroic, and whom the land made divine.
A home, not a temple. A daiva lives in a sthāna or daivasthāna: a small shrine at the edge of the fields, or a room in the family house. There its sword, bell, mask and ornaments are kept, sometimes on a swinging cot.
Rank. Daivas have order and seniority. The rājan-daiva is the royal daiva of a manor or village, served by its chief house; lesser daivas follow it.
The manor. The great land-holding houses, the guttus and bīḍus, were the daivas’ hosts. The head of the house stages the daiva’s festival, and in doing so shows his standing in the village.

ZAPPY: So a daiva needs a village to stay powerful?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. A deva is great whether you come or not. A daiva is as strong as the village that gathers for it. No gathering, no voice.

THE DAIVAS

A few of the hundreds
PañjurliThe boar daiva, guardian of the crops. Born as a boar child, he was sent to earth by Śiva as a gaṇa after Pārvatī pleaded for him. Some link him with Varāha.
GuḷigaBorn from a stone, hungry from birth, he bit the little finger of Viṣṇu. Calmed and sent down to earth, he became a fierce protector. Among the most widely worshipped daivas.
JumādiAlso called Dhūmāvatī: a joined form of Śiva and Pārvatī, half man and half woman, that destroyed the demon Dhūmāsura.
Kalkuḍa and KallurṭiA sculptor and his sister. Tradition makes him the carver of Kārkaḷa’s colossal Bāhubali, maimed by the king so he could never make another; she avenged him, and both became daivas.
UllāltiA goddess-daiva, patron of the Baṅga royal house.
BobbaryaDaiva of the sea and of the fishing community, the Mogavīras.
KordabbuA daiva honoured by the senior daivas themselves with the title Daivarāja, “king of daivas”.
MaisandāyaA daiva of the buffalo, honoured by farming families.

ZAPPY: A daiva that bit Viṣṇu? And survived?

PROF. BOLT: Viṣṇu didn’t punish him; he gave him a job. That’s the pattern here: the wild power isn’t destroyed, it’s put to work guarding people.

A NIGHT OF KŌLA

When the daiva comes to speak
Readying. The shrine is cleaned and the daiva’s ornaments and weapons carried out and set in place, after hours of preparation and chanting.
The song. The performer sings the daiva’s pāḍdana: who it was, where it came from, how it reached this village.
Becoming. He paints his face, ties a skirt of tender palm leaves, and has the great halo, the aṇi, fixed to his back.
Arrival. The attendant, the pātri, hands him the sword and bell; the host family gives him burning torches. When the sacred headgear is placed on him, the daiva is held to have arrived.
The court. After offerings, the daiva speaks in its own voice, the nuḍi. Villagers bring quarrels, losses and fears; judgments are spoken or read by tossing betel leaves and counting areca petals. Prasāda goes to the houses in order of rank.

HEROES OF THE PĀḌDANAS

People who became daivas

⚔️ Kōṭi and Cennaya

Twin warriors of the 16th century (about 1556–1591), born at Paḍumale near Puttūr to Deyi Baidedi. They fought for justice against a corrupt minister and fell in battle near Eṇmūr. Their epic is one of the two long epics of Tuḷu. They are worshipped in garaḍis, old gymnasium-shrines, together with Bermeru, as Brahma–Baidarkaḷa.

👑 Siri

A princess, Siri Āḷvedi, wronged by her husband, who walked out of her marriage and into divinity. Her story continues with her son Kumāra, her daughter Sonne and granddaughters Abbaga and Dāraga. Her pāḍdana, 15,683 lines long, is the longest poem in Tuḷu. At the yearly Siri jātre women by the hundred enter trance as Siri and her family; her shrines are the Ādi Āḷade, with centres at Nidgal and Hiriyaḍka.

🧺 Koragajja

Koraga Taniya, an orphan boy taken in by Bairakke Baidedi, grew into a basket-maker of great strength. One festival day he vanished at a shrine and returned as a daiva. Today Koragajja, “grandfather Koraga”, is among the most loved daivas of the coast, called on for lost things, stuck work and vows.

ZAPPY: So any good person can become a daiva?

PROF. BOLT: Not any. Most of these heroes were wronged: cheated, maimed, turned away. The coast remembers injustice, and the wronged one comes back as its judge.

THE NĀGAS

Groves, drawings and the night of the serpents
The nāgabana. A grove where cobras are believed to rest, with stone images of hooded serpents. Its trees are never cut, and harming a cobra calls for a rite of atonement. Each lineage looks to its mūla nāga, the ancestral serpent, in its mūlasthāna, the grove of origin.
Nāgamaṇḍala. The grandest rite. Through the night a great serpent design is drawn on the ground in five colours: white earth, red lime with turmeric, green leaf powder, yellow turmeric and black from burnt paddy husk. Two ritualists bring the serpents to life around it: the pātri, who breathes in areca flowers and becomes the male serpent, and the Vaidya, dressed as the nāgakannikā, the serpent maiden, who sings and plays the hourglass drum, the ḍakke. Their dance enacts the union of the serpents, and goes on till dawn.
Ḍakke bali. A shorter night rite in the same spirit, with the same pairing of serpent priest and drummer-singer.
Āśleṣā bali. Done on the day of the Āśleṣā star, the nakṣatra of the serpents: pūjā, fire offering and food offerings with the recitation of the serpent hymns.

The greatest serpent shrine of the coast is Kukke Subrahmaṇya. The festival of the nāgas has its own guide: Nāgara Pañcamī.

WHERE DEVA MEETS DAIVA

The story of Dharmasthaḷa
Kuḍuma. About eight centuries ago, tradition says, the place was a village called Kuḍuma, home of a Jain chief, Birmaṇṇa Pergaḍe, and his wife Ammu Baḷḷālti, in their house Nelyāḍi Bīḍu.
Four guests. Four guardians of dharma came to them in human form, looking for a place where dharma was truly lived. The couple received them with such care that the guests revealed themselves as the Dharma Daivas: Kālarāhu, Kālarkāyi, Kumārasvāmi and Kanyākumārī.
The gift of a house. The Pergaḍe gave up Nelyāḍi Bīḍu for their worship and built separate shrines for the four, as they asked.
A liṅga from Kadri. The priests asked that a Śiva liṅga be installed beside the daivas. The daivas sent their servant Annappa, who brought one from Kadri near Maṅgaḷūru; the temple of Mañjunātha grew around it. Annappa himself is worshipped as a daiva.

What this tells us. At Dharmasthaḷa the daivas came first and the great god second; a Jain family hosts both; and the daily worship of Śiva is done by Vaiṣṇava (Mādhva) Brahmins. It is the clearest single picture of how the four worlds of this coast fit together.

ZAPPY: A Jain family looks after a Śiva temple run by Vaiṣṇava priests, for guardian daivas?

PROF. BOLT: Welcome to Tuḷunāḍu! Here nobody asks you to pick one. Each keeps its role, and the village needs all of them.

YAKṢAGĀNA

The temple’s all-night theatre
The art. Dance, song, spoken improvisation and towering costume, staged from dusk to dawn on stories from the epics and Purāṇas. “Yakṣagāna”, the song of the yakṣas, is a scholar’s name of the last two centuries; people still say āṭa, bayalāṭa (“open-field play”) or daśāvatāra.
Its roots. Tradition credits Madhvācārya’s disciple Naraharitīrtha with bringing it to Uḍupi. The earliest written record is an inscription of 1556 at Kurugōḍu in Baḷḷāri district.
Theatre as worship. A family that makes a vow, a harake, to a deity promises to host a night of Yakṣagāna if the wish is granted. Temple troupes, the melas, perform these vow shows without tickets, and their bookings run years ahead.
The great melas. The Kaṭīl Mela of Durgāparameśvarī, founded in the mid-19th century, launched its seventh troupe on 16 November 2025; in the 2025–26 season the seven together staged more than 1,000 shows. Mandārthi runs five troupes; Dharmasthaḷa has its own mela.

ZAPPY: Theatre as an offering? The goddess watches the show?

PROF. BOLT: At Kaṭīl they believe she comes to wherever her mela is playing. The most requested play is the Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts, her own story.

Sun, not moon. The Tuḷu month starts when the sun enters a new sign of the zodiac. The first day of a month is tiṅgoḍe and the last is saṅkrānti. Paggu matches the solar month Meṣa, so Bisu is the same day as Viṣu in Kerala.

The lean month. Āṭi brings the heaviest rain of the year. Fields are flooded, food stores run low and no weddings are held.

On the streets. At Aṣṭami and Mārnemi, troupes of painted pilivēṣa “tigers” dance from house to house.
