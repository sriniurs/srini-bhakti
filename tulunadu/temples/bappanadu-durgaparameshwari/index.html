ŚRĪ DURGĀPARAMEŚVARĪ · MULKI · A REFERENCE GUIDE

BAPPANĀḌU

The Goddess and the trader whose boat stopped

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

At Mulki, where the Śāmbhavī runs to the sea, a Goddess of the coast has a village named after a Muslim trader.

The story says Bappa, a Beary merchant, was sailing upriver when his boat stopped dead and the water around it turned red. In the night the Goddess spoke to him, and he became the reason her temple was built. The place has been Bappanāḍu, “Bappa’s land”, ever since, and his family still receives her prasāda at the festival.

This guide tells the older story of the Goddess too: seven sisters, a demon and a boat of sandalwood.

Śrī Durgāparameśvaryai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A temple named after a Muslim?

PROF. BOLT: The village is. The temple is the Goddess’s; the place remembers the man the story says she chose to build it.

RiverThe Śāmbhavī, near its meeting with the Nandinī and the sea
Name“Bappa’s land”, after Bappa Beary, a Muslim trader
BuilderBy tradition the Jain ruler of Mulki, the Sāvanta, with Bappa’s help
First priestBaila Uḍupa
Famous forIts giant ḍōlu drum and festival drumming
Great festivalThe Brahma Rathōtsava in the month of Mīna, about April

ZAPPY: Who are the Bearys?

PROF. BOLT: A Muslim community of the Tuḷu coast with their own language, long known as traders.

WHERE IT IS

Rivers, sea and a moving shore

The temple. On the bank of the Śāmbhavī at Bappanāḍu, in Mulki town, beside the highway from Maṅgaḷūru to Uḍupi.

Two rivers. Tradition places the Goddess’s first appearance at the meeting of the Śāmbhavī and the Nandinī at the edge of Mulki; the Nandinī is the river of Kaṭīl.

A shifting shore. The rivers here have moved. The Portuguese traveller Barbosa (1516) and the Italian Della Valle (1623) found the area of today’s Mulki under water; later shifts of the Śāmbhavī made the land the town stands on.

THE GODDESS AS A LIṄGA

Durgāparameśvarī of Mulki

🪨 Her form

As at many Devī shrines of the coast, the Goddess here is a liṅga, a smooth stone, dressed and adorned as the Mother.

🛡️ Her name

Durgāparameśvarī: Durgā, the Supreme Goddess, the protector who destroys demons.

👥 With her

Gaṇapati and Narasiṁha are worshipped at the temple too, and Guḷiga belongs to her story.

🌊 Found in water

The liṅga is said to have been found under the river, which is why its discovery needed a boat.

ZAPPY: Why a liṅga for a goddess?

PROF. BOLT: On this coast the Goddess often takes that shape: Kaṭīl’s Durgā is a self-born stone too. The form is plain; the worship makes it the Mother.

THE SEVEN DURGĀS

The demon Dārikāsura
The demon. Dārikāsura ruled Śōṇitapura. With a boon from Brahmā he defeated the gods and even Viṣṇu, took Viṣṇu’s weapons and gave them to his wife to keep in her shrine.
Seven women. The Goddess appeared to Viṣṇu as seven women, the Sapta Durgās, and promised to destroy him. With them went Guḷiga.
The trick. One sister, Bhagavatī, came to the demon’s palace as an old woman begging for food, and won the weapons from his wife.
The war. Dārika defeated Guḷiga, but after seven days of battle he fled to the underworld.
The end. Bhadrakāḷī found him there at evening, worshipping Śiva, and killed him.

A shared story. The slaying of Dārika by Bhadrakāḷī is also the great myth of the goddess temples of Kerala, to the south along the same coast.

THE SANDALWOOD BOAT

From Vaikuṇṭha up the coast

The gift. After the victory, Bhagavatī, her sisters and Guḷiga asked Viṣṇu for a boat to go from Vaikuṇṭha down to earth. He gave them a sandalwood tree, and from it they made their boat.

The voyage. It came down to earth and sailed north up the coast, past Kāsaragōḍ, Kumbḷe, Uppaḷa, Pāttattūr, Mañjēśvara, Udyāvara, Uḷḷāla and Kudroḷi, and reached Sasihitlu near Mulki.

Another telling. One version has the sisters walk north from Kanyākumārī instead, resting in a coconut grove at Mulki.

THE TENDER COCONUT

Why she stayed
Thirsty. At Mulki a toddy-tapper offered Bhagavatī a tender coconut, and she drank it.
Left behind. In one telling, her sisters would not travel on with her after she accepted a drink from a man of low caste, and went on without her.
Her choice. She declared the man her devotee and chose to stay where she had been welcomed.
Into the earth. She took the form of a liṅga and settled between the Śāmbhavī and the Nandinī, unseen, until she was found.

ZAPPY: So the Goddess stayed for the one who served her?

PROF. BOLT: That is the shape of the story. She stayed for a toddy-tapper’s coconut, and later chose a Muslim trader to build her house. Both times, the honour goes to the outsider.

BAPPA’S BOAT

The trader and the red river
The trader. Bappa Beary, a Muslim merchant, often said to be from Kerala, was carrying goods up the Śāmbhavī to trade.
Stopped. In mid-river near Mulki his boat would not move, and he saw the water around it turn red as blood. Some tellings say the boat had struck something under the water.
The dream. He said his prayers and slept in the stranded boat. The Goddess appeared to him, told him there is one God for the whole world, and asked him to have a temple built for her.
The promise. She promised that his name would be remembered for ever. He agreed, went on with his trade, and on his return was reminded of his pledge.

THE KING AND THE PRIEST

How the temple was built
The voiceTold Bappa to have the temple built through the king of Mulki, and to have the worship done by Baila Uḍupa
The kingThe Mulki Sāvanta, a Jain ruler remembered as just; one telling names him Duggaṇṇa Sāvanta
The moneyBappa contributed; the king gathered other gifts and built the temple
The priestBaila Uḍupa was made chief priest
The findingThe liṅga, found under the water, was installed in the new shrine
The nameBappa settled nearby, and the place became Bappanāḍu

ZAPPY: A Muslim, a Jain and a Brahmin build a Devī temple?

PROF. BOLT: That is how the story tells it: each with his part. It is why the temple is so often held up as a symbol of the coast living together.

WHAT THE RECORDS SAY

Tradition and evidence
14th–18th c. The Sāvanta family rules the Mulki region, with capitals in turn at Eliñje, Sīmantūr and Paḍupaṇambūr, where the family still lives.
1516 Barbosa describes the area of present Mulki as flooded.
1607 A copperplate in the Kōṭageri Jain basadi names a Bappa in connection with an offering of 113 varāhas.
1623 Della Valle also finds the area under water.
The question Documents for the temple’s early history are scarce, and Bappa’s part is not confirmed by them. One local writer asks whether the Bappa of the records was a Muslim, a Sārasvata named Bāpa Pai, or a Jain.
Age The temple is commonly described as about 800 years old.

ZAPPY: So is the story true?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition is old and lived: the family honour, the name of the village. The documents are thin. This guide gives both and leaves the question where it stands.

BAPPA’S FAMILY TODAY

A promise kept

🍚 First prasāda

On the first day of the annual festival, the temple’s prasāda is traditionally sent to the house of Bappa Beary’s descendants.

🌼 Their offering

When the festival image comes out in procession, the family receives prasāda and offers flowers and fruit.

🤝 Open doors

Muslims of the area accept the temple’s prasāda and take part in its festivals.

📛 The name

The Goddess’s promise, that his name would be remembered, is kept every time someone says “Bappanāḍu”.

THE ḌŌLU OF BAPPANĀḌU

The drum the whole coast knows
The drum. An outsize ḍōlu stands by the main entrance; it has been shown as a heritage piece of Tuḷunāḍu at the Karāvaḷi Utsav in Maṅgaḷūru.
The drummers. The festival drumming is the right of a particular community, which the temple pays an honorarium.
The saying. “Go and beat the ḍōlu at Bappanāḍu” is a Tuḷu taunt for someone good for nothing, sent off to do noisy, useless work.
A great rite. One scholar of Tuḷu ritual lists the Bappanāḍu ḍōlu among the seven great ritual events of Tuḷunāḍu.

THE JĀTRE

Eight days in Mīna
Day 1Dhvajārōhaṇa, the flag raised, on Mīna śuddha caturdaśī
Day 2Bali utsava and Āyana dīpōtsava
Day 3Pēṭe savāri, the procession to the market
Day 4Koppaḷa savāri
Day 5Bakimāru dīpōtsava
Day 6Kere dīpōtsava, lamps at the tank
Day 7Morning chariot; at night the Candramaṇḍala chariot and śayanōtsava
Day 8Opening of the doors, kavāṭa, and the night Brahma Rathōtsava

2025. During the night Brahma Rathōtsava in April 2025, the decorated upper part of the great chariot toppled. No one was seriously hurt, and the procession continued in the Candramaṇḍala chariot.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Other days of the Goddess

🪔 Navarātri

Nine nights of special worship of the Goddess.

✨ Dīpōtsava

Festivals of lamps through the year.

🌙 Ananta Caturdaśī

Observed at the temple, with Dhanurmāsa worship in winter.

🎪 The fair

A yearly fair of about three weeks gathers around the temple.

Every day. Worship three times a day, morning, noon and night.
