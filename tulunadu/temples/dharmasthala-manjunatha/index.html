ŚRĪ KṢETRA DHARMASTHAḶA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

DHARMASTHAḶA

Mañjunātha and the guardians of dharma

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

On the banks of the Netrāvatī, at the foot of the Western Ghats, Śiva is worshipped as Mañjunātha. Yet he was not the first to be worshipped here.

First came four guardians of dharma, who asked a Jain chief for a home. The god arrived later, carried in by their servant. The chief’s descendants still keep the place, Vaiṣṇava priests serve its Śiva, and the guardians still speak once a year.

This guide tells that story, the family who keep it, and the festival of a hundred thousand lamps.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: “Dharmasthaḷa”: the place of dharma. Isn’t every temple that?

PROF. BOLT: Every temple teaches dharma. This one was founded because dharma was already being lived here, in a family’s kindness to strangers. The temple came second.

ZAPPY: A Śiva temple, Vaiṣṇava priests and a Jain family. Who’s in charge?

PROF. BOLT: Each in his own place. The priests do the pūjā, the family keeps the kṣetra, and the daivas, people say, keep an eye on everyone.

WHERE IT IS

On the Netrāvatī, below the Ghats

Forest country. Dharmasthaḷa lies inland, where the coastal plain meets the first slopes of the Western Ghats, in the Beḷtaṅgaḍi taluk of Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa.

The river. The Netrāvatī, the great river of Maṅgaḷūru, flows past the town. Pilgrims bathe at its ghāṭ before going to the shrine, and on the great festival days many walk in from Ujire.

A town around a family. The town grew up around the Heggaḍe’s household and the temple together; the family home still stands beside the shrine.

MAÑJUNĀTHA

The Lord of the kṣetra
The name. Mañjunātha, “the lovely Lord”, is a name of Śiva found in several old coastal shrines; Dharmasthaḷa’s liṅga came from one of them, the Mañjunātha temple of Kadri in Maṅgaḷūru.
The liṅga. A large liṅga in the sanctum, with the festival images standing on either side.
The Mother. Beside him is worshipped Ammanavaru, “the Mother”, the Goddess as Pārvatī, and with her Gaṇapati.
A Vaiṣṇava rite for Śiva. The daily worship follows the Mādhva tradition of Uḍupi. It is one of the few great Śiva shrines in India served by Vaiṣṇava priests.

ZAPPY: Why would Vaiṣṇava priests worship Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: Mādhvas honour Śiva as the greatest of Viṣṇu’s devotees. And at Dharmasthaḷa the arrangement goes back to the Mādhva saint Vādirāja himself, so it is the house custom.

THE FOUR GUESTS

How Kuḍuma became Dharmasthaḷa
Kuḍuma. About eight centuries ago, tradition says, this was Kuḍuma, a village in Mallarmāḍi. Here lived a Jain chief, Birmaṇṇa Pergaḍe, and his wife Ammu Baḷḷālti, in their house Nelyāḍi Bīḍu. They were known for never turning a guest away.
The guests. Four strangers came to the door, looking, they said, for a place where dharma was truly practised and could be carried on. The couple received them with every honour.
The dream. That night the guests appeared to Birmaṇṇa in a dream as the Dharma Daivas, guardians of dharma. They asked him to give up his house for their worship and to give his life to dharma.
The gift. Birmaṇṇa built himself a new home and left Nelyāḍi Bīḍu to the daivas. They then asked for shrines of their own, two oracles of good family to speak for them, and four helpers to serve them.

ANNAPPA AND THE LIṄGA

A god carried in overnight
The priests’ request. Brahmins were called to consecrate the daivas’ shrines. They asked that a Śiva liṅga be installed beside them.
Annappa’s errand. The daivas sent their servant Annappa, a man of great strength, to fetch one. Overnight, the story goes, he brought the liṅga of Mañjunātha from Kadri near Maṅgaḷūru and set it in the ground a short way from the family house.
The vanishing. His work done, Annappa disappeared. He is worshipped today as a daiva himself, Annappa Pañjurli, a hero and guardian of the kṣetra.
Vādirāja’s blessing. In the 16th century the administrator Devarāja Heggaḍe invited the Mādhva saint Vādirāja Tīrtha of Uḍupi. Finding the liṅga not consecrated by the full Vedic rite, Vādirāja reconsecrated it and, so the temple tells it, named the place Dharmasthaḷa, “the abode of dharma”.

Kadri’s gift. The bond with Kadri is remembered in both places: one Mañjunātha in the city by the sea, one in the forest by the Netrāvatī, the second carried from the first.

ZAPPY: So the liṅga was moved from Kadri without asking?

PROF. BOLT: The story doesn’t say who asked whom! It is told as a wonder of the daivas’ servant, not a theft. Kadri still has its own Mañjunātha.

THE DHARMA DAIVAS

The first lords of the kṣetra
KālarāhuOne of the four guardians; shares a shrine with Kanyākumārī and Kumārasvāmi
KālarkāyiThe fourth guardian, with her own place in the kṣetra’s worship
KumārasvāmiGuardian worshipped in the Mother’s shrine
KanyākumārīThe maiden goddess among the four
AnnappaTheir servant, now a daiva, with a hill shrine of his own

Jain guardians, coastal rite. The four are counted among the protective deities of Jainism, the family religion of the Heggaḍes, yet they are honoured in the coastal manner, as daivas with oracles and nights of worship.

Nelyāḍi Bīḍu. The house Birmaṇṇa gave away is still the daivas’ seat. The Heggaḍe alone worships them there, and he also makes the yearly offerings to his ancestors in the same house.

The hill. Annappa Beṭṭa, also called Baḍinēḍe Beṭṭa, is the hill where the four daivas are said to have walked; Annappa’s shrine stands on it beside theirs. By custom, women and children do not climb it.

ZAPPY: Why do the daivas need oracles?

PROF. BOLT: Because a daiva speaks. At their festival the oracle takes their voice, and what the daiva says is treated as its own judgment, not the man’s.

THE HEGGAḌE

Keeper of the kṣetra

👑 The family

The Pergaḍe family, Jain Baṇṭs, have kept the kṣetra since Birmaṇṇa. The eldest male of the line takes the title Heggaḍe and becomes Dharmādhikārī, chief administrator.

⚖️ The judge

The Heggaḍe was once the lord of the temple town and settled its disputes. People still bring him complaints, hoyilu, and his rulings are held to carry the will of the deities.

🔢 Twenty-one

The temple counts twenty-one Dharmādhikārīs from Birmaṇṇa, under the names Chandayya, Mañjayya, Devarāja and others repeated down the generations.

🍚 Four gifts

The kṣetra’s charity is summed up as four gifts: anna (food), auṣadha (medicine), vidyā (learning) and abhaya (freedom from fear).

The modern kṣetra. Twentieth-century Heggaḍes turned a forest shrine into a temple town: Mañjayya Heggaḍe III founded its yearly gatherings of faiths and of letters, Ratnavarma Heggaḍe began the Bāhubali, and D. Veerendra Heggaḍe, Dharmādhikārī since 1968, built schools, hospitals and rural programmes in the kṣetra’s name.

HISTORY

ZAPPY: Why a Jain colossus at a Śiva temple?

PROF. BOLT: Because the family who keep the temple are Jain, and Bāhubali is their great saint. Dharmasthaḷa gives each faith its own hill and its own festival.

THE TEMPLE

Shrines of the kṣetra

🪷 Candranātha Basadi

A Jain temple of the Tīrthaṅkara Candraprabha, the family deity of the Heggaḍes, a few steps from the Śiva shrine.

🍛 Annapūrṇa

The great dining hall, where a free meal is served to every pilgrim, every day. Feeding is the first of the kṣetra’s four gifts.

The coastal style. Like the older shrines of the coast, the temple has sloping tiled roofs and a sanctum kept low and dark, with the brass and gold of the festival images catching the lamp-light.

BĀHUBALI OF RATNAGIRI

The saint who stood still
Who he was. Bāhubali, son of the first Tīrthaṅkara Ṛṣabhanātha, won a duel with his brother for the empire, then gave it all up and stood in meditation so long that creepers grew up his limbs. Jains honour him as the first to reach liberation in this age.
The carving. The statue was cut from a single rock at Kārkaḷa by the sculptor Reñjāḷa Gopāla Śeṇai, who, it is said, gave up salt until it was finished. It stands 39 ft on a pedestal of 13 ft.
The journey. Moving a stone giant of around 170 tonnes on narrow roads and over weak bridges took years of planning. It left Kārkaḷa in 1975 and was raised on Ratnagiri hill in 1982.
The anointing. At the Mahāmastakābhiṣeka, held at long intervals, the statue is bathed from scaffolds above its head in milk, water and coloured powders before huge crowds.

LAKṢA DĪPOTSAVA

A hundred thousand lamps
WhenThe last five days of Kārtika (November–December)
Day 1Hosakaṭṭe utsava: Mañjunātha’s festival image goes out in procession
Day 2Kerekaṭṭe utsava, by the tank
Day 3Lalitōdyāna utsava, in the garden pavilion
Day 4Sarva Dharma Sammēḷana: speakers of many faiths on one stage
Day 5Sāhitya Sammēḷana: a Kannaḍa literary meeting

Since 1933. The two sammēḷanas were begun by Mañjayya Heggaḍe III and have met every year since; 2024 saw the 92nd session. Around them run a fair, an exhibition of rural life, music, dance and Harikathā, and thousands walk to the kṣetra on foot.

ZAPPY: A meeting of all religions inside a festival of Śiva?

PROF. BOLT: That’s Dharmasthaḷa in a sentence. Jain keepers, Vaiṣṇava priests, a Śiva liṅga and daiva guardians. A stage for every faith is just the house rule said out loud.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Other festivals of the kṣetra
MahāśivarātriNight-long worship of Mañjunātha; padayātras from across the region
Mahā NaḍāvaḷiAn eleven-day festival for Mañjunātha, Candranātha, the Dharma Daivas and Annappa together
Daiva nēmasThe guardians’ own nights, with their oracles
MahāmastakābhiṣekaThe great anointing of Bāhubali, at intervals of a decade or more
Samūha vivāhaMass weddings held by the kṣetra for families who cannot afford one

One festival for four faiths. The Mahā Naḍāvaḷi is the clearest picture of the kṣetra: the Śiva of the temple, the Jina of the basadi, and the daivas of the house are honoured in a single sequence of rites over eleven days.

AROUND DHARMASTHAḶA

The kṣetra and beyond
Mañjūṣā MuseumThe kṣetra’s collection of old manuscripts, sculpture, coins, lamps and everyday objects of the coast
Netrāvatī snāna ghāṭThe bathing place on the river below the town
UjireThe neighbouring town, with the kṣetra’s colleges
Kadri, MaṅgaḷūruThe Mañjunātha temple from which the liṅga came
Kārkaḷa and VeṇūrThe two other colossal Bāhubalis of the coast, older than Ratnagiri’s

Three Bāhubalis. Of the five great monolithic Bāhubalis of Karnataka, three stand in Tuḷunāḍu: Kārkaḷa (about 42 ft, 1432), Veṇūr, and Dharmasthaḷa, which is the youngest.

On the coast. For the land, the daivas and the nāgas that frame this kṣetra, see the series introduction.
