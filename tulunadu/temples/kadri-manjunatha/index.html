KADRI

Where Buddha, yogi and Śiva meet

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

On a laterite hill in the middle of Maṅgaḷūru, a spring pours from a stone cow’s mouth into a ladder of ponds. Below them stands one of the oldest Śiva temples of the coast.

Inside are bronzes made for Buddhist monks a thousand years ago, now worshipped under Hindu names. Above, in caves and a hilltop monastery, lived the yogis of the Nātha order.

This guide tells how Kadri became all three: a vihāra, a yogis’ seat and the home of Mañjunātha.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A Buddha statue in a Śiva temple?

PROF. BOLT: Kadri remembers everyone who ever prayed on this hill. Nothing was thrown out. It was renamed and kept.

ZAPPY: Why do so many temples on this coast have Mādhva priests?

PROF. BOLT: Because Uḍupi is close and the Mādhva Brahmins of the Śivaḷḷi villages served shrines of every god along the coast. Here too they serve Śiva.

WHERE IT IS

A hill in the city

In the middle of Maṅgaḷūru. Kadri hill rises a few kilometres east of the old port, now surrounded by the city. The temple stands at its foot; above it the ground climbs in red laterite to the caves and the monastery at the top.

A water hill. A perennial spring high on the slope has supplied the shrine for centuries, and the ponds it fills step down the hillside to the temple door.

A city garden. Beside the temple lies Kadri Park, one of Maṅgaḷūru’s large public gardens.

MAÑJUNĀTHA

The lovely Lord
Śiva with Pārvatī. The Lord of Kadri is Śiva as Mañjunātha, worshipped as a liṅga, with the Goddess beside him. Legend says the three great gods joined their powers into this one form.
A Buddhist echo in the name. Mañju means lovely. The Buddhist bodhisattva of wisdom is Mañjuśrī, also called Mañjughoṣa, and scholars see in “Mañjunātha” a name that passed from the bodhisattva of the old monastery to the Śiva of the new temple.
The parent of Dharmasthaḷa. The Mañjunātha of Dharmasthaḷa came from here: tradition says Annappa carried his liṅga overnight from Kadri to the Netrāvatī.
Daily worship. Rudrābhiṣeka, mahāpūjā and Kārtika pūjās; a silver chariot carries the Lord on festival evenings.

ZAPPY: So Mañjunātha is Śiva wearing a bodhisattva’s name?

PROF. BOLT: That’s one scholarly reading. For devotees he is Śiva, full stop. Both can be true: the name remembers the hill’s past, the worship belongs to its present.

PARAŚURĀMA’S BANANA GROVE

The temple legend
A sage without a home. After his wars against the kṣatriyas, Paraśurāma gave all the land he had won to the sage Kaśyapa and prayed to Śiva for a place to live and do penance.
The promise. Śiva told him to go to the Kadaḷī Kṣetra, the golden banana grove by the well called Rasakūpa. If he did penance there, Śiva would appear as Mañjunātha for the good of the world.
Land from the sea. The grove lay under the ocean. Paraśurāma threw his axe and the sea drew back, uncovering the land for his penance.
The Lord appears. Pleased, Śiva came as Mañjunātha with Pārvatī and agreed to stay at Kadri. At his word the seven tīrthas, the sacred waters of the hill, came into being, and Viśvakarmā built the first temple.

The source. The Kadri legend is told in the Bhāradvāja Saṃhitā, which praises the hill as the golden banana forest, “the giver of every success and good fortune”.

KADARIKA VIHĀRA

The Buddhist hill

☸️ A Vajrayāna monastery

Before the temple, Kadri was a Buddhist monastery of the Vajrayāna, the tantric school. Inscriptions call it Kadarika Vihāra, and the poet-scholar Govinda Pai derived “Kadri” from that name.

🪷 Its gods

Its devotion centred on the bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara, the compassionate, and Mañjuśrī, the wise. Their images remained when the monks were gone.

📉 Its end

Buddhism was practised on the hill until about the 10th century. As it faded, the Nātha yogis and Śaiva worship took over the site.

🔁 New names

The bronzes were renamed rather than removed: an Avalokiteśvara is worshipped as Nārāyaṇa and a Buddha as Vedavyāsa.

ZAPPY: Why would Hindus worship a Buddha as Vyāsa?

PROF. BOLT: Both are great teachers who sit in meditation. When the old meaning faded, people saw in the seated sage the one they knew best. Kadri didn’t erase him. It gave him a new name.

THE LOKEŚVARA BRONZE

A thousand-year-old masterpiece
The figure. A seated Lokeśvara, a form of Avalokiteśvara, cast in pañcaloha, the five-metal alloy, about five feet high. It has three faces and six arms, two of which hold flowers. In the temple it is honoured as a form of Brahmā.
The inscription. Its pedestal records that the Āḷupa king Kundavarma installed the Lokeśvara in the Kadarika Vihāra. It names the town Maṅgaḷāpura, one of the oldest written forms of the city’s name.
The date. Most read the date as 968; some as 1068.
A Śaiva king, a Buddhist gift. Kundavarma calls himself a devotee of Śiva in the same inscription that dedicates a bodhisattva, the clearest picture of the hill’s shared faiths.
Its fame. It is often called the oldest and finest bronze image in any South Indian temple still in worship.

THE JŌGI MAṬHA

The yogis on the summit
The Nāthas arrive. Tradition says the Nātha masters Matsyendranātha and Gōrakhanātha came to Kadri; images of both are kept in the temple. The Nātha order, partly tantric and close in spirit to Vajrayāna, made the hill one of its southern seats.
The monastery. On the hilltop stands the Kadri Jōgi Maṭha, the house of the Jōgis, as local people call the Nātha yogis. Kundavarma is credited with building a maṭha here.
The caves. Around it, in the red laterite, are small rock-cut chambers called the Pāṇḍava caves; local belief says the Pāṇḍava brothers lived in them during their exile.
A link to the Goddess. In one telling the Nātha teacher came to Kadri with his disciple Maṅgaḷā, the Goddess of Maṅgaḷūru; Kadri and Maṅgaḷādevī have kept their bond since.

ZAPPY: Do yogis still live up there?

PROF. BOLT: The maṭha still stands and its head is installed by the Nātha order’s own rules. The hill has never stopped being a yogis’ hill.

HISTORY

Vihāra, maṭha, temple
to 10th c. Kadarika Vihāra, a Vajrayāna Buddhist monastery.
968 King Kundavarma installs the bronze Lokeśvara (or 1068 by another reading).
10th–11th c. The present temple of Mañjunātha takes shape.
Nātha era The yogis settle on the hill; the Jōgi Maṭha is built.
12th–13th c. A stone inscription in Tuḷu, Kannaḍa and Malayāḷam scripts records a gift of land to the temple.
c. 1200 By Dharmasthaḷa’s tradition, Kadri’s liṅga is carried to Kuḍuma.
18th c. A sthala purāṇa, the Kadri Māhātmya, retells the hill’s legends.

The style. Visitors see a coastal temple whose stonework has been compared to early Vijayanagara building, with a balipīṭha, the offering stone, of old design before the shrine and a tall dīpastambha, lamp pillar, in front.

ZAPPY: Five-foot bronzes, a thousand years old, still worshipped?

PROF. BOLT: Not behind glass in a museum, but in a sanctum, with lamps and flowers every day. That's what makes Kadri rare.

GŌMUKHA AND THE TANKS

The water of the hill
Gōmukha. High on the hill a natural spring flows out through a carved cow’s mouth, the gōmukha. It has never run dry in living memory and is held to be the Gaṅgā herself.
The ponds. From it the water fills a chain of tanks stepping down the slope, usually counted as nine. Pilgrims bathe in them before entering the temple.
Seven tīrthas. The temple legend speaks of seven sacred waters created at Mañjunātha’s word; devotees take the spring and the tanks as their living form.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Festivals of Kadri
Jātre (Jan)Nine days from Makara Saṅkrānti: processions, utsavabali, a dīpōtsava, the mahārathōtsava and a tulābhāra
Daiva nēmaAt the end of the jātre, the nēma of Malarāya and the guardian daivas
KārtikaDīpōtsava at the lamp pillar; Kārtika Mondays
MahāśivarātriNight-long worship of Mañjunātha
OthersSauramāna Yugādi, Nāgara Pañcamī, Kṛṣṇa Janmāṣṭamī, Gaṇeśa Caturthī, Navarātri

The jātre’s journey. On the sixth day the Lord goes out in procession to Koñcāḍi; on the eighth the festival images are mounted on the great chariot and drawn at dusk; on the ninth, after the final pūjā, devotees are weighed against offerings in the tulābhāra.

Deva and daiva together. The jātre ends not with Śiva but with the daivas: the same pattern as everywhere in Tuḷunāḍu, where the guardians are honoured once the god has had his festival.
