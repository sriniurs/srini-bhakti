ANANTA PADMANĀBHA · VEṄKAṬARAMAṆA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

KĀRKAḶA

Two homes of Viṣṇu in a Jain kings’ town · explained and illustrated

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

HOW THIS WORKS: Two Viṣṇu temples of Jain Kārkaḷa: the reclining Ananta Padmanābha found in a lake and installed in 1567 for a Śṛṅgeri pontiff, and Padu Tirupati, the Veṅkaṭaramaṇa of the Gauḍa Sārasvatas who fled Goa, with the idol lost in a Mulki well, the cow that chose the site, Vīra Māruti and the carved Garuḍa pillars.

FOREWORD

Kārkaḷa is famous for its Jains: the great Gommaṭa on the rock, the four-faced basadi, the pillars. But its Jain rulers also gave Viṣṇu two homes in the heart of the town.

One is Ananta Padmanābha, Viṣṇu asleep on the serpent, carved from a single black stone found in a lake and installed in 1567 so that a visiting Śaṅkarācārya would stay. The other is Veṅkaṭaramaṇa, “Padu Tirupati”, the Tirupati of the west, built by Konkaṇi families who had fled Goa.

This guide visits both.

Om Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Jain kings building Viṣṇu temples?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and it says a lot about the coast. The Bhairarasa rulers patronised Jainism above all, yet gave land and welcome to Hindu teachers and refugees.

AT A GLANCE

The essential facts on one page
TownKārkaḷa, Uḍupi district, about 37 km from Uḍupi and 50 km from Maṅgaḷūru
RulersThe Jain Bhairarasa Oḍeyas of the Sāntara line, from the 14th century
Temple 1Ananta Padmanābha, also Anantaśayana or Śēṣaśāyi Anantēśvara, installed 1567
Its imageReclining Viṣṇu in one black stone, found in a lake at Nellikāru
Its statusA monument protected by the Archaeological Survey of India
Temple 2Veṅkaṭaramaṇa, “Padu Tirupati”, installed by tradition on 25 April 1537
Its peopleThe Gauḍa Sārasvata Brāhmaṇas (GSB) who came from Goa
SharedA five-day Lakṣa dīpōtsava at both

ZAPPY: Two dates thirty years apart?

PROF. BOLT: Veṅkaṭaramaṇa first, in 1537, by the temple’s reckoning; Ananta Padmanābha in 1567. Both belong to the great age of the Bhairarasa court.

THE TOWN OF THE BHAIRARASAS

Jain kings and their neighbours
Origins The Bhairarasa Oḍeyas, a branch of the Sāntara house of Humcha, rule the Kaḷasa–Kārkaḷa country from about the 14th century. The old name of the town is given as Pāṇḍyanagari.
1432 Vīra Pāṇḍya Bhairarasa, on the advice of the Jain teacher Lalitakīrti, raises the great Gommaṭa (Bāhubali) on the rock of Kārkaḷa.
1537 The Veṅkaṭaramaṇa of the Konkaṇi settlers is installed, by temple tradition.
1567 Ananta Padmanābha is installed for a visiting Śṛṅgeri pontiff.
1586 Immaḍi Bhairava (Bhairava II) completes the Caturmukha basadi, the four-faced Jain temple on a nearby hill.
Since Kārkaḷa remains a Jain pilgrimage centre with its own Jain maṭha, beside its Hindu temples.

ZAPPY: The Gommaṭa is the one in the Kalkuḍa story?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Tradition makes the sculptor Kalkuḍa its maker; the record names only the king. See the Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi guide.

WHERE THEY ARE

Both in the heart of town

Ananta Padmanābha stands at the centre of the town, about a kilometre from the bus stand.

Veṅkaṭaramaṇa is a short way off, on the road towards Hiriyaḍka.

Around them. The Gommaṭa rock to the north, the Caturmukha basadi on its own low hill, and the Rāmasamudra lake: a small town packed with monuments of two faiths.

ANANTA PADMANĀBHA

Viṣṇu asleep on the serpent

🐍 Anantaśayana

Viṣṇu reclines on the coils of Ādiśēṣa, the endless serpent, in the sleep between ages.

🪷 Padmanābha

“Lotus-navel”: from his navel rises the lotus on which Brahmā sits, about to create.

🙏 At his feet

Śrīdēvī and Bhūdēvī, Fortune and Earth, attend him.

🪨 One stone

The whole group is carved from a single black stone, which gives the temple its popular name, Anantaśayana.

THE PONTIFF WHO WOULD NOT STAY

How the temple came to be
A royal guest. In 1567, the pontiff of Śṛṅgeri, Śrī Narasiṁha Bhāratī, came to Kārkaḷa. The Jain king of the Bhairava line welcomed him with honour.
A refusal. The Svāmi told the king he would not stay in a place that had no temple.
A building. The king had a shrine at hand: a building raised as a Jain basadi, or newly planned as one, depending on the account.
An image. A reclining Viṣṇu in black stone had been found in a lake at Nellikāru, some 15 km away. It was brought and installed in the shrine.
A temple. So the basadi became the temple of Ananta Padmanābha.

ZAPPY: A basadi turned into a Viṣṇu temple?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the story, and the building supports it: it looks like the basadis around it. The king offered what he had so his guest would stay.

A TEMPLE LIKE A BASADI

Granite, cloisters, a west-facing god
MaterialGranite, built to stand up to the coast’s heavy rain
LookFrom inside the cloistered walls it looks like a Jain temple, like the basadis nearby
FacingThe temple and the main image face west
AroundDāmōdara, Saṅkarṣaṇa and Pradyumna face the other three directions around the sanctum
SculptureFine carvings in the basadi manner
CareProtected as a monument by the Archaeological Survey of India

Four forms of Viṣṇu. Dāmōdara, Saṅkarṣaṇa and Pradyumna belong to the twenty-four names of Viṣṇu (the caturviṁśati mūrtis). Placing them on three sides makes the sanctum face every direction.

ANANTA PADMANĀBHA’S FESTIVALS

Lamps, a vow and the sun

🪔 Lakṣa dīpōtsava

The yearly five-day festival of a hundred thousand lamps.

🧵 Ananta vrata

The vow to Ananta Padmanābha, known locally as Nōmpu, kept on Ananta Caturdaśī with the fourteen-knotted thread.

☀️ Rathasaptamī

The sun’s chariot day in Māgha, observed each year.

📅 Every day

Worship in a protected monument: the shrine is both a living temple and a heritage site.

ZAPPY: What’s the fourteen-knotted thread?

PROF. BOLT: On Ananta Caturdaśī devotees tie a thread with fourteen knots, the Ananta dāra, for Viṣṇu as Ananta. Nōmpu is the local name for this vow.

PADU TIRUPATI

The Tirupati of the west

🧭 The name

Paḍu is “west” in Kannaḍa and Tuḷu. Tirumala lies far to the east; Kārkaḷa is its western twin.

💰 Vows

Offerings vowed to the Lord of Tirupati can be made here; once, a part was sent on to Tirupati.

👥 The community

The temple belongs to the Gauḍa Sārasvata Brāhmaṇas, a Konkaṇi-speaking community of the west coast.

🏛️ Status

Among the leading GSB temples of the coast, with a Brahmakalaśa after renovation in 2016.

ZAPPY: Why would anyone need a second Tirupati?

PROF. BOLT: For the GSB families who settled here, the real one was very far. Padu Tirupati let them keep their bond with Veṅkaṭēśa close to home.

FROM GOA TO KĀRKAḶA

Refugees and a welcoming king
1500s Under Portuguese rule in Goa, many Gauḍa Sārasvata families leave and settle along the Karnataka and Kerala coast.
First Sōire Prabhu is remembered as the first of them to come to Kārkaḷa; the priest Sōma Śarma comes with an image of Veṅkaṭaramaṇa.
Land The Jain king, named as Immaḍi Bhairava, grants land for a temple and black stone from the area.
A shed The first shrine is simple: a raised platform, four corner pillars and a roof of coconut leaves.
A second image Sōma Śarma receives another image from the priests of Tirumala and installs it beside the first.
25 Apr 1537 The date the temple gives for the installation.

ZAPPY: The dates don’t quite fit together.

PROF. BOLT: They don’t. Different accounts put the land grant as early as 1450 or as late as 1550. The installation date of 1537 is the one the temple uses.

THE WELL, THE DREAM, THE COW

How the present image came
Raiders. Bandits fell on Kārkaḷa. The GSB families fled to Mulki and hid their valuables, images included, in a well.
One lost. When they came back for them, Sōma Śarma’s image was missing. A man of Mulki had found it and installed it in a temple there.
The dream. The Lord told them in a dream to return to Kārkaḷa: the Lord of Tirupati would come to them with a holy man.
The stranger. A hermit arrived with a Viṣṇu image from Tirupati. He asked that offerings go to Tirupati now and then, and that the image be taken east once a year.
The site. After worship, a cow and her calf were let loose; where the cow rested after feeding the calf, they built the shrine.

TWO LORDS, ONE SHRINE

And who serves them
Paṭṭada dēvaruThe main image, Śrīnivāsa, the image from Tirupati
Utsava dēvaruVeṅkaṭaramaṇa, the image carried in processions
ParivāraMukhyaprāṇa (Hanumān), Garuḍa, Lakṣmī and Gaṇapati, installed about two years after the 1699–1700 renovation
PriestsThe right of worship alternates year by year between the Purāṇik and Jōśi families; a Tantri family performs the other rites
The saintA holy man who lived in Kārkaḷa was cremated on the temple’s western grounds; his tomb is honoured on certain days

An old oracle. Devotees once brought marriage, sickness and land disputes to the spirit of Basava, who spoke through a darśana pātri. The practice has stopped in recent years. Locals recall that it foretold that Kārkaḷa, not Mūḍabidri, would become the tālūk headquarters, as it did.

VĪRA MĀRUTI AND THE GARUḌA PILLARS

Stone that surprises
Vīra Māruti. Opposite the main shrine on the north stands a monolithic Hanumān, about fifteen feet tall, said to date from the 16th century under the Bhairarasas and to have been dug up near Ānekere.
A silver gift. Ṭīpu Sultān is said to have sent silver to cover the image; it was not enough, so a silver necklace was made instead, which he still wears.
The Garuḍa maṇṭapa. Its four pillars are counted marvels: carved honeybees, spiders and butterflies, and chains of loose links cut from single stones.
The carver. They are the work of Rañjāḷa Gōpāla Śeṇai, a master craftsman honoured with a national award, the same sculptor who carved the Mahākāḷī of Uppinaṅgaḍi.

VEṄKAṬARAMAṆA’S FESTIVALS

A journey east, a chariot and lamps
Vanabhōjana Both images go in procession to Śrīnivāsāśrama, about 3 km away: the Lord’s yearly journey “towards Tirupati”, as the hermit asked.
The return They pause at Maṇṇa Gōpura. Śrīnivāsa comes home on a decorated chariot and Veṅkaṭaramaṇa in a palanquin, with worship at temporary chariots, the gurjis, along the way.
Rathōtsava The annual chariot festival.
Lakṣa dīpōtsava The festival of a hundred thousand lamps, among the temple’s most famous days.
Paryāya The yearly hand-over of worship between the Purāṇik and Jōśi families, marked as a festival.

ZAPPY: The god travels east because the hermit asked?

PROF. BOLT: That’s how the tradition explains the Vanabhōjana: a symbolic yearly journey to Tirupati, as far as Śrīnivāsāśrama and back.
