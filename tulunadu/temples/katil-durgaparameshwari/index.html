ŚRĪ DURGĀPARAMEŚVARĪ · KAṬĪL · A REFERENCE GUIDE

KAṬĪL

The Goddess in the middle of the river

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

A drought, a sage, and a heavenly cow’s daughter who refused to come down to earth. Cursed to become a river, she flowed, and in the middle of her stream the Goddess rose from a rock.

That is Kaṭīl, “the middle of the land”: an island shrine of Durgāparameśvarī, who once took the form of a swarm of bees, and whose love of theatre fills the coast with night-long plays.

This guide tells her legends, her island, her fire festival and her troupes.

Oṃ Śrī Durgāyai Namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A goddess who turns into bees?

PROF. BOLT: Bees were the one thing the demon's boon forgot. When every big weapon fails, the Mother finds the small one that works.

ZAPPY: A goddess in the form of a liṅga?

PROF. BOLT: Here she rose from the rock on her own, not carved by anyone. The stone is shaped like a liṅga, and it is dressed and adorned as the Mother.

WHERE IT IS

An island in the Nandinī

The river parts. The Nandinī divides into two streams just above the shrine and joins again below it. The sanctum stands on the rock between, reached from the bank by a footbridge through two decorated arches.

The name. Kaṭi is “middle” or “waist” and iḷa is “land”: Kaṭi-iḷa, Kaṭīl, the land in the middle. Tradition says it marks the midpoint of the Nandinī’s course from her source at Kanakagiri to the sea.

Two villages. The banks on either side belong to the villages of Attūr and Koḍettūr, who meet in the temple once a year in a battle of fire.

THE MOTHER OF KAṬĪL

Durgāparameśvarī
Born from the rock. The sanctum holds no carved statue. The Goddess is a stone that rose by itself, a svayambhū form, shaped like a liṅga and decked each day with a face, crown, jewels and silk.
Two names. She is Durgāparameśvarī, Durgā the supreme Goddess, as the one who protects, and Bhrāmarī, the bee-goddess, as the one who destroyed Aruṇāsura.
The Mother’s daughter. In the Kaṭīl story the Goddess is the daughter of the river herself, so that devotees speak of the river and the shrine as mother and child.
Lover of plays. She is called Yakṣagānapriyā, “she who loves Yakṣagāna”; a play staged in her name is counted among her most pleasing offerings.

JĀBĀLI AND NANDINĪ

How the river came
The drought. Long ago, the story goes, the land suffered years without rain. The sage Jābāli resolved to perform a great sacrifice to bring the rains back, and went to Indra for the divine cow Kāmadhenu.
The refusal. Kāmadhenu was away, so Indra offered her daughter Nandinī. Nandinī refused: earth, she said, was too full of sinners for her to set foot on.
The curse. Angered, Jābāli cursed her to flow on that same earth as a river. Nandinī pleaded with the Goddess, who replied that the curse must be fulfilled, but that she herself would be born as Nandinī’s daughter in the middle of the stream, and so purify her.
The river and the rain. On Māgha śuddha pūrṇimā Nandinī sprang from Kanakagiri as a river. Jābāli performed his sacrifice on her banks and the rains returned.

THE BEES AND ARUṆĀSURA

How the Goddess was born in the river
The fugitive. When the Goddess destroyed the demons Śumbha and Niśumbha, their minister Aruṇāsura escaped and gathered a new army.
The boon. By penance he won from Brahmā a boon that no creature with two legs or four could kill him. He also gave up chanting the Gāyatrī, which the gods had hoped would weaken him.
The rock. The Goddess came to him as a beautiful woman and withdrew into a rock. In rage he struck at it with his sword.
The swarm. Out of the rock burst a host of black bees, the bhramaras. Bees have six legs, neither two nor four: the boon could not protect him, and they stung him to death.
The Goddess stays. At the gods’ prayer the Goddess took her place in the middle of the Nandinī as the rock-born Durgāparameśvarī, keeping her promise to the river.

ZAPPY: A demon lawyer’s boon, beaten on a technicality?

PROF. BOLT: Every Purāṇic villain writes his boon to cover everything and still misses one case. The lesson: no clever wording outruns the Mother.

THE ISLAND TEMPLE

Plan and approach

🌉 The crossing

Pilgrims cross from the bank to the island by the footbridge. In heavy monsoon floods the river can rise almost to the temple’s plinth.

🏛️ The building

A coastal temple of sloping roofs and carved pillars around a small dark sanctum, with a walkway round the shrine that circles the island.

Old, often renewed. The shrine is ancient by tradition, and has been rebuilt and re-consecrated many times; the most recent great renewal, a Brahmakalaśōtsava, drew thousands in 2026.

A DAY OF WORSHIP

From abhiṣeka to raṅgapūjā
Before dawnAbhiṣeka, the ritual bath, and the morning pūjās, from about 3 a.m.
MorningRice porridge, gañji, served to pilgrims
NoonMahāpūjā, followed by a free meal
DuskPradōṣa pūjā
NightRaṅgapūjā, worship with rows of lamps and drawn patterns, then the night meal

The Āsraṇṇas. The worship is done by the hereditary priests of the Āsraṇṇa family, who also oversee the temple’s Yakṣagāna troupes.

Offerings. Among the favourite offerings are raṅgapūjā, flower worship in the holy month of Siṃha, annadāna for the day’s pilgrims, and the vow-play itself, a night of Yakṣagāna.

ZAPPY: Porridge for breakfast and a meal twice a day?

PROF. BOLT: Every day. On the coast a temple that doesn’t feed people is hardly a temple. Kaṭīlamma feeds her children first and blesses them after.

THE FIRE PLAY

Attūr against Koḍettūr
A battle of devotion. During the annual jātre, men of the two neighbouring villages, Attūr and Koḍettūr, face each other before the shrine and hurl burning bundles of dried coconut fronds across at one another. It is called Tūtedhāra or agni kēḷi, “fire play”.
For the Goddess. The Mother of Kaṭīl is believed to delight in this offering of fire. It begins only after her abhiṣeka and the special sevās of the day.
Rules of play. The bundles are thrown, not held; the bare-chested players take the sparks on their bodies as her blessing; and once the play ends, the two villages leave as friends.

ZAPPY: Throwing fire at your neighbours sounds dangerous!

PROF. BOLT: It is a rite, not a riot. Generations have played it the same way, and the villagers say the Mother herself keeps the burns away.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Festivals of Kaṭīl
Jātre (Apr)About eight days from the eve of Mēṣa saṅkramaṇa: daily utsavabali, a silver-chariot procession, the great rathōtsava and the fire play
Siṃha māsa (Aug–Sep)The Goddess’s holy month, with special flower worship from Siṃha saṅkramaṇa
NavarātriNine nights of the Goddess; the temple stays open late
Māgha pūrṇimāThe day the Nandinī first flowed
Mela season (Nov–May)The troupes set out on their tour after the rains and return to perform before the Goddess at its close

Chariots. Kaṭīl has wooden chariots for the great festival and a silver chariot for the Goddess’s smaller processions.

THE KAṬĪL MELA

Theatre as an offering
The troupe of the Goddess. The Kaṭīl Śrī Durgāparameśvarī Prasādita Daśāvatāra Yakṣagāna Maṇḍaḷi, the Kaṭīl Mela, was founded in the middle of the 19th century and plays in the Teṅkutiṭṭu style of the south.
Vow shows. A family whose prayer is answered vows a night of Yakṣagāna to the Goddess, a harake. The mela performs these shows without tickets, wherever the family lives; it is believed the Goddess herself comes to the place where her mela plays.
From one to seven. One troupe for a century; more were added from the 1980s as the waiting lists grew. A seventh troupe began on 16 November 2025, and the seven together gave more than 1,000 shows in the 2025–26 season. Bookings still run years ahead.
Her favourite play. The most requested story is Śrī Devī Mahātme, the Goddess’s own glory, drawn from the Devī Māhātmya · 19 parts.
All night or not. In 2022 the mela tried short evening shows; after objections from patrons and a court case over noise rules, it returned to the traditional dusk-to-dawn shows in January 2024.

AROUND KAṬĪL

The river and the coast
KanakagiriThe hill where, by tradition, the Nandinī rises
Attūr and KoḍettūrThe villages of the two banks, partners in the fire play
MūḍabidriThe Jain temple town of a thousand-pillared basadi, inland to the north-east
MaṅgaḷūruThe city of Maṅgaḷādevī, Kadri and Kudroḷi, to the south-west

The Goddess of the coast. Kaṭīl is one of several great Durgā shrines of Tuḷunāḍu, including Bappanāḍu, Poḷali and Mandārthi, each with its own legend; Mandārthi, too, sends out Yakṣagāna troupes in the Goddess’s name.
