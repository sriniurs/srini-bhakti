KŌṬEŚVARA

The Lord of a crore of sages

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

A crore is ten million. Tradition says that many sages once sat in penance here, and Śiva, pleased, appeared before every one of them.

So the place became Kōṭi-īśvara, “the Lord of a crore”: Kōṭeśvara. Its Śiva is Kōṭiliṅgeśvara, and beneath his pedestal, it is said, lie a thousand liṅgas more.

This guide tells the legends, the king who paid his vow with a chariot of sugarcane, and the great festival that newly-weds never miss.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Ten million sages? Where did they all sit?

PROF. BOLT: The legends of this coast don’t count with a calculator! “A crore” means “countless”: so many that Śiva had to multiply himself to bless them all.

ZAPPY: Why “Dhvajapura”?

PROF. BOLT: “The town of the banner”. The name is old, and why it was given is not recorded. Banners, at least, have never been short at Koḍi Habba!

WHERE IT IS

Just south of Kundāpura

Near Kundāpura. Kōṭeśvara lies on the coastal plain a few kilometres south of Kundāpura, a town surrounded by water on three sides where the Pañcagaṅgāvaḷi river nears the sea.

Old ports. Up the river lies Basrūr, once a busy port whose rulers are linked by inscriptions with this temple. To the west, beaches such as Koḍi and Bījāḍi line the shore.

KŌṬILIṄGEŚVARA

Śiva of the crore
The liṅga. Śiva is worshipped as a liṅga, dressed with a silver face of the Lord, so that worshippers see both the formless sign and the face of God together.
The hidden liṅgas. Below the pedestal, it is said, is a well holding about a thousand small liṅgas, submerged in water. It is this unseen host that the name Kōṭiliṅgeśvara, “Lord of a crore of liṅgas”, calls to mind.
Nandi. A great stone Nandi, Śiva’s bull, sits facing the sanctum.

ZAPPY: You can’t see the thousand liṅgas?

PROF. BOLT: No, they're under water, under the pedestal. You worship one, and by faith you worship them all.

THE LEGENDS

Sages, a creator and five brothers
A crore of sages. Countless sages, a crore by tradition, gathered here and performed penance to Śiva. He appeared before each one, and the place became Kōṭi-īśvara, then Kōṭeśvara.
Brahmā’s penance. Another tradition says Brahmā himself sat in penance here and worshipped Kōṭiliṅgeśvara.
Paraśurāma’s kṣetra. As one of the seven Muktisthalas, Kōṭeśvara is counted among the sacred places Paraśurāma established on the land he won from the sea.
The Pāṇḍavas. Local belief holds that the present temple was built by the five Pāṇḍava brothers.

A pond of sages. One telling joins the legends to the great tank beside the temple: the crore of sages, it is said, were absorbed into its waters, which is why it is called Kōṭi Tīrtha, the tank of the crore.

KING VASU’S VOW

A chariot of sugarcane
A childless king. Mahārāja Vasu, a ruler of Basrūr, had no children. He vowed to Kōṭiliṅgeśvara that if he were blessed with a child, he would build the Lord a temple at Kōṭeśvara.
The blessing. Children were born to him, and he built the temple as he had promised.
No chariot in time. When the festival came, the temple chariot was not finished. Rather than let the Lord go without his procession, the people built a chariot of bamboo and sugarcane and drew him round on it.
Remembered every year. The Koḍi Habba is held in memory of that sugarcane chariot, and newly married couples still come to the Lord who answered a king’s prayer for children.

HISTORY

From Paraśurāma to Basrūr
Legend Paraśurāma establishes the Muktisthala; the crore of sages; Brahmā’s penance.
Legend The Pāṇḍavas build the temple, by local belief.
10th–11th c. The Āḷupa king Kundavarma builds the Kundeśvara temple nearby, from which, it is said, Kundāpura takes its name.
Medieval Stone inscriptions in the area link the temple with the rulers of Basrūr, the river port up the Pañcagaṅgāvaḷi.
Undated King Vasu of Basrūr builds the temple after the birth of his children; the first sugarcane chariot.

Legend and record. The two kinds of history fit together here: local belief speaks of the Pāṇḍavas, the inscriptions of Basrūr’s kings, and the temple’s best-loved story of a Basrūr king’s vow.

ZAPPY: Who really built it then, the Pāṇḍavas or King Vasu?

PROF. BOLT: The stones point to Basrūr. The Pāṇḍavas are how villagers say “very, very old”. The Vasu story is the one everybody acts out each year.

The look. Kōṭeśvara is a classic coastal temple in the Kerala manner, with sloping tiled roofs, carved woodwork and laterite walls, recognisable from far off across the open ground of the car street.

KŌṬI TĪRTHA

The great tank
The largest of its kind. Beside the temple lies Kōṭi Tīrtha, a tank of about four acres, described as the largest temple tank, puṣkariṇī, in Karnataka.
Its shape. Steps run down on all four sides to the water, with a small maṇḍapa standing in the middle. Fish swim in it.
Its power. Its water is believed to heal, and it plays a part in the rites of the Koḍi Habba.
A tunnel tale. Local legend says an underground passage leads from the tank to Vaṇḍāru Kambaḷa Gadde, a field some twenty kilometres away, used for the buffalo races.

THE KOḌI HABBA

The fair of the newly-weds
When. Every year in the solar month of Vṛścika, which falls in November–December.
The chariot. The festival image of Kōṭiliṅgeśvara is drawn round the car street on the great Brahmaratha, in a procession of folk dancers, drums and fireworks.
The couples. Newly married couples are the heart of the fair. They worship the Lord together and take home a pair of sugarcane stalks, believed to bless them with healthy children, just as the Lord blessed King Vasu.
The crowds. The Koḍi Habba draws lakhs of people from across coastal Karnataka and beyond, and is counted among the great fairs of the coast.

Sugarcane everywhere. In the days of the fair the roads into Kōṭeśvara are lined with stacks of sugarcane, bought in pairs by every couple and many families too: the sweetest reminder of a king’s answered prayer.

ZAPPY: Why do couples take home two sugarcanes, not one?

PROF. BOLT: A pair for a pair! One for each of them, together, for the children they hope will come.
