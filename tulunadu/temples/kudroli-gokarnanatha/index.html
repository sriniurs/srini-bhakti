ŚRĪ GOKARṆANĀTHA KṢETRA · KUDROḶI · A REFERENCE GUIDE

KUDROḶI

A temple whose door opened for everyone

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Most temples on this coast are counted in centuries. Kudroḷi is counted from a single year: 1912.

It was built because the people who built it were not allowed into the old temples. They asked a saint from Kerala to help, and he came, bringing a Śiva liṅga with him and a teaching: one caste, one religion, one God for all.

This guide tells how Gokarṇanātha came to Maṅgaḷūru, and how a young temple became the heart of the city’s Dasarā.

Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Only a hundred-odd years old? Is that even “old” for a temple?

PROF. BOLT: Old isn’t the point here. Kudroḷi matters for what it opened, not for how long it has stood.

ZAPPY: What does “Ādi” mean for the Billavas?

PROF. BOLT: “First”, “original”. Kudroḷi is the mother temple of the community, the one from which others were inspired.

WHERE IT IS

In old Maṅgaḷūru

Near the old port. Kudroḷi lies in the north-west of the city, close to the old harbour and the mouth of the Phalguṇi (Gurupura) river, a few kilometres from the centre.

The Dasarā route. At Navarātri the whole city’s attention turns here: the great procession leaves from the temple and returns to it after winding through the main streets of Maṅgaḷūru.

GOKARṆANĀTHA

Śiva of Gokarṇa, in Maṅgaḷūru
The name. Gokarṇa, “cow’s ear”, is the great Śiva shrine of the coast to the north, home of Mahābaleśvara and his Ātmaliṅga, and one of Paraśurāma’s seven Muktisthalas. Nārāyaṇa Guru gave the new temple that ancient name: Gokarṇanātha, the Lord of Gokarṇa.
The liṅga. The Guru brought the liṅga with him from Kerala and consecrated it himself.
An old god for new devotees. By naming the shrine after one of the oldest kṣetras of the coast, the Guru gave people long kept outside the temples a Śiva as ancient and as great as any.

ZAPPY: Why not give it a brand-new name?

PROF. BOLT: Because the point wasn’t a new god. It was the same god, at last open to everyone. An old name said: this is yours too, and always was.

NĀRĀYAṆA GURU

The saint who consecrated temples for all

🧘 Who he was

Śrī Nārāyaṇa Guru (1856–1928), a saint, philosopher and reformer of Kerala, born into the Ezhava community, which shared the Billavas’ place in the old caste order.

🔱 Aruvippuram, 1888

He consecrated a Śiva liṅga at Aruvippuram himself, though not a Brahmin. Challenged, he replied that he had consecrated “our Śiva”: a god for those to whom the temples were closed.

📜 His teaching

“One caste, one religion, one God for humankind.” He taught Advaita Vedānta and practical reform together: education, cleanliness, and temples open to all.

🛕 His temples

He consecrated many temples across Kerala and beyond. Kudroḷi is the only one he founded in Karnataka.

ZAPPY: A saint who consecrated temples without being a priest by caste?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. He held that devotion and knowledge, not birth, make a person fit to serve God. Kudroḷi was built on that idea.

THE FOUNDING

Koragappa’s quest
A closed door. In early 20th-century Maṅgaḷūru the Billavas, a large community of the coast, were barred from entering many temples. Hoige Bazar Koragappa, a respected Billava businessman and elder, was pained by this and resolved that his people should have a temple of their own.
1908: a delegation. Koragappa led a group of Billava elders to Kerala to meet Nārāyaṇa Guru and invited him to guide them in building a Śiva temple. The Guru agreed.
Choosing the spot. When the Guru reached Maṅgaḷūru, Koragappa gave him a horse-drawn carriage to go about the town and select the site himself. He chose Kudroḷi.
1912: the consecration. The Guru installed the liṅga he had brought from Kerala and named the temple Gokarṇanātha. The temple was built in the Kerala style of the time.

Koragappa’s family still keeps the chair on which the Guru sat in their house.

HISTORY

From 1908 to today
1888 Nārāyaṇa Guru consecrates the Śiva of Aruvippuram in Kerala.
1908 Koragappa’s delegation invites the Guru.
1912 The Guru consecrates Gokarṇanātha at Kudroḷi.
1928 Passing of Nārāyaṇa Guru.
1991 The renovated temple is inaugurated; the first Maṅgaḷūru Dasarā procession, the Śōbhāyātre, is introduced.
2011 Over 2,000 widows perform a Caṇḍikā hōma at the temple.
2012 The temple’s centenary.
Oct 2013 Two widows, Lakṣmī Śānti and Indirā Śānti, begin serving as priests.
Sep 2014 Two more widows, from Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe communities, are appointed as priests.

The renovation. The 1991 rebuilding was led by H. Somasundar, Koragappa’s son, and B. Janārdhana Pūjāry, the temple’s managing trustee and a former Union minister.

The look. The 1991 temple is a large, bright complex of gold-toned gopuras and halls, a modern take on the old Kerala form, built to hold the crowds of its festivals.

ZAPPY: The founder-saint has a shrine of his own?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and he is worshipped there with pūjā like the gods around him. For the community he is the one who opened the door.

THE MAṄGAḶŪRU DASARĀ

The common people’s Dasarā
Nine plus one. For Navarātri, clay images of the Navadurgās, the nine forms of the Goddess, are installed together with Śāradā, the Goddess of learning, in the temple’s great hall. Organisers describe it as the only temple to install all nine Durgās and Śāradā together.
Ten days of worship. Pūjās through the nine nights, with Sarasvatī or Śāradā pūjā, music, dance and Yakṣagāna on the temple’s stages.
No king, no state. Unlike the royal Dasarā of Mysuru, the Maṅgaḷūru Dasarā has neither royal nor government patronage. It is carried by the temple and the city’s people, and is called the common man’s Dasarā.
The crowds. Organisers estimate that more than 12 lakh people visit the temple across the festival, and over 5 lakh line the procession route.

THE ŚŌBHĀYĀTRE

The great procession
Vijayadaśamī evening. On the last day the Navadurgās and Śāradā leave the temple in a great procession, the Śōbhāyātre, each on her own decorated float.
Through the city. The procession winds through the main streets of Maṅgaḷūru with tableaux, folk troupes, music and the pilivēṣa tiger dancers, through the night.
The immersion. At dawn the images return to the temple, and are immersed in the temple tank.

One Dasarā, many temples. The city’s Dasarā is wider than Kudroḷi: Maṅgaḷādevī’s chariot to Mārnamikaṭṭe and the Śāradā festival of Car Street are older strands of it. But since 1991 it is the Kudroḷi procession that the city calls “the” Maṅgaḷūru Dasarā.

ZAPPY: Tigers in a temple procession?

PROF. BOLT: The pilivēṣa dancers, painted in stripes from head to toe, are Tuḷunāḍu’s own Navarātri custom, said to honour the Goddess whose mount is a tiger.

WOMEN AT THE ALTAR

The Guru’s teaching in practice
The widows’ hōma. Widows on the coast were long kept away from auspicious rites. In 2011 more than two thousand of them performed a Caṇḍikā hōma together at Kudroḷi.
Priests, 2013. At the start of the 2013 Dasarā, two widows, Lakṣmī Śānti and Indirā Śānti, were ordained as priests, entering in a procession with music, and performed pūjā to the Goddess Śāradā. They also serve the main deity when the regular priests are away.
Priests, 2014. The next year two more widows, from Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe communities, were appointed priests and performed worship at the shrines of the Navagrahas, Annapūrṇēśvarī, Gaṇapati, Śani, the Navadurgās and the Guru.
Lamps at Dīpāvali. Since then, widows by the thousand have gathered at the temple for Lakṣmī pūjā at Dīpāvali.

The reason given. The temple trust has said these steps follow Nārāyaṇa Guru’s principle that all are equal before God, and that devotion, not birth or circumstance, makes a person fit to worship.
