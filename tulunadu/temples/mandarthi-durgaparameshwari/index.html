MANDĀRTHI

The Goddess who loves the all-night play

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Across the Uḍupi countryside, on a winter night, you may come upon a lit stage in a field, a crowd on mats, and the ceṇḍe calling. Often the troupe on that stage belongs to a goddess: Durgāparameśvarī of Mandārthi.

Families who make a vow to her keep it by hosting a night of Yakṣagāna in her name. So many vows have been made that her five troupes are booked for years, and for some families, decades, ahead.

This guide tells the Goddess’s own story, the robber who brought her the dancers’ anklets, and how her troupes work.

Śrī Durgāparameśvaryai namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A goddess who wants theatre as an offering?

PROF. BOLT: Here it’s the favourite offering. People promise her a play, and when the prayer is answered, they host it.

ZAPPY: Nāga Sundarī?

PROF. BOLT: “The serpent beauty”. The Goddess here is linked with a serpent princess, Mandarati, from whom the place is said to take its name.

WHERE IT IS

Inland from the Uḍupi coast

The village. Mandārthi lies inland from Brahmāvara in Uḍupi district, in the Baḍagu tiṭṭu country of Yakṣagāna.

The temple. An old temple with a tall gateway and a flag-mast before it, and the small tank Nāgatīrtha beside it.

The river of the story. The temple legend says the Goddess’s image was found in the Vārāhī river, which reaches the sea to the north.

THE FIVE SERPENT PRINCESSES

How Mandarati came here
The king of the serpents. Śaṅkhacūḍa, king of Nāgalōka, had five daughters: Dēvarati, Nāgarati, Cārurati, Mandarati and Nīlarati.
A wish. All five wished to marry Subrahmaṇya, and set out for Kailāsa.
The curse. On the way, Nandī stopped them and cursed them to become snakes, but said a royal person would one day free them.
Fallen to earth. As snakes they fell to earth. The sage Vyāghrapāda sheltered them; later an exiled king, Devavarma, without knowing it, set them free near an anthill.
Mandarati. One of them, Mandarati, came to the forest that took her name: Mandārthi.

ZAPPY: The village is named after a snake?

PROF. BOLT: After a serpent princess. That’s why the Goddess here is also called Nāga Sundarī, and why the tank is the Nāgatīrtha.

DEVAVARMA AND MAHIṢA

The Goddess comes to stay
A king in exile. Devavarma married the princess Jalajākṣī, rescued her from the demon Mahiṣa, and took shelter with the sage Sudēvamuni.
The attack. Mahiṣa came after them. The Goddess Durgā appeared and destroyed the demons, with her attendant spirits, among them Vīrabhadra, Haiguḷi and Bobbarya.
A promise. Pleased with their devotion, Durgā promised to remain at Mandārthi as Śrī Durgāparameśvarī.
The finding. Guided by a dream from Subrahmaṇya, Devavarma found her image in the Vārāhī river and installed it where the temple stands.

THE GODDESS OF MANDĀRTHI

Durgā, Cāmuṇḍā, serpent queen

⚔️ Mahiṣāsuramardinī

The Goddess as slayer of the buffalo demon, the main stone image, fitting her story of Mahiṣa’s end.

🔥 Cāmuṇḍeśvarī

A second stone image, the fierce Cāmuṇḍā, worshipped with her.

🐍 Nāga Sundarī

Praise-name recalling Mandarati, the serpent princess of the place.

🎭 Nine masks

The temple keeps nine old masks showing different forms of the Goddess.

ZAPPY: Why two images?

PROF. BOLT: Both are forms of the one Goddess: the battle-queen who killed Mahiṣa, and the fierce Cāmuṇḍā. Devotees approach her in both.

SURGŌḶI ANTU AND THE ANKLETS

How Yakṣagāna came to the Goddess
A robber with a cause. Surgōḷi Antu robbed the rich and gave to the poor. He set out to rob a Gaṇeśa temple on a hill, but a great elephant blocked the way.
A prayer. He begged Gaṇeśa not to stop his work for the poor. Gaṇeśa told him to come alone, with a pure heart, and take the first thing he found.
The box. Before the temple he found a box. Near Mandārthi it grew too heavy to carry; inside were a Gaṇeśa image and the ankle bells of a Yakṣagāna dancer.
Bārāḷi Gaṇapati. The image would not move, so it was installed where it lay: Bārāḷi Gaṇapati.
The voice. On the way back, Antu’s helper tied on the bells and began to dance. A voice from the sky told Antu to offer the anklets to the Goddess at Mandārthi, and so the Goddess’s troupe began.

THE MANDĀRTHI MĒḶA

Five troupes on the road
NameMandārthi Śrī Durgāparameśvarī Daśāvatāra Yakṣagāna Mēḷa
Run byThe temple administration
TroupesFive; there were four until demand led the temple to add a fifth
Each troupeAbout 45 members, with its own bus
StyleBaḍagu tiṭṭu, the northern school
PlaysEpisodes of the Rāmāyaṇa and the Mahābhārata
DressingThe artists dress and prepare at the Bārāḷi temple before going out

ZAPPY: How old is the mēḷa?

PROF. BOLT: Sources disagree. One report calls it more than five centuries old and says it appears in Vādirāja’s writings; others speak of a century. The Sources page notes both.

HARAKE: THE PLAY AS A VOW

Why the bookings run for years
The vow. A devotee makes a harake to the Goddess: if this prayer is answered, I will give you a night of Yakṣagāna.
The booking. The family books one of her troupes to play in her name, usually in their own village or yard, as a Yakṣagāna sēve.
No tickets. Harake troupes don’t sell seats. They play only where a devotee has made a vow, and the whole village comes free.
The queue. Demand has outrun the troupes. One report counted over 12,000 shows booked up to 2027–28; a later one, over 19,000 booked up to 2044.
At the temple. To clear the backlog, the troupes have also played every day on the temple premises for years, and give shows in the monsoon off-season too.

A SEASON OF THE TROUPES

From the first bells to the last
Opening The Prathama sēve āṭa, the season’s first performance: five Gaṇeśa images installed with a Gaṇa hōma, an all-night show, and the Gejje sēve, worship of the ankle bells.
Late Nov The five troupes set out on tour.
Nov–May Night after night in the villages of the coast, wherever a vow is due.
Navarātri On the last day of the temple’s Navarātri, all five troupes perform as a Yakṣagāna sēve.
Late May The season ends around Pattanāje, before the rains.
Monsoon Shows at the temple and special performances in the off-season.

ZAPPY: Why worship the bells first?

PROF. BOLT: The story says the mēḷa began with a pair of anklets given to the Goddess. Each season starts by honouring them.

THE KENDA SĒVE

Walking on fire

What it is. Kenda is a bed of glowing embers. In the kenda sēve, devotees who have vowed it walk across the coals before the Goddess.

When. It opens the temple’s great festival in the month of Kumbha, and is the rite most visitors come to see.

THE JĀTRE

Three days in Kumbha
Day 1Kenda sēve; milk offered to Subrahmaṇya; ḍakke bali; raṅga pūje
Day 2The Mahā Rathōtsava, the great chariot festival; worship in the valaga maṇṭapa; śayanōtsava, the Goddess put to rest
Day 3Gaṇa hōma; worship in the Lakṣmī maṇṭapa; tulābhāra, devotees weighed against offerings; palanquin procession; kere dīpōtsava, lamps on the tank
WhenFrom Kumbha saṅkramaṇa, about mid-February

ZAPPY: Ḍakke bali here too?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The serpent strand of the story runs right through the festival: the ḍakke bali for the nāgas, and milk for Subrahmaṇya.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Other days at Mandārthi

🪔 Navarātri

Nine days: daily Caṇḍikā hōma, Vēda recitation, a new adornment each day, evening worship with offerings from eight households, and on the last day all five troupes on stage.

🐘 Gaṇēśōtsava

Two days at Gaṇeśa Caturthī, run by a local committee: Gaṇa hōma, village games and a closing procession.

🔱 Cāmuṇḍēśvarī

At the nearby Cāmuṇḍēśvarī shrine, a māri pūje in Kumbha.

🌙 Kallu Maliṅgēśvara

A nearby Śiva temple with Kārtika lamps and a Yakṣagāna sēve at Śivarātri.
