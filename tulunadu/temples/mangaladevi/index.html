MAṄGAḶĀDEVĪ

The Goddess who named a city

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Many cities are named after a king, a river or a fort. Maṅgaḷūru is named after a Goddess.

In a quiet neighbourhood near the river mouth stands the shrine of Maṅgaḷādevī, “the auspicious Goddess”. Her story joins a princess who walked away from her palace, two wandering yogis from the north, and a king who dug up a buried temple.

This guide tells those stories, and how the city’s Navarātri still ends at her door.

Oṃ Śrī Maṅgaḷādevyai Namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: So everyone who says “Mangalore” is saying her name?

PROF. BOLT: Every time! Maṅgaḷa-ūru, “the town of Maṅgaḷā”. Every letter posted to the city carries her name.

ZAPPY: What does “Mahatōbhāra” mean?

PROF. BOLT: It’s a title for a great, powerful deity, used for many coastal temples. Think of it as “the Most Mighty”.

WHERE IT IS

Bōḷāra, near the river mouth

South of the centre. Bōḷāra lies in the southern part of the city, a few kilometres from its heart, near where the Netrāvatī turns to meet the sea.

Three shrines, one city. Maṅgaḷādevī in the south, Kadri on its hill to the east, and Kudroḷi to the north frame old Maṅgaḷūru.

The katte. A short way from the temple is Mārnamikaṭṭe, “the platform of Mārnami”, where the Goddess goes in procession at the end of Navarātri.

THE AUSPICIOUS GODDESS

Maṅgaḷādevī
The name. Maṅgaḷā means auspicious, bringer of good fortune. As Maṅgaḷādevī she is worshipped as a form of Śakti, the Mother who protects her city.
Śiva at her side. A small liṅga stands to the left of the Goddess in the sanctum, so that she is worshipped with her lord beside her.
Many forms in one. Through Navarātri she is honoured in turn as different goddesses: Caṇḍikā, Mahāsarasvatī, Vāgdevī; the same Mother wearing the faces of power, learning and speech.
A Goddess of blessings. Devotees come to her above all for auspicious beginnings: marriages, new homes and new ventures.

THE PRINCESS AND THE YOGI

The first story
A princess of Malabar. Long ago, the story goes, a princess of the Malabar coast, Parimaḷā or Premaladevī, gave up her kingdom and became a disciple of Matsyendranātha, the founder of the Nātha tradition.
A new name. When she took up his path, Matsyendranātha renamed her Maṅgaḷādevī.
The journey. She travelled north with her teacher along the coast. Near Bōḷāra she fell ill and could go no further, and stayed behind.
The shrine. When she died, the people of the place consecrated a temple in her honour, and the settlement around it took her name.

ZAPPY: So she was a real woman who became a goddess?

PROF. BOLT: In this version, yes, the way many on this coast are. In the next story she was the Goddess from the start. The temple keeps both.

THE KING AND THE BURIED SHRINE

The second story
Two sages from Nepal. In the 9th century, when Kundavarman, the most celebrated king of the Āḷupa dynasty, ruled Tuḷunāḍu, two Nātha saints, Macchindranātha (Matsyendranātha) and Gōrakhanātha, arrived from Nepal, seeking the holy land where Paraśurāma had lived.
A temple under the earth. They told the king that an ancient shrine of the Goddess, set up by Paraśurāma himself, lay buried at the spot, hidden for ages under the soil.
Uncovered and rebuilt. Kundavarman had the place dug up. Under the sages’ guidance he raised a grand new shrine to Maṅgaḷādevī on the sacred ground.
A lasting bond. The same Nātha sages are remembered at Kadri, and the two temples have kept their connection ever since.

Three stories, one Goddess. Local tradition gives three accounts of the shrine’s origin: Paraśurāma’s ancient foundation, Kundavarman’s rebuilding with the Nātha sages, and the princess who became the Goddess. Devotees do not choose between them; each explains a part of what the place means.

Another theory. Some historians link the name Maṅgaḷā with the Buddhist goddess Tārā, worshipped in the Vajrayāna tradition that flourished at Kadri before the Nāthas.

WHO WERE THE NĀTHAS?

The yogis of the earrings

🧘 A yoga order

The Nātha sampradāya is a tradition of Śaiva yogis who trace their lineage to Ādinātha, Śiva himself, through the teacher Matsyendranātha and his disciple Gōrakhanātha.

💍 Split ears

Nātha yogis wear large earrings through the cartilage of the ear, which gave them the name Kānphaṭa, “split-eared”.

🌍 From Nepal to the coast

Nātha monasteries spread from Nepal and the Himālaya across India. Kadri in Maṅgaḷūru became one of their seats in the south.

🔥 Haṭha yoga

Gōrakhanātha is credited with shaping haṭha yoga, the yoga of the body and breath, from which much of modern yoga practice descends.

ZAPPY: So the yogis who shaped haṭha yoga walked through Maṅgaḷūru?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the tradition, and Kadri still has a Nātha maṭha to show for it. Next time someone does a headstand, they owe a little to this coast.

HISTORY

From Āḷupa shrine to city temple
Ancient Tradition credits Paraśurāma with the first shrine.
9th–10th c. The Āḷupa king Kundavarman builds the temple with the Nātha sages (sources give the 9th or 10th century).
Āḷupa era Maṅgaḷūru serves as one of the Āḷupa capitals.
Medieval The port of “Mangalore” grows famous with Arab, Persian and European traders, carrying the Goddess’s name across the seas.
1799 Maṅgaḷūru becomes headquarters of British South Canara.
1 Nov 2014 The city is officially renamed Maṅgaḷūru, restoring the local form of her name.

Names on the map. Travellers and traders wrote the city as Mangalor, Mangalur and Mangalore; in Tuḷu it is Kuḍla, in Koṅkaṇi Koḍiyāl, in Beary Maikāla. All the Kannaḍa and Sanskrit forms keep the Goddess at their root.

THE TEMPLE

A Kerala-style shrine

🏠 Kerala style

Sloping tiled roofs that shed the monsoon, a wooden-pillared outer hall, and a sanctum kept low and lamp-lit, the classic form of the coast from Kerala to Tuḷunāḍu.

🛕 Two-tier gopura

The entrance tower has two storeys, modest beside the towering gopurams of Tamiḻ Nāḍu, and opens on a courtyard broad enough for festival crowds.

Old and renewed. Like most coastal temples, Maṅgaḷādevī has been rebuilt and re-consecrated many times; what the visitor sees is the 9th-century foundation in the clothes of later centuries.

NAVARĀTRI

The city’s Mārnemi
Days 1–6Special pūjās every day, with music, dance, drama and devotional programmes in the temple grounds
Day 7The Goddess worshipped as Caṇḍikā, also called Mārikāmbā
Day 8As Mahāsarasvatī
Day 9, MahānavamīAs Vāgdevī, goddess of speech; āyudha pūjā, the worship of tools and weapons
Day 10, VijayadaśamīThe great rathōtsava: the Goddess rides her chariot to Mārnamikaṭṭe

To the śamī tree. At Mārnamikaṭṭe the Goddess is worshipped together with the śamī tree, the tree of victory in the Mahābhārata, where the Pāṇḍavas hid their weapons in exile.

Part of Maṅgaḷūru Dasarā. In Tuḷu the festival is Mārnemi. The city’s Dasarā is shared among several temples; Maṅgaḷādevī’s chariot is one of its oldest strands.

ZAPPY: Why worship a tree on victory day?

PROF. BOLT: Because Arjuna took his bow back from a śamī tree before winning the war. Bowing to the śamī is asking for that same victory.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Other festivals
Rathōtsava (Mar)The annual chariot festival in spring, drawing large crowds
KārtikaDīpōtsava, the festival of lamps
FridaysThe Goddess’s day of the week, with the temple open later in the evening

Two chariots a year. Maṅgaḷādevī rides out twice: at the spring festival in her own honour, and at Vijayadaśamī for the whole city.
