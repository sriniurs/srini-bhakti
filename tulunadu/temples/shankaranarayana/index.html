KRODHA ŚAṄKARANĀRĀYAṆA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

ŚAṄKARANĀRĀYAṆA

Hari and Hara in one hidden stone

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

Śiva and Viṣṇu are often painted as rivals by their followers. In a green valley below the Western Ghats they share a single stone.

At Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa, Śaṅkara (Śiva) and Nārāyaṇa (Viṣṇu) are worshipped as one self-born liṅga, hidden below the floor of the sanctum, under water that never dries.

This guide tells the story of the angry sage who called them both, the demons they destroyed together, and the shrine that grew over the water.

Oṃ Śrī Śaṅkaranārāyaṇāya Namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Śiva-people and Viṣṇu-people in one temple? No arguments?

PROF. BOLT: Here there’s nothing to argue about. The god himself is both. Fight over him and you are fighting with half of him!

ZAPPY: A whole village named after the temple?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Here the god came first and the village grew around him. Ask for the place and you are saying his name.

WHERE IT IS

Below the Ghats

Inland. Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa lies some 30 km east of Kundāpura on the road that climbs to Āgumbe in the Western Ghats: a valley of paddy, areca and forest, near the river Vārāhī.

Away from the coast. Unlike the seaside Muktisthalas, this one sits in the hills, closer to the Ghats than to the sea.

WHO IS ŚAṄKARANĀRĀYAṆA?

Harihara, the god who is two

🔱 Śaṅkara

Śiva, the auspicious, lord of the ascetics, with matted hair, the crescent moon, the trident and ash.

🌀 Nārāyaṇa

Viṣṇu, the preserver, crowned and jewelled, with the conch and the discus, dark as a rain-cloud.

One god, two halves. In art, Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa, also called Harihara, is shown as a single figure split down the middle: the right half Śiva, the left half Viṣṇu. The image teaches that the two great gods are one reality seen in two ways.

On this coast. Harihara worship is old in Karnataka. The Muktisthala of Kumbhāśi has its own Harihara shrine, and the Hoysaḷa temple at Harihara on the Tuṅgabhadrā is famous; but Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa is where the union is the main deity of a great kṣetra.

ZAPPY: So which half is on which side?

PROF. BOLT: Śiva on the right, Viṣṇu on the left, the usual way, though the Lord here is a liṅga you can’t see. The idea matters more than the drawing.

THE HIDDEN LIṄGA

God under water
Self-born, below ground. The god is an udbhava liṅga, a stone that rose by itself, about a foot below the floor of the sanctum. The shrine was built around it, over it.
Never seen directly. The liṅga cannot be seen straight on. Devotees see only its reflection, in a mirror.
Water that never fails. All year round, water stands around the liṅga. It is called Śuddhāmṛta Tīrtha, “the water of pure nectar”, and is given to devotees as tīrtha.
The images above. Silver-plated images of Śaṅkara and Nārāyaṇa stand in the sanctum, so that the hidden union can be worshipped in visible form.

“Built on water.” Local tradition says the whole temple was built over a lake or spring, which is why water rises around the liṅga.

THE SAGE KRODHA

How Hari and Hara came together
Two demons. The Padma Purāṇa tells of two demons, Kharāsura and Raṭṭāsura, who terrorised the land below the Sahyādri.
A sage’s penance. The sage Krodha retired to a cave in the hills and performed fierce penance to free the world from them.
Two gods as one. Pleased with his devotion, Śiva and Viṣṇu came together, as Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa, and destroyed both demons.
They stayed. At the sage’s prayer the united Lord remained here for the good of the world, and the place became Krodha Kṣetra, the kṣetra of Krodha.

ZAPPY: A sage called Krodha, “anger”?

PROF. BOLT: A fierce name for fierce penance. His anger was at the demons, not the world. Here it turned into devotion strong enough to call two gods at once.

KĀMADHENU’S MILK

The marks in the sanctum
The divine cow. Tradition holds that Kāmadhenu, the wish-granting cow of heaven, stood at this spot and let her milk flow continuously onto the hidden liṅga in worship.
Her footprints. Marks in the sanctum are believed by devotees to be Kāmadhenu’s hoof-prints, left from that worship.
Milk to water. Some devotees see in the water that never dries around the liṅga the continuing gift of her milk, the Śuddhāmṛta, the pure nectar.

A coastal pattern. Stories of a cow pouring milk on a hidden liṅga are told at many South Indian shrines; they usually explain how a self-born stone was first discovered. At Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa the cow is no village cow but Kāmadhenu herself.

HISTORY

Purāṇa and stone
Purāṇic Krodha’s penance; the slaying of Khara and Raṭṭa; the Lord stays.
Purāṇic Paraśurāma counts the kṣetra among his seven Muktisthalas.
Text The Padma Purāṇa, Puṣkara Khaṇḍa, chapter 24, describes Krodha Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa; the Skanda Purāṇa also refers to it.
11th–12th c. The present temple, by the Karnataka State Gazetteer’s dating.
Later Wood carving and a bronze Harihara image added to the shrine’s treasures.

ZAPPY: Is the liṅga older than the temple?

PROF. BOLT: By faith, it’s as old as the world: self-born, not made. The building around it is what historians can date.

THE TEMPLE

Shrine and treasures
Udbhava liṅgaBelow the floor, seen in a mirror, surrounded by water
Silver imagesŚaṅkara and Nārāyaṇa, in the sanctum
Bronze HariharaA fine image of the united god
WoodworkCarved wood in the halls, in the coastal manner
Kōṭi TīrthaThe temple tank, whose water devotees sprinkle on themselves before darśan
Silver palanquinFor the Lord’s visits to the village

Water first. Before facing the Lord, devotees perform prokṣaṇa, sprinkling water from the Kōṭi Tīrtha on themselves; then they receive the Śuddhāmṛta from within the sanctum. Water opens and closes the visit.

THROUGH THE YEAR

Festivals of Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa
Jātre (Jan)About ten days in January, with the rathōtsava on the seventh or eighth day
Kaṭṭe pūjeThe Lord visits devotees’ platforms through the village in the silver palanquin
MahāśivarātriFor Śaṅkara
Vaikuṇṭha EkādaśīFor Nārāyaṇa

Both calendars. As the god is both, the kṣetra keeps the great nights of both: Śivarātri for one half, Ekādaśī for the other, and one chariot for the whole.

KRODHA GIRI

The sage’s hill
The hill. Beside the temple rises Krodha Giri, the hill on which the sage meditated.
The cave. Near its top is Krodha Guhe, the cave of his penance.
The hilltop shrine. On the summit stand images of Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa with the goddesses Gaurī and Lakṣmī: Śiva’s consort and Viṣṇu’s, side by side, as the gods below are one.

ZAPPY: Two goddesses for one god?

PROF. BOLT: One for each half! Gaurī for Śaṅkara, Lakṣmī for Nārāyaṇa. The family photo is complete.
