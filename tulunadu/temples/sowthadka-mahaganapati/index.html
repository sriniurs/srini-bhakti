ŚRĪ MAHĀGAṆAPATI · SOWTHAḌKA · A REFERENCE GUIDE

SOWTHAḌKA

The Gaṇapati who chose the open sky

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA · GAṆEŚA

FOREWORD

Every temple is a house for a god. At Sowthaḍka, in the hills of Beḷtaṅgaḍi, the god declined the house.

Mahāgaṇapati sits in an open meadow under a great tree, with no sanctum, no walls and no roof, in sun and monsoon alike, open to anyone at any hour. Around him hang thousands of bells, each the mark of a wish that someone believes he heard.

This guide tells how he came here, why he stays outside, and the offerings that make the place famous.

Om Gaṃ Gaṇapataye namaḥ,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A god who doesn’t want a temple?

PROF. BOLT: He wants no door between him and his devotees. No wall means no one is ever kept out, at any time.

ZAPPY: Is it really open all the time?

PROF. BOLT: The shrine has no doors to close. Devotees describe it as open round the clock; formal worship is held at set times in the day.

WHERE IT IS

In the foothills of the Ghāṭs

The village. Sowthaḍka lies about 3 km from Kokkaḍa, in Beḷtaṅgaḍi tālūk, in green, hilly country at the foot of the Western Ghāṭs.

Neighbours. It sits between two great shrines: about 16 km from Dharmasthaḷa and about 35 km from Kukke Subrahmaṇya. Maṅgaḷūru is about 75 km away.

The setting. Meadow, forest and plantation all round, which is the whole point: the shrine is the landscape.

THE NAME

A cucumber meadow

🥒 Sowte

Tuḷu (and Kannaḍa savate): the cucumber. The place was a field where cucumbers grew in plenty.

🌾 Aḍka

A meadow, an open grassy upland. Many place names in the region end in -aḍka.

✍️ Spellings

Sowthaḍka, Sauthaḍka, Southaḍka, Southeḍka: all the same place.

🙏 Kṣētra

Formally Śrī Mahāgaṇapati Kṣētra, Sowthaḍka, and counted among the siddhi kṣētras, places where prayers bear fruit.

ZAPPY: Named after a vegetable?

PROF. BOLT: After the field where the god first rested, and the first crop offered to him. The cucumber is part of the story.

THE COWHERDS’ RESCUE

The sthala purāṇa
A royal temple. Long ago, the story says, a Gaṇapati temple of a local royal family stood in the region.
Destroyed. Enemies attacked and wrecked the temple, but the image survived.
Carried off. To keep it from the enemy, local cowherd boys took up the image and carried it with them, worshipping it wherever they stopped.
The meadow. They set it down at last in a meadow thick with cucumbers, and the farmers there offered him their cucumbers every day.
Plenty. The fields gave great harvests, and the grateful farmers resolved to build their Gaṇapati a proper temple.

UNDER THE OPEN SKY

The wish of the god

The dream. When the farmers planned the temple, Gaṇapati is said to have appeared to them in a dream and told them not to build it.

The reason. He wished to stay open to everyone, so that no wall, door, hour or rule would stand between a devotee and him. Their god’s wish was the farmers’ command, and he has sat in the open ever since.

What that means. Rain, sun, wind and the seasons all reach him. There is no inner room where only the priest may go: everyone sees him the same way, from the same open ground.

ZAPPY: So the story is the rule?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. The god asked to stay outside, so the image stays uncovered. The arch and bell poles around him are as close as any structure comes.

Not alone. Other gods of the coast also stay under the sky, notably many nāga shrines and daiva stones. A great Gaṇapati in the open is what makes Sowthaḍka unusual.

THE BELLS

Every bell a wish
The vow. A devotee with a special wish (a child, a job, a cure, an exam, a wedding) makes a vow, a harake, to Mahāgaṇapati.
The bell. When the wish is granted, or after the rites, he or she brings a brass bell and ties it up near the god with a red thread.
The belief. Tying the bell is believed to secure the wish; so some tie it as the vow is made, and others as thanks.
The result. Bells of every size hang in their thousands, ringing at every breeze and every hand, the sound of the shrine.

WHAT HE IS OFFERED

Food a cowherd would understand
Avalakki pañcakajjāyaThe signature sēve: beaten rice with jaggery, coconut, sesame, honey and banana
AppaSweet fried rice-and-jaggery cakes, Sowthaḍka’s famous prasāda, made daily
LaḍḍuA sweet ball, given as prasāda
HannukāyiFruit and coconut, offered at every worship
NaivēdyaCooked rice at the midday worship
CucumberThe first offering of the farmers, remembered in the place’s name

For everyone. The temple also serves a meal to devotees as prasāda.

THE MŪḌAPPA SĒVE

A god buried in sweets
What it is. In the Mūḍappa sēve, the image is covered entirely with appa, heaped up into a mound, with sugarcane around it.
The name. Mūḍu is to cover or heap up, appa the sweet cake: the god “heaped with appa”.
When. It is the high point of the year, held around late January or early February, with Gaṇapati hōma and a festival crowd.
After. The appa is given out as prasāda to the thousands who come.

COWS AND MONKEYS

The other devotees

🐄 Cows

A custom has grown of sharing the avalakki pañcakajjāya prasāda with the cows that roam the grounds, fitting for a god carried here by cowherds.

🏡 Gōśāla

The temple keeps a cow shelter as part of its complex.

🐒 Monkeys

Monkeys live in the trees around the shrine and come down for the offerings. Visitors are warned to mind their prasāda.

🌳 The tree

The great tree that shades the god is the shrine’s only roof and the monkeys’ home.

ZAPPY: So the cows get a share of the god’s food?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and no one minds. A god who came with cowherds is happy to share with the herd.

A DAY AT SOWTHAḌKA

The order of worship
Morning Abhiṣēka, the offering of fruit and coconut and of avalakki pañcakajjāya, then worship.
Midday An offering of cooked rice, fruit, coconut and betel, then the great worship of noon, the mahāpūje.
Evening Another offering, fruit and coconut, and the evening mahāpūje.
Between Devotees come and go to the open shrine, ringing bells, tying bells, and offering sēves.
January A yearly mahāpūje, followed by the Mūḍappa sēve.
Caturthī Gaṇeśa Caturthī draws large crowds, as at every Gaṇapati shrine of the coast.
