UḌUPI ŚRĪKṚṢṆA

The child with the churning rod

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

A storm, a ship from Dvārakā, and a lump of sacred clay used as ballast. Inside the clay, a small boy with a churning rod in his hand.

That boy is the Kṛṣṇa of Uḍupi. He is not served by temple priests but by monks, eight lineages of them, who hand him from one to the next in a ceremony the whole town stays awake for.

This guide tells how he came, who brought him, the poet who saw him through a crack in the wall, and the life of the town that grew around him.

Śrī Kṛṣṇārpaṇamastu,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A god who arrives as cargo?

PROF. BOLT: As ballast, even! The lowest thing on the ship. In this story the most precious treasure is hidden in the most ordinary clay.

ZAPPY: Why “maṭha” and not “temple”?

PROF. BOLT: Because Madhva did not build a public temple run by priests. He set up a home for Kṛṣṇa run by monks. The eight maṭhas are the family, and Kṛṣṇa is the head of the house.

WHERE IT IS

A temple town near the sea

The town. Uḍupi stands on the coastal plain a few kilometres inland from the sea. The town grew outward from the temple square, and its old streets still run round it.

The name. Tradition reads “Uḍupi” as Uḍupa, “lord of the stars”, the Moon, who is said to have done penance here and been freed of a curse by Śiva. The Śiva he worshipped is honoured as Candramaulīśvara, “the Lord who wears the Moon”.

The coast. The port of Malpe, on the sea west of the town, is where the story of the ship is set.

THE CHILD WITH THE ROD

Bālakṛṣṇa of Uḍupi
The image. A small standing figure of Kṛṣṇa as a boy, carved, by tradition, in śālagrāma stone. In his right hand he holds a churning rod; in his left, the rope that turns it.
Why a churning rod. The boy has just left his mother Yaśodā’s butter-churning, the scene of the stolen-butter stories of Gokula. He is caught between play and mischief.
Facing west. The image looks west, toward the sea, unlike most Hindu images, which face east. Devotees see him through a silver screen of nine openings, the Navagraha Kiṇḍi, set in front of the sanctum.
Monks, not priests. The worship of this Kṛṣṇa is done by sannyāsis, the svāmis of the eight maṭhas, not by householder priests. Each day the presiding svāmi dresses the image anew in an alaṅkāra, a festive costume, often of another god or another story.

THE SHIP FROM DVĀRAKĀ

How the Lord came to Uḍupi
Rukmiṇī’s Kṛṣṇa. Tradition says Rukmiṇī, Kṛṣṇa’s queen, longed to see him as the child Yaśodā had known. An image of the boy with his churning rod was made for her at Dvārakā, and she worshipped it there. In time it lay hidden, buried in the pale sacred clay called gopīcandana.
The storm. Centuries later a ship carrying that clay as ballast was caught in a storm off the coast near Malpe. Madhvācārya, on the shore, waved his upper cloth and guided it safely in.
The gift. The grateful captain offered him anything aboard. He asked only for a large lump of the clay. When it was broken open, the image of Bālakṛṣṇa was inside.
The walk. Madhva carried the image to Uḍupi, singing as he went, bathed it in the tank now called Madhva Sarovara, and installed it on Makara Saṅkrānti. The songs of that walk are counted among his twelve hymns, the Dvādaśa Stotra.

ZAPPY: Why did he want the clay and not gold?

PROF. BOLT: Because he already knew what was inside. That's how devotees tell it: the saint wasn't bargaining, he was collecting his Lord.

MADHVĀCĀRYA

The teacher who brought him

📛 Three names

Born Vāsudeva into a Tuḷu-speaking Brahmin family. As a monk he was Pūrṇaprajña, “of complete wisdom”, and Ānandatīrtha; the world knows him as Madhva.

🧘 The monk

He took sannyāsa in his teens under the teacher Acyutaprekṣa of the Ekadaṇḍi order, and soon outgrew his teacher’s Advaita.

⚖️ The teaching

Tattvavāda, “the doctrine of reality”, later called Dvaita: God, souls and matter are eternally distinct; Viṣṇu is supreme and independent, and souls depend on him for everything, including release.

📚 The works

Thirty-seven works, the Sarvamūla Granthas: commentaries on the Brahma Sūtras, Upaniṣads and Gītā, the Mahābhārata Tātparya Nirṇaya, the ritual manual Tantrasāra Saṅgraha and the Dvādaśa Stotra.

Vāyu on earth. His followers hold Madhva to be the third descent of Vāyu, the wind god, after Hanumān and Bhīma. That is why Hanumān, honoured as Mukhyaprāṇa, “the chief life-breath”, stands so close to Kṛṣṇa in every Mādhva shrine.

The vanishing. Tradition says that in his last year, while teaching the Aitareya Upaniṣad to his disciples, he disappeared under a shower of flowers, to live on unseen with Vyāsa at Badarī in the Himālaya.

ZAPPY: He just vanished in the middle of class?

PROF. BOLT: So his followers believe. There's no tomb for Madhva anywhere. The Mādhvas say their teacher never died; he went home to his own guru.

KANAKANA KIṆḌI

The window of the shepherd-poet
The poet. Kanaka Dāsa (1509–1609), born Timmappa Nāyaka into the Kuruba shepherd community, a soldier turned singer of Kṛṣṇa, and a disciple of the great Mādhva teacher Vyāsatīrtha. His songs carry the signature of his home god, Ādikeśava of Kāginele.
Kept outside. When he came to Uḍupi, the story goes, he was not allowed into the shrine because of his caste. He stayed outside the western wall, singing to the Lord he could not see.
The turning. The wall cracked, and the image of Kṛṣṇa turned from east to west to face him through the gap.
The window. The head of the maṭha, Vādirāja Tīrtha, did not plaster the crack. He had it widened into a window. Devotees still look through it, and it bears the poet’s name: Kanakana Kiṇḍi, “Kanaka’s window”.

Two tellings. Madhva’s own ritual manual, the Tantrasāra Saṅgraha, already describes the image as facing west, and the eight maṭhas’ images face west too. Mādhva tradition reads this as design; the popular story reads it as Kanaka’s miracle. This guide gives both.

HISTORY

Eight centuries of the maṭha
1238 Birth of Madhva at Pājaka (some scholars date it to 1199).
13th c. Madhva installs Bālakṛṣṇa at Uḍupi; one widely cited date is 1285. He gives the image to his eight disciple-monks to worship in turn.
1317 Madhva vanishes, by tradition, while teaching (or 1278 on the earlier dating).
1509 Birth of Kanaka Dāsa; his darśan through the western wall comes in Vādirāja’s time.
1522 or 1532 Vādirāja Tīrtha of Sōde Maṭha lengthens each maṭha’s turn from two months to two years; sources differ on the year of the first two-year Paryāya.
16th c. Vādirāja builds up the temple’s worship and festivals and installs an image of Madhva at Pājaka.
18 Jan 2026 Vedavardhana Tīrtha of Śirūru Maṭha, aged 20, takes up his first Paryāya; the next turn falls to Sōde Maṭha in 2028.

The rotation’s length. Under Madhva each monk held the worship for two months, so the full circle of eight came round every sixteen months. Vādirāja’s two-year turn gave each svāmi time both to serve Kṛṣṇa and, in the fourteen years between turns, to travel and teach. A full circle now takes sixteen years.

ZAPPY: So a svāmi gets the Lord only once in sixteen years?

PROF. BOLT: Once in sixteen, for two years. Some svāmis manage four or five Paryāyas in a long life. For them it's the high point of everything.

Brothers in pairs. The eight are joined two by two as dvandva maṭhas: Palimāru–Adamāru, Kṛṣṇāpura–Puttige, Śirūru–Sōde, Kāṇiyūru–Pejāvara. Partners stand in for each other, and a svāmi without a successor may be followed by one chosen from the partner maṭha.

Named for villages. Each maṭha takes its name from its home village in the region, but each also keeps a house on the Uḍupi temple square.

Disciples of Madhva. All eight first svāmis were monks Madhva himself initiated. The lines have continued unbroken since; the present svāmis are in the twenties and thirties of their lineages.

PARYĀYA

The night the Lord changes hands
Years of getting ready. The incoming maṭha begins long before with a chain of muhūrtas: the Bāḷe (planting plantains), the Akki (storing rice) and the Kaṭṭige (stacking firewood), so that the kitchen can feed every visitor for two years. The incoming svāmi goes on pilgrimage beforehand, for once in office he may not leave Uḍupi.
Before dawn, 18 January. The incoming svāmi bathes at Daṇḍatīrtha near Kāpu, then rides in procession through the night-lit town.
The two Śivas first. He worships at Anantēśvara and Candramaulīśvara before entering the maṭha, and takes darśan of Kṛṣṇa through the window.
The handover. The outgoing svāmi receives him and gives him the Akṣaya Pātra, the vessel handed down from Madhva, and the sattuga, the serving ladle. He then takes his seat on the Sarvajña Pīṭha, “the throne of the all-knowing”, Madhva’s own.

ZAPPY: A ladle as a symbol of power?

PROF. BOLT: Of service! The Paryāya svāmi's first duty is to feed people. Whoever holds the ladle holds the kitchen, and the kitchen is half of Uḍupi's worship.

THE TEMPLE SQUARE

Car Street and the maṭha

🛤️ Ratha Bīdi

The square street around the maṭha, wide enough for the great chariots. Every procession of the year circles it, always clockwise.

💧 Madhva Sarovara

The temple tank beside the shrine, where Madhva first bathed the image. Its sacred water is held to be the Gaṅgā herself, honoured here as Bhāgīrathī.

ZAPPY: Why may only the Paryāya svāmi sit on Madhva’s seat?

PROF. BOLT: Because for those two years he stands in for Madhva himself. The day he hands over the ladle, he is one of eight again.

Three chariots. On Saṅkrānti night the festival images are carried in the golden palanquin to Madhva Sarovara and floated three times round it on a decorated raft. Then Kṛṣṇa rides the great Brahmaratha, Mukhyaprāṇa the Garuḍa Ratha, and Anantēśvara with Candramaulīśvara the Mahāpūjā Ratha. On the earlier nights only the two smaller chariots run.

Viṭla Piṇḍi. A clay image of Kṛṣṇa rides the golden chariot round the square while men dressed as cowherds smash pots of milk, curd and coloured water hung from tall poles. The clay image is then immersed in the tank.

The season. Chariot processions run nightly from Utthāna Dvādaśī in autumn until the Bhāgīrathī festival at the start of the monsoon.

AROUND UḌUPI

Places of the Madhva story
AnantēśvaraThe ancient Śiva temple on the square, over a thousand years old. Madhva’s parents prayed here for a son, and tradition places his vanishing here.
CandramaulīśvaraŚiva of the Moon, facing the Kṛṣṇa Maṭha across the square
PājakaMadhva’s birthplace south-east of Uḍupi, with an image of him installed by Vādirāja
MalpeThe port where the ship came ashore; nearby, the Vaḍabhāṇḍeśvara shrine, where Madhva installed an image of Balarāma
Daṇḍatīrtha, KāpuWhere Madhva studied under Acyutaprekṣa, and where each Paryāya svāmi bathes

Hari and Hara. The Kṛṣṇa Maṭha is the youngest of the three shrines on its square. Kṛṣṇa’s own year begins with his svāmi bowing to the two older Śivas, and they ride in his chariot festival.

On the Muktisthala circuit. The other sacred places of Paraśurāma Kṣetra, Kollūr, Kumbhāśi, Kōṭeśvara and Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa, lie north of Uḍupi; Kukke Subrahmaṇya lies inland to the south-east. See the series introduction.
