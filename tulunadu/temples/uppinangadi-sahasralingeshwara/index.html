UPPINAṄGAḌI

Where two rivers meet and a thousand liṅgas sleep

TEMPLES OF DAKṢIṆA KANNAḌA

FOREWORD

The Netrāvatī comes down from the Ghāṭs past Dharmasthaḷa. The Kumāradhārā comes down past Kukke. At Uppinaṅgaḍi they meet, and on the bank between them stands Śiva as Sahasraliṅgeśvara, Lord of a Thousand Liṅgas.

The thousand are said to lie in the river itself, small stone liṅgas in the sand and rock, and the original liṅga of the temple is buried in the bank and uncovered only for the festival. In a great monsoon the rivers rise and meet at the very door of the shrine.

No wonder the town is called the Kāśī of the South.

Om Namaḥ Śivāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: A temple whose main liṅga is buried?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the riddle of Uppinaṅgaḍi. The river keeps covering it with sand, and once a year the people dig it out to worship it.

ZAPPY: Sahasra means a thousand?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, but think of it as “countless”. The name is about the many liṅgas in the river, not an exact count.

WHERE IT IS

Between two rivers

The town. Uppinaṅgaḍi, also called Ubar, in Puttūr tālūk, on the highway from Maṅgaḷūru to Bengaḷūru. The Kumāradhārā wraps one side of it and the Netrāvatī the other.

The temple. On the bank where the two rivers join; the confluence lies to its right as you face it.

Holy waters upstream. Each river has just passed a great shrine: Dharmasthaḷa on the Netrāvatī and Kukke Subrahmaṇya on the Kumāradhārā.

THE NAMES

Salt, a market, and two holy cities

🧂 Uppina-aṅgaḍi

Read as uppu, salt, and aṅgaḍi, a market or shop: a river port where goods, salt among them, changed from boat to cart.

🏷️ Ubar

The town’s other name, used alongside Uppinaṅgaḍi.

🕉️ Dakṣiṇa Kāśī

“The Kāśī of the South”, the title the temple’s own history gives the town.

👣 Gayāpada Kṣētra

“The place of Gayā’s footprint”, after the great pilgrimage town of Bihar.

ZAPPY: A salt market and a holy city at once?

PROF. BOLT: The river did both. Boats brought cargo up from Maṅgaḷūru to unload here for the Ghāṭs, and the same meeting of waters made it sacred.

THE TWO RIVERS

And a third, unseen
NetrāvatīRises at Eḷanīr near Samse in the Ghāṭs. Said to be born from the tears of Varāha, Viṣṇu as the boar.
KumāradhārāRises on Kumāraparvata in the Puṣpagiri hills of Koḍagu, about 1,600 m up, and passes Kukke Subrahmaṇya.
GuptagāminīA third, hidden river believed to join them here, as the unseen Sarasvatī joins at Prayāga.
AfterThe joined river, as the Netrāvatī, flows on west to Maṅgaḷūru and the sea.

A sangama kṣētra. Confluences are sacred across India. Local writers count Uppinaṅgaḍi as Karnataka’s second great river-meeting shrine, after Kūḍala Saṅgama.

ZAPPY: Like Prayāga, then?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the comparison people make: two visible rivers and one invisible. Prayāga has the Gaṅgā, Yamunā and Sarasvatī; here, the Netrāvatī, Kumāradhārā and Guptagāminī.

BHĪMA AND THE PURUṢAMṚGA

How the liṅgas came to be
The errand. For Yudhiṣṭhira’s Rājasūya sacrifice, Bhīma was sent south, the local telling says to the country around Uppinaṅgaḍi, to bring the Puruṣamṛga, a being with a man’s head and a lion’s body and a great devotee of Śiva.
The chase. The creature agreed to come only if Bhīma could stay ahead of it; if caught, he would be its prey. Bhīma ran.
The liṅgas. Whenever it drew close, Bhīma dropped something behind him, in this telling a hair from his head, and each became a liṅga.
The escape. The Puruṣamṛga, unable to pass a liṅga without worshipping it, stopped at each one, and Bhīma kept his lead. The liṅgas he left became the thousand of the river.

THE THOUSAND LIṄGAS

In the sand and the rock

🪨 In the river

Liṅgas are said to lie all along the banks and bed near the temple, showing when the water falls and the sand shifts.

🚶 Vādirāja

The 16th-century saint Vādirāja is said to have crossed here on his knees, so as not to step on a single liṅga.

📖 In print

A 1910 Kannaḍa book on the shrine, Śrī Gayāpada Kṣētra Māhātmyavu by Purōhita Pālttila Ananta Bhaṭ, records thousands of liṅgas in the river.

⏳ Hidden again

Each monsoon lays down new sand. In many years most of the liṅgas stay covered.

ZAPPY: Can you see them?

PROF. BOLT: Some years, when the river is low and the sand has moved, people find them. Other years the river keeps them. That uncertainty is part of the place.

THE BURIED LIṄGA

The temple’s first Śiva
Where it lies. The temple’s original liṅga is buried in the sand on the Netrāvatī bank, to the left of the present temple.
Once a year. For the annual festival the sand is dug away and a temporary structure is put up over the liṅga, so that Sahasraliṅgeśvara can be worshipped where he first was.
A losing battle. The rains deposit so much sand each season that clearing it fully every year is very hard.
The daily temple. The rest of the year, worship goes on in the temple beside it.

THE SAṄGAMA

When the rivers come to the door
Dry season The two rivers join a little away from the temple, across a wide bank of sand.
Monsoon As they swell, the rivers spread across the bank toward each other.
The meeting In a heavy monsoon they meet directly in front of the sanctum. People from the surrounding villages crowd the banks to see it.
The bath A bath at that moment, the saṅgama snāna, followed by worship of Śiva, is held especially purifying, a step toward liberation.
Not every year Some years the water never rises that high. One saṅgama was reported as the first in five years.
Too high In great floods the water enters the temple itself.

ZAPPY: So people wait for a flood?

PROF. BOLT: They wait for one moment in it: when the two rivers touch at the temple door. It’s the great event of the monsoon here.

KĀŚĪ OF THE SOUTH

Why the comparison holds

ŚivaViśvanāthaSahasraliṅgeśvara
GoddessMahākāḷīMahākāḷī
GuardianKālabhairavaKālabhairava
RiversGaṅgā, with the Gaṅgā–Yamunā meeting at nearby PrayāgaNetrāvatī and Kumāradhārā meeting at the temple
For the deadRites of liberationGayāpada: rites for ancestors

A place of release. The temple describes itself as a mōkṣa dhāma, a place where rites done here help the dead to liberation. Families come to perform rites for ancestors at the river.

MAHĀKĀḶĪ

The fierce Mother on the bank
Her shrine. Mahākāḷī has her own shrine on the Netrāvatī bank, in her fierce form as slayer of Raktabīja.
Her image. She holds a trident, a sword, a ḍamaru and a bowl. The present image was carved in 1953 by the sculptor Rañjāḷa Gōpāla Śeṇai, who took no payment; her new shrine had been built in 1934.
Her worship. Kuṅkumārcane, worship with vermilion, and raṅga pūje are the favoured offerings.
In hard times. People turn to her especially in times of epidemic.
Mecci nēma. A yearly rite with a pātri, a medium, is held for her on the second day of the Makhe jātre.

THE MAKHE JĀTRE

Three festivals in Māgha
FirstOn the full moon of Māgha
SecondOn Kṛṣṇa aṣṭamī of Māgha
ThirdOn Śivarātri
LengthAbout 15 days if it starts at the full moon; up to 22 if Śivarātri comes early
DanceThe Madimmāya–Madimmāl, “bridegroom and bride”, or Kanyāpu dance, is integral to the festival

ZAPPY: “Makhe” is Māgha?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, the Tuḷu form of the month’s name. Makhe jātre is simply “the Māgha fair”.

The daiva too. A shrine of the daiva Kalkuḍa, described as ancestral, belongs to the temple’s world.

THE TOWN AND THE FLOODS

Port, court and high water
Tradition The shrine is held to be very ancient, by some reckonings 2,500 years.
13th c. The present temple structure is thought to date from about this time.
River port Boats carried goods upriver from Maṅgaḷūru to unload at Uppinaṅgaḍi; bullock carts took them on over the Ghāṭs to Sakaleśapura and Hāsana.
1923 A great flood washes out much of the town; its courts are moved to Puttūr.
1974 Another severe flood.
Today A busy highway town of temples, mosques and a church on the two rivers.
