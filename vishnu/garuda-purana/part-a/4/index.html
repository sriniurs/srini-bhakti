GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART A · 4 OF 8 · RATNA PARĪKṢĀ

THE TESTING OF GEMS

A demon’s body, a jeweller’s eye, and a jeweller’s manual inside a Purāṇa

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

In the middle of a holy book sits a jeweller’s manual. Thirteen chapters tell where each gem comes from, how to tell a real one from glass, what flaws ruin it, and what it should cost.

It opens with a myth: the asura Bala, tricked into his own sacrifice, his body scattered across the world as the seeds of every gem. Then it turns strictly practical: weights in grains of rice, tests by scratching, heating and soaking.

The section is called the Ratna Parīkṣā, the examination of gems, and is ascribed to the sage Agastya.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Why would a Purāṇa care about gem prices?

PROF. BOLT: Because kings and merchants wore and traded gems, and a gem was believed to work on its wearer. Getting a fake was a spiritual as well as a financial loss.

BALA’S SACRIFICE

How the gems were born
A conqueror. The asura Bala (Vala) defeats Indra and rules the universe.
The jest. At a sacrifice the gods ask him, half in fun, to play the sacrificial animal. He agrees and lets himself be bound to the post.
The betrayal. The gods make the jest real and kill him.
The seeds. His parts become the seeds of gems. Gods, yakṣas, siddhas and nāgas gather them; what falls to earth forms mines in seas, rivers, hills and forests.

ZAPPY: A gift from a murdered asura?

PROF. BOLT: The myth has that edge. Bala offered himself honestly and was cheated; his body still blesses the world. Beauty from betrayal.

DIAMOND: WHERE AND WHAT COLOUR

Saurāṣṭraa copper glow

Kōsalayellow
Puṇḍrablue

Gods of the colours. Green diamonds belong to Sūrya, white to Varuṇa, yellow to Indra, brown to Agni, blue to the lord of the ancestors, copper to the Maruts. Good crystals are six-, eight- or twelve-sided, with sharp points.

DIAMOND: WHO MAY WEAR IT

Rules of the old social order

⚪ By varṇa

Brahmin: conch-white. Kṣatriya: hare’s-eye brownish yellow. Vaiśya: plantain-leaf green. Śūdra: the shine of a washed sword.

👑 Kings

Yellow and coral-red stones are for kings only; a king may wear any colour if the stone is flawless.

🚫 Women

The text forbids diamonds as women’s jewellery, claiming they bring childlessness.

🌊 Alone

A diamond that floats on water should be worn by itself, with no other gem.

ZAPPY: Women can’t wear diamonds?

PROF. BOLT: That is the rule as written, from its time. Nobody follows it now; a modern reader should take it as history, not advice.

DIAMOND: TESTS AND PRICE

Hardness is the proof

🔪 Scratch

A true diamond scratches every metal and gem and is scratched by none; only a diamond cuts a diamond.

⚙️ Wheel and lye

Further tests on the grinding wheel (śāṇa) and in alkaline solutions.

❌ Flaws

Two colours, chipped points, a cloudy centre, red streaks, scratches, a squat or flat shape: they bring loss and ruin.

⚖️ Price

Weighed in taṇḍula, a grain of rice equal to eight white sesame seeds. Small flaws cut the value to a tenth; many flaws, below a hundredth.

What a good one gives. Long life, family, cattle and harvests; safety from snakes, tigers, thieves, hidden poison, fire and flood.

PEARL: EIGHT SOURCES

Not all from oysters

The best. Oyster pearls rank first and can be drilled end to end. Elephant, bamboo and conch pearls lack lustre. A cobra-hood pearl brings fortune; a cloud-born pearl is rarest of all.

Places. Siṁhala (Lanka), Saurāṣṭra, Tāmraparṇī, Pāraśava (Persia) and others; but the text says place of origin should not change the price, only shape and size.

Value. Set by weight and roundness, in silver coins: a top round oyster pearl is put at about 1,305.

PEARL: CLEANING AND TESTING

Practical chemistry
Soak. In rice water with lime juice, then simmer until soft enough to drill.
Heat. In a covered crucible; then boil in milk, water or wine.
Polish. Rub with linen until it shines.
Test. Leave a doubtful pearl overnight in warm salted oil: a real one survives. Or rub it with vrīhi grain: a real one keeps its colour.

The ideal. White, large, heavy, clear, round, cool and bright, and evenly drilled; such a pearl guards its owner.

RUBY AND RĀVAṆA’S GAṄGĀ

Padmarāga, from Bala’s blood
The Sun takes the blood. Sūrya gathers Bala’s gem-bearing blood and flies off with it.
Rāvaṇa blocks him. Frightened, Sūrya drops it into a pool in Laṅkā.
The river. The pool becomes the Rāvaṇa Gaṅgā, holy as the Gaṅgā, its banks glittering at night with rubies.

🌺 Colours

Like bandhūka, guñjā and kiṁśuka flowers, pomegranate seed, vermilion, lac, a cakōra bird’s eye, the red lotus.

✅ Good

Bright colour, weight, cool to touch, even clarity. Only diamond or kuruvinda scratches it.

Its gift. Protection from enemies, illusion and illnesses of the humours. Never wear it with a stone of a clashing kind: one bad stone cancels the rest.

EMERALD AND GARUḌA

Marakata, from Bala’s bile
Vāsuki’s flight. The serpent king takes Bala’s bile and tears across the sky.
Garuḍa strikes. Vāsuki drops the bile; it falls beyond the Himālaya and turns an inland sea into a bed of emeralds.
A sneeze of gems. Garuḍa picks up some stones, faints, and blows them out through his nostrils: the bird is part of the gem’s story.

Its worth. Green like a parrot’s neck or a peacock’s feather is best. A flawless emerald beats a ruby of the same weight; it is said to counter poison that herbs and mantras cannot.

SAPPHIRE AND THE MILK TEST

Indranīla, from Bala’s eyes

👁️ Origin

Bala’s eyes were cast into Siṁhala; its shores, edged with kētaka, yield the sapphire.

🥛 The test

Drop it into milk a hundred times its weight. If the milk turns its blue, it is a mahānīla, a great sapphire.

🔥 No fire

A sapphire stands more heat than a ruby, but the text forbids fire tests: they bring bad luck.

🪞 Imitators

Glass, marble and crystal copy its colour; weight and hardness expose them.

ZAPPY: The milk turns blue?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the claim: a deep stone tints the whole liquid. It’s a test of saturation, how strong the colour is.

VAIDŪRYA

The stone from Bala’s roar

🔊 Origin

Bala’s war-cry churned the ocean and threw gems onto its shores.

⛰️ Name

From the hill of Vidūra, where the stone was first found.

🦚 Best colour

Like a peacock’s breast, or pale green like a bamboo leaf.

🔎 Tests

A stone that cannot scratch a known vaidūrya is glass; lighter weight exposes imitations.

What is it? Dutt translates vaidūrya as lapis lazuli; most scholars take it as cat’s-eye chrysoberyl or beryl. The text says Brahmā first taught its tests to Vyāsa.

Coral’s price. The text notes that a coral’s price depends on how it is cut. Coral and crystal are counted as gems and must be tested like the rest.

CATCHING FAKES

The tests, gathered
🪨Scratch: a true stone scratches its fakes; only diamond scratches diamond
⚖️Weigh: glass is lighter; of two lookalikes, the heavier is genuine
🖐️Touch: a real ruby feels cool
💡Light: watch how rays leave the stone when it is tilted
🥛Soak: milk for sapphire, warm salt oil for pearl
🎨Colour: dyed or two-toned stones are rejected outright
🤝Compare: set the doubtful stone beside a proven one

ZAPPY: That’s real gemmology.

PROF. BOLT: Much of it, yes: hardness, specific gravity, optics. Jewellers still use the same principles with better tools.

THE PRICE LADDER

How the stones are weighed against each other

Diamond to ruby. A taṇḍula of diamond is matched with a māṣaka of polished ruby.

Ruby to sapphire. One māṣa of ruby equals four of sapphire.

Emerald. A flawless emerald is worth more than a ruby of equal weight, a flawed one less.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Why a jeweller’s manual is scripture

🔍 Test everything

Nothing is taken on trust, not even a seller’s word: compare, weigh, scratch.

💎 Flawless or nothing

A small flaw cuts value tenfold. The text’s moral side is clear: purity is the measure.

🛡️ Gems as protection

Every stone is believed to guard against poison, beasts, fire or enemies.

📜 Knowledge preserved

Trade knowledge of its age survives here because a Purāṇa kept it.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: A gem is judged by its flaws, and so, the Purāṇa hints, are we.

Not verified: the details of chapters 74, 75, 77 and 78 beyond their names; the identifications in the lesser-stones table are common scholarly guesses, not the text’s.
