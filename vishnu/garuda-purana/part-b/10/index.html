GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 10 OF 16

THE CREMATION AND THE BONES

From the house to the pyre to the river

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

Chapter 10 is the most practical chapter in the Sārōddhāra: about a hundred verses on what the son and family do from the moment of death to the day the bones reach the river.

It covers the bathing of the body, the procession and its halts, the pyre, the skull rite, the return home, the lamp, and the collecting of the bones. It adds rules for hard cases: a body never found, a child, a pregnant woman, an ascetic.

Part of the chapter, verses 35 to 55, is missing from the scanned translation used here; that gap is marked where it falls.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is this how every family does a cremation?

PROF. BOLT: The outline, broadly. Details differ by region, community and priest. This is the digest’s version.

THE SON’S DEBT

Verses 1–3

Garuḍa asks. About the rites for burning the bodies of the good, and the greatness of a faithful wife.

The answer. These rites release sons and grandsons from the debt they owe their forebears.

Enough. Many gifts are not needed: a son who performs his parents’ funeral gains merit like the great Agniṣṭōma sacrifice.

ZAPPY: No need for many gifts?

PROF. BOLT: Verse 3 says so plainly. Doing the rites yourself counts more than spending on them.

PREPARING

Verses 4–6
Shaving. The son and relatives shave, to remove sin; nails and armpit hair are spared.
Bathing. They bathe together and put on clean cloth.
A true son. One who will not shave for a dead parent is not counted a true son.

THE BODY

Verses 7–10

💧 Bathed

With river water.

🌸 Adorned

With sandal paste, garlands or Gaṅgā clay; covered with new cloth.

🍚 First piṇḍa

The son, sacred thread over the right shoulder, says the family name and offers a piṇḍa at the place of death, pleasing the earth.

🚪 At the door

An offering at the threshold in the departed’s name, guarding against harmful spirits.

THE PROCESSION

Verses 11–15
Farewell. The daughter-in-law and others walk around the body and honour it.
Carried. The son carries it on his shoulder with relatives; each step earns the merit of a horse sacrifice.
A debt repaid. Carrying a father on shoulder, back or hip repays the debt of a parent’s care.
Halfway. The bearers stop, clean the ground, bathe the body and make an offering.
Guarded. Offerings keep goblins and demons from disturbing the body.

ZAPPY: Every step a horse sacrifice?

PROF. BOLT: The text wants sons to carry the body themselves, not hand it over. That’s the merit.

From the house to a shared road. The procession moves a private loss into a space that others can see and take part in. Offerings made at different points give the movement a sequence. Each stop marks a further separation from the house and from the ordinary returns that a journey would otherwise promise.

AT THE BURNING GROUND

Verses 16–19

🧭 Head north

The body is laid with its head to the north.

🧹 Prepared

The ground swept and smeared with cow dung; an altar made and sprinkled.

🔥 The fire

The sacred fire set down and worshipped with flowers and coloured rice.

🙏 A prayer

A prayer is offered to Agni before the burning.

THE PYRE

Verses 20–24

🪵 The wood

Sandalwood, sacred woods, palāśa and aśvattha.

🍚 On the pyre

Two piṇḍas offered as the body is laid on it.

🔀 A new state

From the moment of the pyre, the departed is called a prēta, “the one gone forth”.

✋ Five

In all, five piṇḍas protect the dead; without them, disturbing forces interfere.

Why the pyre is a turning point. Many actions beforehand prepare or accompany the body. At the pyre the mourners encounter an irreversible change. Much of what follows concerns returning home, tending a light and recovering the bones, so the chapter uses the pyre to join two different kinds of care.

PAÑCAKA

Verses 25–33

The period. The five lunar mansions from the middle of Dhaniṣṭhā to Rēvatī are called Pañcaka.

The fear. A death or a burning in this period is said to bring harm, even another death in the family.

The remedy. Figures of darbha grass are placed beside the body, consecrated with mantras and gold, and burned with it; gifts of sesame, gold, silver and ghee follow. Then no harm comes.

Still observed. Many families still ask a priest whether a death fell in Pañcaka, and perform the grass-figure remedy if so.

A GAP IN THE TEXT

Verses 34–55

Missing. These verses are absent from the scanned edition of the 1911 translation used for this guide.

Likely subject. Garuḍa’s opening question (v. 1) also asked about “the greatness of a faithful wife”, and that topic does not appear in the surviving verses; it was probably treated here.

What we don’t do. We do not fill the gap from memory or other texts.

ZAPPY: Why not just guess?

PROF. BOLT: Because guessing would put words in the text’s mouth. A clearly marked gap is more honest.

THE SKULL RITE

Verses 56–59
When. When the body is half or fully burned.
How. The skull is opened: with a piece of wood for a householder, with a coconut for an ascetic.
The offering. The son pours ghee at the crown with a mantra.
Then. He weeps aloud.

Its name. The act is widely known as kapāla-kriyā. It is linked with the release of the life-breath through the crown (chapter 9).

THE WALK HOME

Verses 60–62
Bathe Women first, then sons; water with sesame offered in the family name.
Bitter Mourners chew neem leaves and speak of the virtues of the dead.
Walk Home together, women in front, men behind.
At home Bathe again, feed a cow, eat from a leaf plate, not from food already in the house.

ZAPPY: Neem leaves?

PROF. BOLT: Bitter, purifying, and a small marker that this is not an ordinary day. Many families still do it.

The first return. The walk home brings the mourners back to familiar rooms without the person they accompanied out of them. The chapter makes this return part of the rites. Its concern with a lamp and other acts at home shows that care for the departed continues after the procession has ended.

THE LAMP AND THE POT

Verses 63–66

🪔 The lamp

The place of death is cleaned with cow dung; a lamp facing south burns there till the twelfth day.

🥛 The pot

For three evenings, milk and water in an unbaked clay pot at a crossroads or the burning ground.

🪵 Three sticks

The pot is held on three sticks bound together.

📿 The words

A mantra beginning “Thou hast been burned” is recited.

COLLECTING THE BONES

Verses 67–72
When. The fourth day; or the second or third if nothing prevents it.
Prepared. The son bathes, wears wool and a kuśa ring, feeds the spirits of the ground, and circles the pyre three times reciting a mantra to Yama.
Gathered. Milk, then water, on the pyre; the bones gathered onto palāśa leaves and placed in an earthen pot.
Offered. A śrāddha; three piṇḍas facing south on a triangular plot; the ashes gathered; an open jar of water on a three-legged stand.

Why the chapter returns to the site. Collecting the bones requires a second visit after the fire. The text gives that return a purpose and a sequence rather than leaving it as a bare instruction. In the story of the rites, the remains become something that can be carried and placed elsewhere with care.

THE JAR AND THE RIVER

Verses 73–77

Food first. Rice, curd, ghee, water and sweets offered.

Buried. Fifteen steps north, the jar of bones is placed in a hole, with a piṇḍa to ease the pain of burning.

To the river. Later the bones are washed with water and milk, honoured with sandal and saffron, put in a leaf box, touched to heart and head, and dropped by the son into the middle of the Gaṅgā.

WHY THE GAṄGĀ

Verses 78–83

⬇️ If they sink

Bones that sink within ten days: a lasting stay in Brahmā’s world.

⬆️ If they float

Bones that float: thousands of years in heaven.

🌬️ Even the wind

A breeze off the Gaṅgā’s waves that touches the dead removes their sin.

🏔️ Bhagīratha

He brought the Gaṅgā down to lift his ancestors, the sons of Sagara, burned to ash; they rose to heaven.

ZAPPY: Asthi visarjan at Haridvār or Prayāga.

PROF. BOLT: This chapter is why. Families travel far to put the bones in the Gaṅgā.

A river changes the destination. When the chapter follows the bones to the Gaṅgā, the geography expands beyond house and cremation ground. The river connects the obligations of one family to a sacred place shared by many families. That movement helps explain why the chapter gives the destination as much attention as the journey there.

THE HUNTER AND THE CROW

Verses 84–86
A hunter. He killed many creatures; a lion killed him, and he went to hell.
A crow. By chance a crow carried one of his bones and dropped it in the Gaṅgā.
Lifted. He rose in a divine chariot to the world of the gods.
The duty. So a good son should drop the bones in himself, then begin the ten-day rites.

WHEN NO BODY IS FOUND

Verses 87–89

Lost. If someone dies in a wilderness, a forest or at the hands of robbers, and the body is never found.

An effigy. A figure of darbha grass is made and burned; its ashes go to the Gaṅgā.

Dated. The ten-day rites begin from that day, and it is kept as the date for the annual śrāddha.

A mother. If a pregnant woman dies, the child is first removed and laid on the earth; only the mother is cremated.

ZAPPY: For the missing too.

PROF. BOLT: No one is left without rites, even when there’s nothing to burn.

When the usual sequence is interrupted. The discussion of the absent body acknowledges a case in which the mourners cannot simply repeat the earlier sequence. Including that case makes the chapter’s concern with continuity clearer. It asks how rites can still express an obligation when a physical condition of the usual rite is missing.

CHILDREN

Verses 90–98
UnbornNo rites
InfantBuried up to 27 months; milk offered
On the Gaṅgā’s bankGiven to the river
Older childCremated, bones to the Gaṅgā; a water-pot given, children fed
Over fiveTen piṇḍas with milk-rice and sugar
YouthRites on days 11 and 12, without the bull or great gift

Simpler for the young. Those little attached to action and pleasure need only scant rites (v. 97).

ASCETICS

Verses 99–106

🚫 No cremation

Sannyāsins receive no cremation, water rites or ten-day rites from sons.

🦯 The staff

Holding the triple staff is itself enough; they never become prētas.

🪦 Burial

Haṁsa, Paramahaṁsa, Kuṭīcaka and Bahūdaka monks are buried, or given to a great river.

📅 Still remembered

An annual śrāddha is done for them at holy waters and at Gayā.

ZAPPY: Why bury monks?

PROF. BOLT: They already performed their own funeral when they renounced. That’s the traditional reasoning: the fire was taken at sannyāsa.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Chapter 10 in four lessons

🤲 Do it yourself

Carry, light, collect and immerse with your own hands.

🧾 A debt repaid

The funeral repays a parent’s care.

🌊 The river saves

Even a crow’s accident lifted a sinner.

👶 Every case

The lost, the young and the monk each have a rite.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: Carry them yourself, give them to the river, and leave no one, not even the lost, without rites.
