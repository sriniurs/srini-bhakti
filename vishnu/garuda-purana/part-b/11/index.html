GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 11 OF 16

THE TEN DAYS

Grief, duty, and a piṇḍa each morning by the water

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

The bones have gone to the river. Now come the ten days of daily offerings, the piṇḍas that build the departed a body for the road (chapter 1).

Chapter 11, 42 verses, opens not with ritual but with consolation: why tears do not help, why death and birth are certain. Then it settles who must perform the rites when there is no son, and lays down the daily rite in detail.

It ends each day with a short prayer to Hari for the departed’s release.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Consolation first?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. The mourner must be steady before he can do the rite well. The text looks after the living too.

The question. What good comes of the ten-day rite, and who does it if there is no son? (v. 1)

DO NOT WEEP

Verses 3–8
Courage. The son should stay calm and hold back tears.
Bitter tears. The dead are made to drink the tears of their relatives.
Certain. For the born, death is certain; for the dead, birth.
No exceptions. Not Nala, not Rāma, not Yudhiṣṭhira escaped.

ZAPPY: The tears hurt the dead?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the image: grief doesn’t pull them back, it weighs them down. It’s a gentle way to tell mourners to let go.

The turn from grief to action. The chapter begins with consolation before describing a single offering. Its argument about the cycle of birth and death gives the mourner a way to place this loss inside a larger account. The verses then turn toward actions that can be undertaken even when sorrow has no immediate resolution.

THREE SIMILES

Verses 9–11

💭 A dream

The body is like a dream.

🌳 Shade

People meet like travellers resting in one shade, then move on.

🍛 Food

Food eaten in the morning is gone by evening; a body made of food cannot last.

🧘 So

Too much attachment is unwise.

Why the chapter needs more than one image. A simile can make the temporary nature of a meeting easier to picture. Several images, placed together, let the text return to that thought from different angles. Their work is preparatory: before Garuḍa hears who performs the rites, he hears why a mourner might be able to act at all.

IF THERE IS NO SON

Verses 12–17
1The wife
2A brother
3A pupil or proper kinsman
4A brother’s son or grandson
5A friend
6The family priest

Shared sons. By Manu’s rule, if one of several brothers has a son, all are counted as having a son; so too co-wives.

Never skipped. The rites must not be neglected; if no one else, the priest does them.

THE FRIEND’S MERIT

Verse 18

The verse. A man or a woman who performs these rites for a friend gains the fruit of tens of millions of sacrifices.

Man or woman. The verse names both.

The thread. It continues chapter 7’s story: no one should be left without rites, and anyone who steps in is honoured.

ZAPPY: The highest merit is for a friend.

PROF. BOLT: Higher praise than for a son doing his duty. Doing what you don’t have to do counts most.

The obligation can reach beyond the family. The chapter gives special attention to the person who acts when a closer relative is absent. The friend’s merit makes willingness part of the moral account. Read with Babhruvāhana’s story, it shows how the text can value care undertaken without the strongest claim of kinship.

ONE SON PERFORMS

Verses 19–21

👨 For the father

The son performs his father’s ten-day rite.

💔 Not the father

If an eldest son dies, the father should not perform it, however great his love.

☝️ Only one

Among many sons, one does the ten days and the sixteen śrāddhas.

➗ After division

Once wealth is divided, annual śrāddhas may be done separately by each.

THE MOURNER’S RULES

Verses 22–24
One meal. A single meal a day.
The floor. Sleep on the ground.
Pure. Celibate and devoted.
A piṇḍa a day. One offering each day.

🛕 Its fruit

Equal to walking seven times around the holy places.

🌅 A year

A son who keeps the year of rites from the ten days onward gains the fruit of a śrāddha at Gayā.

WHERE AND WHEN

Verses 25–26

⏰ When

Between nine in the morning and noon.

💧 Where

At a well, tank, garden, holy bathing place or temple.

🧭 Facing

Seated facing south, at the root of a tree.

🟫 The altar

A small square mound cleaned with cow dung.

Without mantras. The bath before the rite is taken silently.

THE GRASS FIGURE

Verses 27–29
The figure. A small figure of darbha and kuśa grass on leaves stands for the departed.
Honoured. Water for the feet and other offerings, with a bow.
The seat. Kuśa spread in front as the piṇḍa’s seat.
The piṇḍa. Cooked rice or barley meal, offered in the departed’s family name.

Why the grass figure matters. At the place of offering the grass figure gives a visible focus to actions addressed to someone absent. The invocation and the name specify whom the offering is for. This combination of figure, place and name helps the mourner give a repeated action a particular recipient each morning.

THE OFFERINGS

Verses 29–30
🌿Uśīra root and sandal paste
🌼Bhṛṅgarāja flowers
🪔Incense, a lamp
🍚Food and mouth-fresheners
🐦‍⬛Food for crows
🥛Milk and water
🫙Handfuls of castor oil in a pot

Crows. The crow’s share is why families watch for a crow to take the offering.

IN THEIR NAME

Verses 30–33

The prayer. “May what is given in this earthly name endure.”

Lasting. Food, clothes, water and wealth given in the departed’s name give the dead lasting good.

Daily. The name is said every day from the first; one piṇḍa a day, nine over nine days, then the tenth.

ZAPPY: Why the name every time?

PROF. BOLT: The name addresses the gift. Without it, the offering has no one to go to.

Naming makes the act particular. The daily offerings recur, but the name keeps them from becoming interchangeable. The person remembered remains identifiable through each step of the rite. Chapter 13 will show a later change, when an offering for that one person is joined to those for the ancestors.

THE NINTH DAY

Verses 34–36
Oil. Kinsmen anoint themselves with oil, wishing the dead heaven.
Outside. They bathe in the open.
To the place. Carrying panic grass and parched grain, women walking in front, they go to the place of the dead.
A blessing. “May the family spread like panic grass and shine like parched grain.”
Home. The grass and grain are left in the house.

THE TENTH DAY

Verses 37–39

🫘 The piṇḍa

A ball of black gram (māṣa); the older ball of flesh is forbidden in this age.

✂️ Shaving

The son and relatives shave; the son shaves completely.

🍽️ Feeding

A learned man is fed seasoned food.

🙏 Praying

Hands joined, the son meditates on Hari for the departed’s release.

ZAPPY: Flesh was once offered?

PROF. BOLT: The verse says so, and then says it is forbidden now. Old practice, set aside by the text itself.

The threshold after ten mornings. The tenth day completes the sequence the chapter has followed. Seen from Chapter 1, the daily gifts belong to the departed’s passage. Seen here, they give a mourner repeated work during the first days of loss. Chapter 12 adds a new set of acts on the far side of that threshold.

THE DAILY PRAYER

Verses 40–42

Govinda. Those who bow to Govinda, the eternal, clad in yellow, lovely as the atasī flower, know no fear.

The invocation. To the beginningless, endless God, bearer of conch, discus and mace: release the departed.

Every day. Said at the end of each day’s śrāddha. Then bathe, go home, feed a cow, and only then eat.

ZAPPY: The cow is fed before the mourner?

PROF. BOLT: Every day of the ten. Others are fed first, then oneself.

THE TEN DAYS TODAY

How the chapter is kept

🏠 At home

Many families keep the lamp, the simple food and the floor bed for the ten days.

✂️ The tenth

Shaving on the tenth day is widely kept.

🐦‍⬛ Crows

Offerings left for crows remain common.

🔀 Varies

The number of days and details differ by community; the priest guides.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Chapter 11 in four lessons

💧 Grieve gently

Tears don’t help the dead; steady service does.

🤝 Someone always

Wife, brother, friend or priest: the rite is never left undone.

🧘 Live simply

One meal, the floor, purity, for ten days.

🙏 Pray daily

Each day ends asking Hari to free the departed.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: Hold back the tears, keep the rite every morning, and end each day with a prayer.
