THE ELEVENTH DAY

The bull release and the forty-eight śrāddhas

Foreword

The first ten days are over. Now Viṣṇu describes gifts, the release of a bull and the groups of offerings that extend through the first year.

After ten mornings

Verses 1–20

The previous chapter gave each of the first ten mornings a place in the household’s mourning. On the eleventh day the scale of the rite changes. The chapter includes gifts such as a furnished bed given in the departed’s name. A bed ordinarily belongs to the intimate life of the person who uses it. Here it is handed to someone living, while the words of dedication keep the departed at the centre of the act.

An altar arranged by direction

Verses 6–13

The four images occupy the perimeter. The mourner prepares the central space with kuśa grass and faces south for the water offering. The translation then moves from water to the fire offering and the eleventh-day śrāddha. The diagram shows that arrangement as it is described in this chapter.

ZAPPY: Why does the text name both the metal and the direction?

PROF. BOLT: The two details make each presence unmistakable. Four images surround the water offering, so the altar gives the action an order the mourner can see.

The bull as dharma

Verses 21–36

The chapter spends time distinguishing animals suitable for dedication and assigns unusual importance to the rite for the ancestors. Its elaborate classifications belong to the ritual world of the text. The central instruction is that the dedicated animal be released. Once set apart by that act, it is taken out of ordinary domestic use.

Two acts involving cattle

Verses 12–20 and 37–42

Keeping these gifts separate helps connect the chapter to B8. That earlier chapter dwelt on the cow that helps at the Vaitaraṇī. Here the bull is addressed as dharma itself. The two passages share the language of crossing while assigning it to different ritual acts.

ZAPPY: Is the cow gift the same thing as releasing the bull?

PROF. BOLT: They are separate acts in the account. One passage describes presenting a cow. Later the bull is honoured and released, and the prayer asks it to help the departed cross the ocean of existence.

The trident and discus

Verses 37–43

The account describes a young bull and cow being honoured together. The bull is marked with Śiva’s trident and Viṣṇu’s discus on opposite sides. The prayer at the release addresses the bull as dharma in animal form and asks for help across the ocean of existence. The gesture makes the prayer visible: someone opens a way and asks that a way be opened for the departed.

From tether to release

Verses 37–43

Later verses imagine an individual undertaking the dedication while still alive. They also condemn cruelty toward bulls. The animal’s release has a consequence in the world of the living, alongside the benefit the chapter ascribes to it for the dead.

ZAPPY: Why honour the animal before setting it free?

PROF. BOLT: The sequence sets it apart from an ordinary animal kept for work. Once the prayer calls the bull a form of dharma, releasing it becomes the act that expresses the prayer.

The release and the care of the animal

Verses 44–56

The text treats the release as an act a person can undertake in life as well as a rite associated with death. It then condemns loading or striking the animal. This detail gives the dedication a consequence beyond the ceremony itself: treating the bull as ordinary labour would contradict the status the rite has just given it.

Three groups of sixteen

Verses 57–69

The first sixteen gather six offerings associated with the death, funeral and collection of the bones, and the ten daily offerings. The middle sixteen are apportioned among divine and other recipients, including the departed. The last sixteen comprise twelve monthly offerings and four at additional points in the calendar. The forty-eight therefore span a series of occasions. The eleventh-day chapter gathers them into an account of how the departed may join the ancestors.

The forty-eight at a glance

Verses 57–69

The first six are associated with the place of death, the threshold, the midway point, the pyre, the hand of the deceased and the collection of bones. The middle group opens with Viṣṇu, Śiva and Yama’s retinue. Soma and the bearers of oblations follow; later the departed is named directly. The chapter repeats some divine recipients. It is counting offerings, not looking for forty-eight different names.

ZAPPY: Where does the total of forty-eight come from?

PROF. BOLT: Add three groups of sixteen. The first includes rites already described; the middle has its own list of recipients; the third follows the calendar.

Looking ahead to B13

Verses 68–80

The text understands the offerings as changing the departed’s situation, and the next chapter makes this change visible through the joining of the rice offerings. There are two stages of the explanation: here the text asks how the departed is helped through the period of transition; next it asks what it means to be received among the ancestors. The chapter describes a particular textual tradition. Family practice can follow a different sequence.

When the ordinary sequence is interrupted

Verses 70–80

Viṣṇu’s question is how the transition can be completed when the usual circumstances do not hold. This concern links the chapter back to the story of Babhruvāhana and forward to the joining of the ancestors in B13.

ZAPPY: Does the chapter imagine anyone besides a son performing the rites?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. It expressly describes a wife performing rites for her husband. Its later verses also take up exceptional deaths and name a separate rite, Nārāyaṇa-bali.

A question for Prof. Bolt

ZAPPY: Were the forty-eight offerings all made on the eleventh day?

PROF. BOLT: The chapter counts three groups. One looks back to the funeral and the ten mornings, one names particular recipients, and one follows the calendar through the year.
