YAMA’S CITY

Citragupta, the assembly of justice and four gates

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

Foreword

The southern road ended at a gate. Garuḍa now asks about the city that lies beyond it, the people in Yama’s assembly and the roads taken by the meritorious.

From the road to the record

Verses 1–23

Chapter 2 came to a halt at the southern gate. Here Garuḍa asks about the city itself. The description distinguishes Citragupta’s abode, where good and evil acts are recorded, from the central palace of the King of Justice. The movement between those places gives the reader a way to follow the inquiry: a person’s life can be accounted for before its consequences are settled.

Three places inside the city

Verses 3–23

The city’s measurements, materials and directions belong to its Purāṇic description. The sequence matters more to the narrative than the distances: there are approaches to the city, a place where lives are recorded and a court where righteousness is made visible.

ZAPPY: Is Citragupta’s dwelling the same as Yama’s palace?

PROF. BOLT: The chapter describes them separately. Citragupta attends to the deeds of each person; the palace contains the assembly where the King of Justice is seen.

A hall without thirst

Verses 24–30

The assembly has light, food, water and a temperate climate. These details recall the opening of Chapter 2, where even shade and drinking water are absent. The change in surroundings tells us how different it is to enter the city as one of its honoured inhabitants. The assembly is part of the same city as the southern approach, but the experience described is entirely different.

From the road to the hall

Verses 24–30 and 48–50

The hall is described as a place without hunger, thirst, old age or sorrow. The earlier road showed what it was like for the traveler to be deprived of every ordinary comfort. Here ordinary comforts are made abundant enough to express the difference between the two accounts.

ZAPPY: Why does the chapter say so much about food and water here?

PROF. BOLT: It is answering the earlier picture of the road. Readers who remember B2 can feel the change as soon as water is plentiful and the air is temperate.

What makes the court just

Verses 31–49

Yama sits as a sovereign among sages, ancestral figures and renowned rulers. Death, Time and Citragupta are all present. After listing these figures the narrative singles out the quality of their assembly: judgment there is free from favouritism. The grandeur of the hall gives the court its setting; this last detail explains why the account calls it the assembly of righteousness.

Who serves in the assembly

Verses 31–49

Among the sages are Vasiṣṭha, Atri, Agastya and Nārada. The kings named include Bhagīratha, Śibi, Nala and Bharata. The names place the court within a larger world of stories about rule, duty, renunciation and generosity.

ZAPPY: What are Death and Time doing in a court that also welcomes good people?

PROF. BOLT: Their presence tells us which court this is. They attend Yama, but the assembly also includes teachers, ancestors and rulers. The chapter makes impartial judgment the reason these very different figures belong together.

Three other approaches

Verses 50–73

The eastern way includes people who teach, console others or provide shelter and warmth as well as those praised for worship. The north includes people who honour guests and those devoted to sacred observances. The west brings together devotees of Viṣṇu, readers of sacred texts and people concerned for the welfare of others. Read side by side, the lists show how practical care and religious discipline both enter the chapter’s account of merit.

The four approaches

Verses 50–73

These groupings are portraits of ways of living. The chapter mentions both very large gifts and acts a person could do in an ordinary day, such as teaching someone or speaking to comfort a distressed person.

ZAPPY: Do the three pleasant roads reward exactly the same things?

PROF. BOLT: Their lists overlap, but each has its own emphases. The east names people who provide shelter and consolation. The north gives hospitality and sacred observance a prominent place. The west includes devotees, students of scripture and people who work for others’ welfare.

The judge rises to receive his guests

Verses 74–81

The welcoming scene gives Yama the gestures of a host. He rises, offers a seat and honours the people who have come to the assembly. Among those admitted through the other gates are people praised for their own hospitality. The welcome lets them receive a form of care that some of them once gave.

Why heaven belongs to the next question

Verses 74–86

The chapter goes on to describe the welcome extended to the meritorious. That welcome makes sense of Garuḍa’s question at the start of Chapter 15: if someone has enjoyed heaven, what happens when they are born again? The two chapters distinguish a favourable result of actions from the end of rebirth itself.

Yama receives the meritorious

Verses 74–86

The narrative describes Yama here in a four-armed form holding conch, discus, mace and sword. That image belongs to the scene of welcome. It offers a deliberate contrast with the frightening figure seen from the southern road.

ZAPPY: Why does Yama leave his seat to greet them?

PROF. BOLT: The passage shows him honouring his guests in person. It brings the catalogue of good deeds to a scene where the people who once acted generously are themselves welcomed.

A question for Prof. Bolt

ZAPPY: Is this the same Yama we met at the southern gate?

PROF. BOLT: Yes. Here we are shown his court and the other approaches. With the meritorious he acts as a host, even rising to welcome them.
