GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 16 OF 16

THE LAW OF LIBERATION

Human life, knowledge and the end of rebirth

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

Foreword

Garuḍa’s last question concerns release from repeated birth and death. Viṣṇu answers through images of daily life, then explains what knowledge and remembrance can mean at the end of life.

Why the body is worth caring for

Verses 1–23

The final chapter treats human embodiment as a chance to seek release. It makes a chain of purposes: the body supports the performance of duty, duty makes room for knowledge, and knowledge directs meditation. This explains why a chapter about letting go also tells the reader to care for the body. Its limited duration is what makes the opportunity precious.

What a human life makes possible

Verses 1–23

Viṣṇu’s answer begins with the restless movement of beings through forms of life. Against that background human birth is presented as an opportunity to pursue what the opening of the chapter calls the highest good.

ZAPPY: Why does a chapter about liberation tell us to look after the body?

PROF. BOLT: Because the chapter calls the body the means by which duty, knowledge and meditation become possible. The same body is temporary, which makes the opportunity worth using.

Digging the well too late

Verses 24–44

An unbaked pot slowly dissolves in water; a householder starts digging a well only after the house is on fire. The two images make different points about delay. The first makes it hard to notice how much time has gone. The second shows a kind of preparation that takes longer than a crisis allows. Together they explain Viṣṇu’s insistence that discernment belongs in ordinary life.

Three images of passing time

Verses 24–44

The animal images put a sharper edge on the thought: age approaches like a tigress, and the fish sees the food before it notices the hook. The chapter uses such scenes to make attachment and delay observable in familiar situations.

Three images of passing time
ItemIn the chapterWhat it explains
Water from a broken potLife steadily passesWhat has drained away cannot be put back
Unbaked pot in waterLoss can be slow and almost imperceptibleThe shape changes before one notices it
A well dug during a firePreparation can be delayed past the moment it would helpThe time to dig belongs before the crisis

ZAPPY: Why use both a leaking pot and a dissolving one?

PROF. BOLT: The leaking pot makes loss visible. The unbaked pot shows change one hardly notices. Both prepare for the image of a man who starts digging a well only once the house is already burning.

The company we keep

Verses 45–69

Viṣṇu turns from the passage of time to habits of attachment. He recognizes that relinquishing everything is difficult and gives a practical starting point in the company of discerning people. The discussion then questions disciplines pursued for display. What matters is the change in attention and understanding that practice makes possible.

Talking about a lamp

Verses 70–91

The teacher compares scriptural discussion without realization to talking about a lamp in a dark room. The discussion can describe what a lamp does, but it has yet to illuminate the room. The following verses distinguish learning acquired by study from discernment. The chapter values the teacher’s word for what it can lead the listener to see.

Hearing, reading and seeing

Verses 70–91

The teacher’s word matters within the same argument. The chapter associates it with discernment, a way of finding out what is true, and distinguishes it from the knowledge of words gained through study.

ZAPPY: Is the lamp image an argument against reading the scriptures?

PROF. BOLT: It is an argument about what reading is for. Describing a lamp cannot light a room, just as accumulating descriptions cannot take the place of seeing what they point toward.

What it means to say ‘mine’

Verses 92–101

The chapter puts bondage and release into two expressions, one claiming possession and the other releasing the claim. This concerns the stance a person takes toward things and relationships, including the thought that they can provide permanent security. The earlier warnings about wealth, time and attachment become more exact when read through this distinction.

What the claim of “mine” does

Verses 92–101

This section resumes a question already visible in the road chapters. The traveller tries to call on old wealth and relationships after their ordinary uses are gone. Here the text asks what it would mean to see their impermanence before the road begins.

ZAPPY: Does letting go mean the reader should stop caring about anyone?

PROF. BOLT: The point of the two expressions is the claim of possession. The text asks the reader to notice the grasping that makes passing things seem permanent and makes their loss feel like the loss of oneself.

The last remembrance

Verses 102–114

The final practice brings detachment, attention and remembrance together. Viṣṇu speaks of uttering Oṃ while remembering him at the departure from the body, and the passage also names places associated with liberation. In this translation the city list includes Gayā, where another familiar Saptapurī list has Māyā or Haridvār. Keeping the lists distinct allows both passages to be read on their own terms.

The final practices

Verses 102–114

The choice of Gayā belongs to this translation’s wording. The separate Saptapurī overview on Bhakti includes Māyā, or Haridvār, in its well-known list. Placing the lists side by side makes the variation plain without equating the two cities.

ZAPPY: How do the closing verses bring devotion back into the argument?

PROF. BOLT: The person who utters Oṃ at death remembers Viṣṇu. The passage brings concentrated attention, the teaching about the self and remembrance together, then names other settings associated with liberation.

Garuḍa’s silence

Verses 115–120

The closing verses distinguish an auspicious destination within the cycle from liberation from the cycle. Then the question-and-answer form itself comes to rest. Garuḍa bows, speaks of his doubts being resolved and falls silent in meditation. His response takes the reader back to the chapter’s image of the lamp: the teaching has been spoken, and attention now turns to what it illuminates.

The four outcomes at the close

Verses 115–120

Sūta returns as the narrator at the end. Garuḍa bows to Viṣṇu and falls silent, and the listener is left with that action. It is a fitting answer to the earlier passage on the lamp: after an inquiry has been answered, the work of attending to what it reveals remains.

The four outcomes at the close
ItemIn the chapterWhat it explains
Knowledge of truthLiberationThe chapter speaks of release from the cycle
Righteous conductHeavenA favourable destination distinct from release
WrongdoingAn adverse conditionThe southern road gives one earlier picture
Continued transmigrationFurther embodied existenceThe series closes by distinguishing it from liberation

ZAPPY: Why distinguish heaven from liberation in the last few verses?

PROF. BOLT: Because the series has described both. Heaven is a favourable destination and the end of rebirth is a different claim. Garuḍa’s last response concerns his doubt about liberation.

A question for Prof. Bolt

ZAPPY: Why does the last chapter keep returning to ordinary things like a pot or a lamp?

PROF. BOLT: They make its distinctions easier to see. A pot can dissolve slowly; a lamp gives light only when it burns. Both images ask the listener to notice what study and preparation are meant to accomplish.
