GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 2 OF 16

THE WAY OF YAMA

Twelve suns, a river of blood, and sixteen cities in a year

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

Chapter 1 ended with the sinner setting out on the thirteenth day. Chapter 2 walks the whole road with him: 86 verses, a year long.

It has three movements. First the road itself, a landscape built entirely of suffering, and the river Vaitaraṇī. Then the traveller’s laments, a long list of the good he never did. Then the sixteen cities, one for roughly each month, each matched to the offering his family makes at home.

Read side by side with a family’s calendar of monthly rites, the chapter turns out to be a map of them.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is any of the road pleasant?

PROF. BOLT: Not on this one. This is the southern road for sinners. The good have three other roads, described in chapter 14.

THE ROAD

Verses 3–6

🌳 No shade

No tree to rest under, no food to keep life going.

☀️ Twelve suns

No water anywhere; twelve suns blaze as at the world’s dissolution.

🌬️ Cold too

Piercing cold winds; thorns that tear; venomous snakes.

🐅 Beasts

Lions, tigers and dogs attack; scorpions sting; fire burns.

ZAPPY: Heat and cold together?

PROF. BOLT: The road gathers every hardship at once. It’s not geography, it’s an inventory of suffering.

The loss of ordinary shelter. Viṣṇu begins with things the traveller needs and cannot find: shade, food and drinking water. Only then does he describe what the road inflicts. The twelve suns evoke pralaya, the dissolution of the world. Yet cold winds also cut through the traveller. The contrast makes the point physical: there is nowhere on the road where the body can recover its balance.

THE SWORD-LEAF FOREST

Verses 7–8

Size. Two thousand yōjanas long and wide.

What lives there. Crows, owls, hawks, vultures, bees and mosquitoes; forest fires run through it.

Its leaves. Like sword blades, they cut whoever passes.

Elsewhere. The same “sword-leaf forest” (asipatravana) is a named hell in other lists.

When a tree offers no refuge. At the beginning of the chapter, the traveller cannot find a tree to rest beneath. Here an entire forest appears, but even brushing against its leaves causes injury. Later, at Saumya, the sight of a fig tree accompanies a brief rest. Read together, these three appearances of trees mark a change in the rhythm of the journey: the absence of shelter, the danger of approaching it, and a pause that cannot last.

PITS AND DARKNESS

Verses 9–14
🕳️A hidden well to fall into; a mountain to fall from
🔪Razor edges and spear points underfoot
🌑Pitch darkness; water, leech-filled mud, hot slime
🔥Sand of molten copper; heaps of embers; great smoke
🌧️Showers of charcoal, stones, thunderbolts, blood, weapons, boiling water, caustic mud
⛰️Chasms, steep climbs, deep valleys
🩸Lakes of pus, blood and filth

ZAPPY: That’s a lot of ways to suffer.

PROF. BOLT: The text piles them up on purpose. The list is meant to feel endless.

THE VAITARAṆĪ

Verses 15–20

📏 Size

A hundred yōjanas; it cannot be forded and its far bank cannot be seen.

🩸 Water

Pus and blood; banks of bones; mud of flesh; hairy moss.

🐊 Creatures

Crocodiles, porpoises, leeches, turtles, vultures and crows with beaks like diamond.

🔥 Seething

When sinners near, it bursts into flame and smoke and seethes like butter in a pan.

One river, two moments in the telling. The chapter first stops to describe the Vaitaraṇī at length. We hear its roar and see what happens to those who fall in before we learn how anyone could cross. The ferrymen appear much later, near the eighth city. That delay matters. By the time a boat appears, the reader already knows what it would mean to reach the river without one.

IN THE RIVER

Verses 21–26
Calling out. Those who fall in cry again and again for brother, son and father.
Thirst. Starving and parched, they drink the blood.
No rescue. Scorpions and black snakes fill it; no one can pull them out.
Whirlpools. Hundreds of thousands of them drag sinners down; they rise for a moment and sink again.
Its purpose. The river was made for sinners to fall into.

ZAPPY: Is there any way across?

PROF. BOLT: Yes, and the chapter gives it at verse 66: a gift. We get there at the eighth city.

DRAGGED ALONG

Verses 27–32

⛓️ Bound

Nooses, hooks; chains on neck, arms, feet and back; loads of iron to carry.

👃 Pierced

Nooses through nose and ears; weapon points from behind.

🔨 Beaten

Hammers from Yama’s messengers; crows pecking at them.

😩 Exhausted

Lamenting their own karma, they go on.

The key phrase. “Bewailing their own karmas.” No one else is blamed on this road.

THE LAMENTS

Verses 33–38: the good he never did
LifeA rare human birth, wasted without duty
GivingNo gifts, no fire offerings, no penance, no worship
Holy placesNo pilgrimage; no bath in the holy river
The goodNo service to the wise or the good
WaterNo tank dug in dry places for people or animals
CowsNo daily feeding of cows
LearningNo study of scripture; no listening to the Purāṇas

Sins of omission. Every line is something left undone. The lament is a checklist of what a good life looks like.

ZAPPY: So the list doubles as advice.

PROF. BOLT: Exactly. Read it backwards and it tells you what to do while you still can.

Regret takes a practical form. The traveller remembers acts of worship he neglected, but his regret extends to the needs of strangers. He thinks of the water tanks he could have dug where people, cattle and birds went thirsty. That detail recalls his own thirst at the start of the chapter. The road gives him time to imagine the relief he could once have provided and can no longer offer.

A WOMAN’S LAMENT

Verses 39–41

What she regrets. Not heeding her husband’s good counsel, not being faithful, not honouring elders, not serving him; as a widow, keeping no austerities or monthly fasts.

Satī. Verse 40 counts not entering the fire after his death among her regrets.

A woman’s body. Verse 41 says she was born a woman because of past misdeeds.

Of its time. These verses reflect the social views of the age in which the digest was written. Satī is outlawed in India and rejected by traditional teachers, and the idea that a woman’s birth is a punishment is not accepted by most of them today.

ZAPPY: Should these verses be skipped?

PROF. BOLT: They’re part of the text, so they’re shown. They’re also clearly of their time, and saying so is part of reading honestly.

SAUMYA

Verses 42–45: the first city
42 Remembering his past life, he cries: how did I come to this?
43 Seventeen days alone, swift as the wind; on the eighteenth, Saumya.
44 A city full of the departed, with the river Puṣpabhadrā and a lovely fig tree.
45 He rests there with Yama’s servants, remembers wife and sons, and is wretched.

A first comfort. The first month’s piṇḍas, given by sons and grandsons out of love or pity, reach him here (v. 51).

Why rest brings its own pain. After seventeen days on the road, the traveller reaches Saumya on the eighteenth. A river and a fig tree finally offer the kind of setting that the opening verses withheld. In the quiet, he remembers his family and former comforts. The scene changes the source of distress: for a moment he can sit still, and that stillness gives him time to think about what he has lost.

NO MARKET ON THIS ROAD

Verses 46–50: the servants reply
Where are they now? As he mourns his wealth and family, the servants ask where his money, children, wife and friends are.
Your own karma. He suffers only the fruit of his own acts.
No provisions. A traveller lives on provisions, but he packed none. On this road there is no buying or selling.
Everyone knew. Even children know of this road, from the learned or from the Purāṇas. Had he not heard?

ZAPPY: No buying or selling.

PROF. BOLT: The line that sticks. Wealth is useless here; only what you gave away travels with you.

What counts as provisions. The messengers answer his grief with the example of an ordinary traveller. Someone setting out on a long journey carries food because there may be nowhere to buy it. On this road there is no market at all. Wealth appears in his memories, while gifts and deeds appear in the messengers’ reckoning. The image asks what a person can prepare while preparation is still possible.

THE FERRYMEN

Verses 62–70: the eighth city, Vicitrabhavana
Vicitra. The city is ruled by Vicitra, Yama’s younger brother, whose huge form terrifies the traveller.
The boat. Ferrymen offer a boat across the Vaitaraṇī, but only to those with enough merit.
The meaning. The sages explain: Vaitaraṇī is crossed by a gift. Had he given a cow, the boat would come.
Without it. The river seethes; he sinks. The messengers skewer his lips and carry him over through the air like a fish on a hook.

The gift and the crossing. The ferrymen name a particular gift, the gift of a cow, as the condition for receiving a boat. Their explanation also associates the river’s name with the act of giving. That is the explanation the chapter puts into their speech. The scene turns a gift made earlier in life into a concrete possibility on the road: the traveller can see a means of crossing that he cannot arrange for himself at the bank.

ZAPPY: Every city names its offering.

PROF. BOLT: That’s the pattern: each stage of the road is paired with a rite back home.

ŚĪTĀḌHYA AND BAHUBHĪTI

Verses 79–83: the year ends

❄️ Śītāḍhya

Cold a hundred times the Himālaya’s. Starving and frozen, he looks in all ten directions: will any relative help?

❓ The question

The servants ask: what merit do you have? After the annual piṇḍas, he takes some courage.

🏚️ Bahubhīti

Near Yama’s abode he sheds the hand-sized body.

👍 Thumb-sized again

The thumb-sized self takes a body for suffering and flies on with the servants to work out its karma.

THE SOUTHERN GATE

Verses 84–86

🎁 Gifts in life

Those who made no gifts for their own living self go tightly bound and in pain.

🏛️ Four gates

Yama’s city has four gateways; this was the road to the southern one.

❓ More?

Viṣṇu asks Garuḍa if he wants to hear more.

➡️ Next

Chapter 3: the torments at Yama’s court.

ZAPPY: Gifts “for the upper body”?

PROF. BOLT: Gifts made by a person for himself while alive. The text says these count most of all.

Why the gate matters. The arrival at the southern gate puts a boundary around what Viṣṇu has told Garuḍa. There are four entrances to Yama’s city, and this chapter has followed the traveller assigned to the southern approach. Its catalogue of hardships belongs to that particular route. The last verses leave the traveller at the threshold, where the road ends and the account can turn to the realm of judgment.

THE ROAD AS A CALENDAR

Each stop matched to a rite

The fit. First-month piṇḍas at Saumya; six-monthly rite at Krūrapura; the river at six months; the annual rite at Śītāḍhya. The family’s calendar and the departed’s road keep pace.

A year measured from both sides. On the road, the months are measured in stops. Among the living, they are marked by offerings. At the city of the Gandharvas the traveller eats the third-month rice balls; at Śailāgama the fourth-month offering brings a little relief amid the falling stones. Near the end of the year at Śītāḍhya, the annual rice balls restore his courage. The chapter brings the household and the traveller into the same chronology, so that a rite performed at home becomes an event in his story.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Under the imagery

🧳 Pack provisions

Gifts, service and duty are the only food for this road.

🛶 Give while alive

The boat across comes to those who gave.

📋 Omissions count

Most laments are for good left undone.

🏠 Home still helps

Every month, the family’s offering reaches the traveller.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: There’s no market on the road to Yama: only what you gave travels with you.
