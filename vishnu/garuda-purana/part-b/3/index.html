GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 3 OF 16

THE TORMENTS OF YAMA

The witnesses, the judgement, and the twenty-one hells · explained and illustrated

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

HOW THIS WORKS: Part B3 of 16: chapter 3 of the Garuḍa Purāṇa Sārōddhāra, verse by verse. The gatekeeper Dharmadhvaja, the Śravaṇa witnesses, Yama's terrible form, Citragupta's rebuke, the blazing silk-cotton tree, the messengers' charge, the torments, the twenty-one hells with their meanings, and the return purified.

FOREWORD

The road of chapter 2 ends at Bahubhīti. Chapter 3 goes the last forty-four yōjanas to the city of the King of Justice and describes what happens there to the sinful: 71 verses.

First the evidence is gathered: the gatekeeper, Citragupta, and the Śravaṇas who have seen and heard everything. Then Yama shows his terrible form, Citragupta reads the charge, and the messengers carry out the sentence.

The chapter ends with the twenty-one great hells, and one line that changes how all of it should be read: after the torments, the soul returns, purified.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is this the same Yama who welcomes the good?

PROF. BOLT: The same. Chapter 14 shows him four-armed and gracious. Here, for sinners, he takes the other form.

AT THE GATE

Verses 3–8
The distance. Yama’s city lies forty-four yōjanas beyond Bahubhīti.
Heard first. The city’s attendants hear the sinner wailing and tell the gatekeeper, Dharmadhvaja, who stands at the gate always.
The report. Dharmadhvaja reports the person’s good and bad deeds to Citragupta, who tells the King of Justice.
Due process. Yama already knows the faithless, yet asks Citragupta; and Citragupta, though he knows all, consults the Śravaṇas.

ZAPPY: Why ask, if they already know?

PROF. BOLT: So that judgement follows evidence, even in a court where the judge knows everything. The text insists on procedure.

The gate is a threshold. Chapter 2 follows the traveller as far as the southern approach. Here the road gives way to an inquiry about the life that preceded it. The point of the gate is to bring a particular traveller before a court, where deeds can be named and examined.

THE ŚRAVAṆAS

Verses 9–15

👂 Who they are

Sons of a Brahmin, roaming heaven, earth and the lower worlds, seeing and hearing from afar.

👂 Their wives

The Śravaṇīs share their nature and know all that women do.

📢 What they report

Everything said or done, openly or in secret; every act of mind, speech and body, of mortals and immortals alike.

⚖️ Friend or foe

Pleased by austerity, charity and truth, they bring heaven and release; against the wicked they bring suffering.

ZAPPY: Can you win them over?

PROF. BOLT: Only by being good: austerity, giving and truthful speech. There’s no other way to please these witnesses.

Why the witnesses come first. The narrative puts witnesses and the keeper of the record between the arrival and the sentence. Their presence gives the court a way to know what happened beyond the sight of other people. In the story, a deed kept secret during life can still enter the record.

FOURTEEN WITNESSES

Verses 16–17
SkySun · Moon · Sky
ElementsFire · Wind · Earth · Water
TimeDay · Night · the two Twilights
Withinthe Heart
AboveYama · Justice (Dharma) itself

Nothing unwitnessed. With the King, Citragupta and the Śravaṇas, these see every being’s sins and merits fully.

The heart. One’s own heart is listed among the witnesses: conscience testifies too.

YAMA’S TERRIBLE FORM

Verses 18–23
🐃Seated on a buffalo, a rod in hand
⛰️Enormous, three yōjanas in extent; dark as a mountain of lampblack
💪Thirty-two arms, weapons flashing like lightning
⛈️A roar like the clouds at the end of the world
👁️Red eyes deep as wells, long nose, gaping fanged mouth
👥Death, Fever and the rest attend him; his messengers look like him

Even Citragupta fears. The scribe himself is afraid of this form. The sinners cry out; those who gave no gifts tremble most.

Two ways of seeing Yama. For the accused traveller the figure of Yama is frightening. Chapter 14 will show him in a luminous assembly, attended by sages and righteous rulers. These scenes are worth reading together: the same office of justice is experienced differently according to the road by which a person arrives.

CITRAGUPTA’S CHARGE

Verses 24–31
Why? Why, full of ego and without wisdom, did you sin?
The roots. Your sins came from lust, anger and bad company.
With delight. You did them with enjoyment; turning your faces away now is useless.
No favourites. Yama treats fool and scholar, beggar and rich, strong and weak alike.
Silence. The sinners grieve and fall silent, standing still “like thieves”; Yama orders fitting punishment.

ZAPPY: The same court for rich and poor.

PROF. BOLT: Verse 29 is the hinge of the chapter. Whatever earthly courts did, this one has no favourites.

The life behind the charge. The court makes the traveller look back on ordinary choices rather than only on the last moments of life. The accusation is intelligible because the person has already lived the events being recalled. The judgment scene gives a public form to the private reckoning that began in the laments on the road.

THE BLAZING TREE

Verses 32–39

🔗 The executioners

Pracaṇḍa, Caṇḍaka and others bind the sinners together with one noose.

🔥 The tree

A silk-cotton tree blazing like fire, five yōjanas wide, one high.

🙏 Begging

Hung head-down and beaten, they beg with folded hands for forgiveness.

🔨 Blows

Rods, hammers, clubs, spears, maces and pestles, until they lie still.

WHAT YOU DID NOT DO

Verses 40–44: the messengers speak
FoodNot even simple offerings of water and food
AnimalsNo food for dogs or crows
GuestsNo honour for guests
AncestorsNo water offered to them
YamaNo meditation on Yama and Citragupta, whose mantra would have protected you
The sacredNo pilgrimage, no worship
OthersAs householders, no compassion, no service

ZAPPY: A mantra of Yama and Citragupta?

PROF. BOLT: The text says remembering them in life would have spared this. Many traditions still honour Yama and Citragupta, for example on Yama Dvitīyā.

FORGIVENESS IS NOT OURS

Verse 45

The line. The messengers say forgiveness belongs to the Lord, Hari, Īśvara; they only carry out the order to punish wrongdoers.

What it implies. The servants of justice cannot pardon. Mercy is God’s, and it is reached through devotion, not through pleading at the tree.

Its echo. Part A8 records the Purāṇa’s own promise: those who call on Hari’s name are never taken by Yama.

ZAPPY: So begging at the tree is too late?

PROF. BOLT: That is the warning. The time to seek mercy is now, in life.

THE TORMENTS I

Verses 46–51

🔥 Heated

Heated until they fall like glowing embers; cut by sharp leaves; bitten by dogs.

🤐 Silenced

Mouths stuffed with dust; beaten while bound.

🪚 Cut

Sawn like firewood; chopped with axes.

⚙️ Crushed

Half-buried and shot with arrows; pressed in a machine like sugarcane; smelted like ore in burning charcoal.

THE TORMENTS II

Verses 52–55

🍳 Fried

Plunged into boiling butter or oil and turned like cakes in a pan.

🐘 Trampled

Thrown before maddened elephants; bound and hung head-down.

🕳️ Thrown

Cast into wells, hurled from heights, dropped into pits of worms.

🦅 Pecked

Great crows and vultures tear at heads, eyes and faces.

ZAPPY: Why such detail?

PROF. BOLT: The detail is the warning. Temple murals and scroll paintings across India drew these same scenes for people who could not read.

THE DEBT COLLECTORS

Verses 56–59
A strange scene. Some in hell cry out for the wealth they say was owed them; they can see their riches being enjoyed by others.
Paid in flesh. As they quarrel, the messengers tear flesh from the debtors with pincers and hand it over.
Into the hells. By Yama’s order all are thrown into the hells, beginning with Tāmisra, which lie near the tree.

The lesson. Debts left unpaid in life are settled after it, at a far higher price.

ZAPPY: Debts follow you after death?

PROF. BOLT: In this text, yes. Clearing what you owe is part of preparing to die, which is why chapter 8 lists it.

An account that has to be settled. The chapter’s language of debt makes consequences sound like obligations that remain after a person can no longer evade them. The comparison also helps explain the sequence: the court reads the record before the story turns to what must be undergone.

WHO GOES WHERE

Verses 65–70

🌑 Secret unions

Men and women who sinned in secret, illicit unions go to Tāmisra, Andhatāmisra, Raurava and the like.

🍖 Living off others

One who fed his body at others’ cost, and fed only his family or his appetite, leaves it all behind and goes alone.

💸 Wrong means

One who supported his family by unrighteous means goes to Andhatāmisra, total darkness.

🤒 Like a robbed man

He suffers like a sick man whose family’s savings have been stolen.

ZAPPY: Even if he did it for his family?

PROF. BOLT: The text is blunt: the family enjoyed the wealth, but he alone answers for how he got it.

RETURNED PURIFIED

Verses 65 and 71

How long. The sinful stay in the hells until the end of the age (v. 65).

Then. Having suffered the torments in their proper order, the soul returns to this world purified (v. 71).

What follows. Chapters 5 and 6 describe the kinds of birth that come next.

A sentence within a longer story. The chapter carries its account beyond the punishments. Return to embodied life matters because the series is describing repeated births and deaths. Chapter 16 will ask how that repetition can come to an end, a question distinct from what happens after this particular judgment.

READING IT TODAY

Views differ

📜 As described

Many traditional readers take the court and hells as real states after death.

🔧 As corrective

The 1911 translators stressed that these hells reform rather than avenge, and are not eternal.

🪞 As conscience

Some teachers read the court inward: the heart is among the witnesses, and the charge is one’s own memory.

⚖️ As justice

All agree on the core: every act is seen, and no one is above the law.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Chapter 3 in four lessons

👁️ Everything is seen

Sun, moon, the elements, the heart: there are no secrets.

⚖️ No favourites

Rank and wealth count for nothing before Yama.

🙏 Seek mercy now

Forgiveness is God’s; the messengers cannot give it.

🔄 Not forever

Punishment purifies and ends; the soul returns.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: Every deed has a witness, every judge is fair, and even hell is a passage, not an end.

SOURCES & NOTES

Where the facts come from

Sources: E. Wood and S. V. Subrahmanyam, The Garuda Purana (Sāroddhāra), 1911, chapter 3, verses 1–71, via hinduwebsite.com and sacred-texts.com. Read the 1911 translation of Chapter 3.

Sources differ: the source misprints verse 38 as 31; several hell names are glossed differently by the 1911 translators (for example Lōhitōda as “iron weights”, Saṅghāta as “accumulation”); the meanings here follow the Sanskrit words; the hell list repeats Sampratāpana.

Not verified: the Yama Dvitīyā custom and the murals and scrolls are general knowledge, not drawn from this text. Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
