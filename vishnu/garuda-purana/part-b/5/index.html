GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 5 OF 16

THE SIGNS OF SINS

What a wrong leaves behind in the next life

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

Chapter 4 ended with the sinner climbing back through every form of life. Chapter 5 asks a sharper question: which wrong leaves which mark? Its 57 verses pair each sin with a sign in the body or a particular birth.

Many pairings are almost poetic: the one who stole salt becomes an ant, the one who lied stammers, the one who would not share has a swollen neck. Others link wrongs to diseases and disabilities.

That second kind needs care. The text uses them to warn the living about the deed; it should never be used to judge a person who is ill or disabled.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: So if someone is blind, it’s their fault?

PROF. BOLT: No. That reading turns a warning into cruelty. The chapter is about what you should not do, not a diagnosis of anyone you meet.

HOW TO READ THIS CHAPTER

Before the list

⚠️ A warning

Its purpose, like the whole digest, is to turn the hearer from wrong.

🎭 Fitting images

Many pairings are symbolic: the thief of salt as an ant, the liar as a stammerer.

🚫 Not a judgement

Teachers today warn against using it to explain anyone’s illness or disability.

📜 Of its time

Several verses enforce caste and family rules of their age; they are marked where they come.

ZAPPY: Then why read it at all?

PROF. BOLT: Because it’s in the text people hear at funerals, and because the core idea, that our acts shape what we become, is one most traditions share.

The story changes its scale. The earlier chapters show a long road and a court. This chapter brings the result of action into the much smaller setting of another life. Its correspondences tell the reader to hold action and outcome together, even when the characters in the story cannot see both at once.

FOOD AND DRINK

Verses 5–6

🍬 Not sharing

Eating sweets alone brings a swollen neck.

🍷 Drinking

Intoxicants bring discoloured teeth.

🥩 Forbidden food

Eating flesh brings a ruddy skin; greed for forbidden food, a big belly.

🍚 The dead’s meal

Serving impure food at a śrāddha brings a spotted skin.

ZAPPY: A swollen neck for not sharing sweets?

PROF. BOLT: The image is the point: what you kept in your throat stays there. Most of these work like proverbs.

SPEECH AND LEARNING

Verses 7–9
False witnessmuteness
Telling liesa stammer
Listening to liesdeafness
Insulting a teacherfits
Despising scripturejaundice
Stealing a bookblindness
Spoiling a meal-rowone eye
Blocking a marriagea lipless face

Listening counts too. Not only the liar but the willing listener is marked.

Words can be an act. By placing speech among consequential acts, the catalogue asks the reader to take spoken harm seriously. A lie or a betrayal can change what someone else is able to do long after the words have been uttered. The chapter treats speech as something for which the speaker can answer.

THEFT I: VALUABLES

Verses 9 and 11

💎 Jewels

A low birth.

🥇 Gold

Diseased nails.

⚙️ Any metal

Poverty.

🦶 A kick

Striking a cow or a learned man with the foot: lameness.

ZAPPY: Gold thief, bad nails?

PROF. BOLT: The hand that took the gold carries the mark. The text keeps the punishment close to the act.

Why the stolen object is named. The lists often specify what was taken. Naming an object makes the offense particular: food, property and things entrusted for a special use carry different responsibilities. The catalogue asks the reader to notice the relationship that made taking each item a wrong.

DUTY NEGLECTED

Verses 15–20

🗡️ Violence for a living

Highway robbers and those who love hunting: a goat in a butcher’s house.

🐘 No restraint

An elephant in a lonely forest.

🐯 No offerings

Those who eat anything and offer nothing: a tiger in the wild.

🦢 Outward piety

Skipping the Gāyatrī and twilight prayer, pious outside and wicked within: a crane.

Priests. Officiating for the unfit: a village hog. Sacrificing to excess: an ass. Eating without grace: a crow. Withholding learning from a worthy student: a bull.

TEACHERS AND GUESTS

Verses 20–22
The lazy student. One who will not serve his teacher is born an animal.
The insulting student. One who threatens or spits at his teacher becomes a brahma-rākṣasa, a learned demon, in a waterless waste.
The broken promise. Withholding what was promised to a learned man: a jackal.
The closed door. No hospitality to the good: a howling fire-mouthed spirit.

FRIENDS AND TRADE

Verses 23–24
Deceiving a frienda mountain vulture
Cheating in tradean owl
Reviling the social ordera forest pigeon
Destroying hope or love; leaving a spouse out of dislikea ruddy goose, for a long time

The ruddy goose. In Indian poetry the cakravāka pair are said to be parted every night and call for each other till dawn: a fitting birth for one who parted lovers.

An obligation created by trust. The entrusted thing matters because another person has relied on the recipient to keep it safe. Turning that dependence to one’s advantage adds betrayal to loss. The chapter uses a recognizable human situation to make its wider concern with accountability easier to grasp.

FAMILY QUARRELS

Verses 25–27

👪 Parents

Hating mother, father or teacher, or fighting with siblings: dying in the womb, for a thousand births.

🏠 In-laws

A woman who abuses her in-laws and keeps the house in strife: a leech.

🗣️ Scolding

One who constantly berates her husband: a louse.

💔 Leaving

Abandoning a husband for another: a bat, a lizard or a snake.

Of its time. The family verses are addressed mostly to women and reflect the household order of their age.

FORBIDDEN UNIONS

Verses 28–33

The list. Relations within one’s own family line, with an ascetic, with a child, with a teacher’s, king’s or friend’s wife, and others the text forbids, each bring an animal or demon birth: hyena, desert spirit, great serpent, chameleon, donkey and so on.

A child. Verse 29 names union with an immature girl among the gravest, punished by birth as a huge forest snake.

The dead’s meal. Eating the eleventh-day food meant for the dead: a dog. Worshipping the gods only for money: born of a hen, barred from offering to gods and ancestors.

THE GREAT SINNERS

Verses 34–37
First hell. The gravely sinful pass through terrible hells and are born only when that karma is spent.
Killer of a learned man. Born of an ass, a camel, a buffalo.
Drinker. Born of a wolf, a dog, a jackal.
Gold thief. A worm, an insect, a bird.
Violator of a teacher’s wife. Grass, bushes, plants.
Thief of a wife or a deposit. A brahma-rākṣasa.

WEALTH THAT CANNOT BE DIGESTED

Verses 38–44

🧪 Worse than poison

Iron filings, ground stone and poison can be digested; a learned man’s stolen wealth cannot.

⏳ Generations

Taken by deceit, it harms a family for seven generations; taken by force, as long as moon and stars endure.

🏖️ Armies

Armies kept on such wealth collapse in battle like sandbanks in a flood.

⛪ Temple property

Seizing it breaks up families.

A fair rule. Giving elsewhere a gift meant for a learned man is neglect, but not if he lacks learning: one may offer into a blazing fire rather than ashes. The neglecter is born blind and poor.

TAKING BACK A GIFT

Verses 45–48

🌾 Land

Reclaiming land once given: sixty thousand years as a worm in filth.

✋ By force

Taking back any gift by force: hell until the deluge.

🛡️ Protect it

Having given someone land and a living, protect it; robbing them later: a lame dog.

🐄 The merit

Giving a learned man a livelihood equals giving a hundred thousand cows.

ZAPPY: Why is taking back a gift so bad?

PROF. BOLT: It destroys the trust that giving builds. Old land grants carried exactly these curses, carved in copper.

A gift creates expectations. Taking back what has been given can unsettle another person’s plans and expose how conditional the giver’s generosity was. The text’s attention to this act is a reminder to read its lists as accounts of damaged relationships, as well as catalogues of prohibited conduct.

THE WHEEL

Verses 49–55

Self-made. These signs and births are the karma beings make for themselves.

The order. Hell first; then the marked births; then thousands of animal lives bearing burdens; then birds in cold, rain and heat.

Human again. When good and bad karma balance, a human birth; then conception, suffering, death; the wheel of time turns through the four kinds of creatures.

How to read the correspondences. These associations are part of a religious account of rebirth. They do not permit a reader to infer someone’s conduct from illness, disability or poverty. The interpretive question the chapter itself poses is what responsibility looks like when a person cannot control how far the consequences of an act may extend.

THE POVERTY TRAP

Verses 56–57

The trap. The non-giver is born poor, sins out of poverty, goes to hell, is born poor again, and sins again.

The only rule. Karma, good or bad, must be lived out; unspent, it does not fade even in tens of millions of ages.

ZAPPY: How do you break the loop?

PROF. BOLT: By giving, the text implies: the first box is the only one you control.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Chapter 5 in four lessons

🪞 Acts shape us

What we do becomes what we are.

🤲 Small thefts count

Salt, honey, a book: nothing is too small.

🤝 Keep your word

Promises, gifts and grants are never taken back.

🎁 Give to break the cycle

Generosity is the way out of the poverty trap.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: We become what we do; so steal nothing, keep every promise, and give.
