GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 7 OF 16

BABHRUVĀHANA AND THE PRĒTA

A king, a starving ghost, and the rite that set him free

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

Chapter 6 ended with a warning: those who get no funeral rites go to hell. Chapter 7 answers with a story, the only full story in the Sārōddhāra.

A good merchant named Sudēva worshipped, fed his ancestors and gave to the poor, yet died with no one to perform his rites. He became a prēta, starving in a forest. A king out hunting found him, and freed him.

The chapter is about the duty of the living to the dead, and about a hope: that no one is left beyond help, because anyone may perform the rite.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: So it’s a ghost story?

PROF. BOLT: A rescue story. The ghost isn’t frightening; he’s hungry and asking for help.

Teller. Sūta tells the chapter; inside it Viṣṇu tells the story, and inside that the prēta tells his own.

GARUḌA’S QUESTION

Verses 1–4

Trembling. After hearing of hells and births, Garuḍa asks “for the good of mankind”.

What he asks. How can sinners, whether they sinned knowingly or not, escape the torments of Yama’s servants? What is the essence of the Purāṇas, and how do people reach a happy state?

ZAPPY: A turn in the book.

PROF. BOLT: Yes: from what happens to sinners, to what can be done for them. The rest of the digest answers this question.

WHY A SON IS “PUTRA”

Verses 5–12

🔤 The word

A son saves his father from the hell called Put, so he is called putra.

🛶 One is enough

A single righteous son carries the whole family across.

💳 A debt paid

Seeing a son’s face frees one from the debt to the ancestors; grandsons help one reach heaven.

🙏 If none come

Hear the Harivaṁśa, perform the Śatacaṇḍī, or worship Śiva with devotion.

Of its time. The text speaks of sons; verses 13–14 add rules on legitimacy and caste. Today daughters perform the last rites in many families, and the next verse already opens the door to others.

ANYONE MAY HELP

Verse 15, and the chapter’s last verse

Verse 15. A śrāddha performed by someone other than a son can still bring the departed to heaven.

Verse 68. Even a stranger’s śrāddha helps the dead; how much more a son’s for his father.

Why it matters. These two verses frame the story: it is told to prove them.

ZAPPY: So the son verses aren’t the last word?

PROF. BOLT: No. The story that follows is about a man with no son at all, freed by a stranger.

THE KING

Verses 16–19

🕰️ When

In the Trētā age.

🏰 Where

Mahōdaya, a prosperous kingdom.

👑 Who

Babhruvāhana, righteous, generous, learned and just.

⚖️ Why him

A good king is the right helper: as the prēta says later, a king is kin to everyone.

A different Babhruvāhana. The Mahābhārata has a Babhruvāhana, Arjuna’s son, king of Maṇipūra. This is a separate king of the Trētā age.

THE HUNT

Verses 20–27
20–22 Hunting in a thick forest, the king wounds a deer; it runs off with his arrow.
23 He follows the blood into another forest, leaving his army behind.
24–25 Tired, hungry and thirsty, he finds a lake; he and his horse bathe and drink.
26–27 Refreshed, he sits at the root of a great fig tree.

ZAPPY: A deer leading a king into the forest.

PROF. BOLT: A classic opening in Indian stories: the chase takes the hero somewhere he was meant to be.

Why the meeting occurs away from court. Babhruvāhana encounters the departed while hunting, away from the people who would usually bring petitions to a king. The setting makes the meeting unexpected. It also gives the departed a chance to be heard by someone who has no prior obligation to him.

THE PRĒTA APPEARS

Verses 28–33
A figure. A frightening being approaches: hunched, wasted, more curious than threatening.
A first gift. He says merely meeting the king has lifted him a little from his state.
The king asks. Who are you? What wrong brought you to this? What gift can end it?

The king’s three questions. They give the rest of the chapter its order: who he was, why he suffers, what will free him.

SUDĒVA’S LIFE

Verses 34–37

🏙️ Home

The town of Vaidaśa, prosperous and devout.

🧑‍💼 Who

Sudēva, a Vaiśya, a merchant.

🔥 Worship

He made fire offerings to the gods and fed the ancestors.

🍚 Charity

He gave to the learned and fed the poor, the blind and the destitute.

ZAPPY: He sounds like a good man.

PROF. BOLT: He was. That’s what makes his fate the point of the story.

Giving the departed a history. Sudēva is allowed to speak about the life he led. That changes the encounter from a frightening sight into a story about a particular person. The king can ask what went wrong and what another person is able to do about it.

WHY IT FAILED

Verses 38–40

Ill fate. His good deeds bore no fruit for him because of his misfortune.

No one. He had no child, companion, relative or friend to perform the rites for his departing self.

The sixteen. Without the sixteen monthly śrāddhas, the prēta state becomes fixed, even if hundreds of annual śrāddhas are done later.

The lesson. Personal merit and the community’s duty are two different things. The rites are not a reward for being good; they are owed to everyone.

The difference between conduct and care. The story sets Sudēva’s account of his former conduct alongside his lack of someone to perform the rites. This distinction explains why the proposed remedy involves a living person’s action. The king can provide a service the departed cannot carry out for himself.

HIS REQUEST

Verses 41–45
Perform my rites. He asks the king to do the rites for him: a king is kin to every caste.
A jewel. In return he offers a precious jewel.
His misery. He cannot bear his state; though there is water and fruit in the forest, as a prēta he cannot reach them.

ZAPPY: Water all around and he can’t drink?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the prēta’s torment in many stories: surrounded by what he needs and unable to take it.

Kinship and responsibility. The request tests how far the obligation of care can extend. The king has no family tie to Sudēva, yet he can respond to what he has heard. The episode gives narrative force to the chapter’s question about someone who dies without the expected relatives.

SEVEN THINGS THAT END IT

Verses 46–48
🙏The Nārāyaṇa rite, with Vēdic mantras and the rites for the departing self
📿Vēdic mantras
🔥Austerity
🎁Gifts
💗Compassion to all beings
📖Hearing scripture; worship of Viṣṇu
🤝The company of the good

Not only ritual. Compassion, scripture and good company stand beside the rite. The list is wider than any single ceremony.

THE NĀRĀYAṆA RITE

Verses 49–56, step by step
The image. Make a Nārāyaṇa from two pieces of honestly earned gold.
Dress it. Two yellow cloths, ornaments; bathe it.
Surround it. Four forms of Viṣṇu at the four quarters, Brahmā and Śiva at the centre.
Worship. Sandal paste and flowers; walk around; fire offerings; ghee, curd and milk to the universal gods.
The rites. Bathe, calm the mind, give up anger and greed, and perform the rites for the departed before Nārāyaṇa.
Gifts. Release a bull; thirteen sets of pots to the learned; a bed; a pot for the departed.

THE ARRANGEMENT

Verses 51–53

Viṣṇu on all sides. Four of his names guard the quarters; the creator and the destroyer sit with him at the centre. The departed is placed, in effect, inside God’s protection.

THE GOLDEN POT

Verses 57–63

🏺 Made of

Refined gold, filled with ghee and milk.

🧭 Consecrated to

Brahmā, Śiva and Kēśava at the centre; the guardians of the directions at the quarters and on the neck.

🌸 Worshipped

With incense, flowers and sandal paste.

🎁 Given

To a twice-born; given in faith, it surpasses all gifts in removing great sins and freeing the departed.

ZAPPY: The king asked about this specially.

PROF. BOLT: Verse 57: “how is the pot prepared?” The prēta calls it a rare gift that destroys evil.

Why the gift is described so carefully. The chapter gives the vessel and its setting much more attention than the plot strictly requires. In doing so it turns the king’s promise into a concrete act. His sympathy acquires an outward form that another person can receive and remember.

FREED

Verses 64–68 and the closing merit
64–65 The king’s army catches up; the prēta gives him the jewel, bows, pleads once more, and vanishes.
66 The king returns to his city, remembering the prēta’s words.
67 He performs the rites; the departed is released and goes to heaven.
68 Even a stranger’s śrāddha helps; how much more a son’s.
Merit Whoever hears this story, or makes others hear it, will not become a prēta, even after sinning.

What the story resolves. The king’s action ends Sudēva’s predicament. The resolution also answers the question posed at the beginning: the absence of a close relative does not end the story, because someone else can undertake the responsibility. Chapter 11 returns to practical questions about who may perform rites.

READING IT TODAY

How the story is used

🏛️ Nārāyaṇa Bali

A rite of this name is still performed for those who died without proper rites or untimely; this story is its classic source.

👧 Daughters

Many families now have daughters perform the rites; verse 15 already allows others than sons.

🤝 Unclaimed dead

Groups and temples that perform rites for unclaimed bodies act in the spirit of verse 68.

📖 Telling it

The chapter is read aloud in mourning homes partly for its promised merit.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Chapter 7 in four lessons

🧾 A debt we owe

The rites are owed to the dead, not earned by them.

👐 Anyone can pay it

A stranger’s rite works; a king did it for a merchant.

💗 More than ritual

Compassion, scripture and good company also free.

🌱 No one is lost

Even a fixed prēta can be released.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: No one should be left without rites, and anyone with a kind heart can give them.
