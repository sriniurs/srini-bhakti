GARUḌA PURĀṆA · PART B · SĀRŌDDHĀRA · CHAPTER 9 OF 16

THE RITES FOR THE DYING

Tulasī, Gaṅgā water, and the last breath

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya

FOREWORD

Chapter 8 was about gifts made as death approaches. Chapter 9, 48 verses, is about the hour itself: where the dying should lie, what should be near them, what should be in their mouth and ears, and what happens as the breath goes.

Its tone is gentle. Tulasī, sesame and sacred grass guard the place; Gaṅgā water and a verse of the Bhāgavata ease the way. The chapter ends not with Yama but with a chariot of the gods.

This is the deathbed the tradition hoped to give everyone.

Ōṁ Namō Nārāyaṇāya,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Many people die in hospital now.

PROF. BOLT: Families adapt: Gaṅgā water, a tulasī leaf, the name of God and a few verses can be given anywhere. The chapter’s heart travels.

Chapter 9, verse by verse

3–15The ring; tulasī, sesame, darbha, kuśa
16–20Why the dying lie on the ground
21–30Gold on the lips; śālagrāma and Gaṅgā water
31–35Scripture; fasting; renunciation
36–45The breath departs; body and Self; rebirth
46–48The chariot of the gods

The aim. Viṣṇu promises rites that lead the one leaving the body to a good state (v. 2).

THE RING BY THE TULASĪ

Verses 3–5
The place. Near a tulasī plant, mark out a ring with cow dung.
Sesame. Scatter sesame seeds in it.
Grass. Spread darbha grass over them.
Śālagrāma. Set a śālagrāma stone on the cleaned platform.
The promise. Release is certain for one who dies near the śālagrāma, which removes ills and sins.

TULASĪ

Verses 6–9

🌿 Its shade

Removes the pain of existence; gives a release hard to win even by gifts.

🏠 Its house

A home with tulasī is a place of pilgrimage; Yama’s servants do not enter.

🙈 Unseen

Yama cannot see one who dies holding a tulasī sprig, however sinful.

👄 In the mouth

Dying with a tulasī leaf in the mouth, on sesame and darbha, one goes to Viṣṇu’s city, even without a son.

ZAPPY: Even without a son.

PROF. BOLT: A quiet answer to chapter 7’s worry: tulasī alone can carry someone who has no one.

Bringing worship close. Tulasi and the other sacred things are placed where the dying person can encounter them. The chapter makes a devotional setting out of items familiar to the household. Its attention to placement suggests how companions can help gather the person’s attention when travelling to a shrine is no longer possible.

SESAME, DARBHA, KUŚA

Verses 10–13
SesameBorn of Viṣṇu’s sweat; demons flee from it
DarbhaBorn of Viṣṇu’s hair; merely touching it leads toward heaven
KuśaBrahmā at its root, Janārdana in the middle, Śiva at the tip

The three holy things. Sesame, darbha and tulasī together keep the ill from a bad end (v. 10).

WHAT STAYS PURE

Verses 14–15

♻️ Always pure

Kuśa, fire, mantras, tulasī, the learned and cows stay pure though used again and again.

⚠️ Except

Darbha used for piṇḍas or the meal for the dead, misused mantras, and fire on a cremation ground become unclean.

ZAPPY: Why does cremation fire become unclean?

PROF. BOLT: Its purpose is the dead. Things used for the dead are kept apart from everyday worship.

ON THE GROUND

Verses 16–20
Not raised. Lay the dying on ground cleaned with cow dung and spread with darbha, not on a cot raised in the air.
Who is present. Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Rudra, the gods and the sacred fire stand on the ring.
Kept pure. Any stain is plastered over.
Who enters otherwise. Demons, goblins and Yama’s followers enter impure places and raised cots.
No ring, no rite. Without it, no fire offering, śrāddha, feeding the learned or worship should be done.

The practice. This is why, in many homes, the dying are moved from the bed to the floor.

Why the space is prepared. The chapter describes the ground before describing what is said or heard there. This order makes the surroundings part of the ritual action. A cleared and marked place separates the bedside from the movements of the rest of the household, giving everyone present a shared point of attention.

GOLD AND HOLY WATER

Verses 21–22

🪙 On the lips

Gold and jewels are placed on the lips of the dying.

💧 Viṣṇu’s feet

Water that has bathed the śālagrāma is given to drink.

✨ A single drop

Even one drop absolves sin and leads to Vaikuṇṭha.

📍 Where

All of this on the cleaned and ringed ground.

Care through small actions. The text focuses on things placed close to the mouth and on names spoken or heard nearby. These are small acts that can still be performed when the dying person can do little for themselves. Within the chapter, their religious meaning comes from remembrance of Viṣṇu and association with sacred places.

THE GAṄGĀ

Verses 23–30
23Removes great sins; equal to bathing at every holy place
24Drinking it equals a thousand cāndrāyaṇa fasts
25Sin burns in it like cotton in fire
26Sun-warmed Gaṅgā water frees from rebirth
27Touching, drinking, even calling on it purifies
28It lifts the meritless by thousands
29Calling “Gaṅgā, Gaṅgā” as life flickers leads to Viṣṇu’s city
30Remembering it with faith at death gives the highest goal

ZAPPY: That’s why families keep a sealed pot of Gaṅgā water.

PROF. BOLT: Exactly so: for this moment, wherever they live.

WHAT TO HEAR

Verses 31–33

📖 Bhāgavata

Listen to the Bhāgavata, giver of release.

¼ A quarter verse

Even a verse, a half or a quarter of it at the last moment, and one never returns from Brahmā’s world.

🕉️ Vēda

Recitation of the Vēdas and Upaniṣads.

🎶 Hymns

Hymns to Viṣṇu and Śiva.

Not the Garuḍa. Notice: at the deathbed the text recommends the Bhāgavata, not itself. The Sārōddhāra is read after death.

When listening is still possible. The movement from objects to speech brings out a difference between hearing and performing a rite. A companion can recite even when the person at the bedside cannot join in. That gives the people present an active part and makes the sound of the divine name central to the scene.

“I HAVE RELINQUISHED”

Verses 34–35

Fasting. At the moment of death one should fast.

Renunciation. The twice-born, done with worldly things, may take sannyāsa at the very end.

The words. Saying “I have relinquished” while life still flickers leads to Viṣṇu’s city, with no rebirth.

The practice. Taking renunciation at the last hour, called āpat-sannyāsa, is still known in some traditions.

ZAPPY: Renouncing at the last minute counts?

PROF. BOLT: The text says so. Letting go is what matters, even at the end.

Letting go at the threshold. Near death the chapter presents renunciation as a change in what claims the person’s attention. The household remains close, but the narrative directs the mind beyond its familiar roles. Chapter 16 will give a longer account of the distinction between living among things and being held by possessiveness.

THE GATES OF THE BREATH

Verses 36–39

Separation. When the rising and falling breaths part, the subtle breath leaves the body; it falls “like a tree struck down by time”.

THE BODY AND THE SELF

Verses 40–43

🪦 The body

Now still, it becomes foul and shunned. To be proud of a body of worms, dung and ash is folly.

🌍 The elements

Each element returns to its own kind.

✨ The Self

Happy, all-pervading, ever free, unborn and undying.

⚖️ The contrast

What rots is not you; what you are cannot rot.

ZAPPY: A bit of Vēdānta at the deathbed.

PROF. BOLT: Right in the middle of the rites, the text stops to say the Self never dies at all.

THE BURNT HOUSE

Verses 44–45

The simile. Bound by desire and karma, the individual enters a new body shaped by its own deeds, “like a householder whose house has burned down”.

Why it fits. He does not choose the new house freely; his past decides where he can go.

THE CHARIOT

Verses 46–48
They arrive. Messengers of the gods come with a chariot hung with countless bells.
They know. Knowing true dharma, they carry away the one for whom the rites were done.
Arrival. He reaches heaven in a shining body, honoured by the holy ones, because of his gifts.

ZAPPY: No Yama’s servants at all?

PROF. BOLT: Not for this one. Chapter 1’s dark messengers came for the sinful; here the gods send a chariot.

Why the departure is visible to the story. The chariot gives the text a way to picture a departure the people at the bedside cannot follow. It marks a transition from the household’s acts to the fate of the departing being. The next chapter returns to what the living must do with the body.

THE DEATHBED TODAY

What families keep

💧 Gaṅgā jal

A few drops in the mouth.

🌿 Tulasī

A leaf on the tongue or in the hand.

📿 The name

Family chanting Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Nārāyaṇa or Śiva.

🏠 The floor

Where possible, laying the dying on a mat on the ground.

Varies. Customs differ by region and community; families follow their priest and their own tradition.

WHAT IT TEACHES

Chapter 9 in four lessons

🌿 Simple things suffice

A leaf, a drop, a verse, a name.

🧘 Let go

“I have relinquished” is enough, even at the end.

✨ You are not the body

The body returns to the elements; the Self is unborn.

🚂 A good end

For the good, a chariot, not a noose.

ZAPPY: One line?

PROF. BOLT: Surround the dying with holy things and holy words, and remind them they are more than the body.
