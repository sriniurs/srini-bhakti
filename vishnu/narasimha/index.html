Narasiṃha

The Man-Lion Who Came for a Child · explained and illustrated

The lion who came for a child

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Narasiṃha is: the boon that seemed to make a king immortal, the child Prahlāda, the pillar, the twilight and the threshold, the fierce form that became calm, the yogī and Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha, his temples from Ahobilam to Mēlukōṭe and Hampi, and a verse that asks for his hand.

WHERE WE ARE

Of all Viṣṇu’s forms, one is half man and half lion. Narasiṃha burst from a pillar to save a child from his own father, and is worshipped as the fiercest protector of devotees and, in calmer forms, as a teacher of yoga seated with Lakṣmī.

This full guide follows his story closely, reads his many images, and visits his great shrines, several in Karnataka and Andhra. His place among the ten avatāras is in Daśāvatāra: Narasiṃha; the nine-shrine hill of Ahobilam and Siṃhācalam have their own guides.

THE PERFECT BOON

Hiraṇyakaśipu’s plan

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 7.

Viṣṇu as Varāha had killed the demon Hiraṇyākṣa. His brother Hiraṇyakaśipu swore revenge on Viṣṇu and all who worshipped him.
He performed such terrible austerity that the worlds burned, and Brahmā came to grant a boon.
He asked for immortality; Brahmā could not grant it. So he listed every way he might die and asked to be safe from each: not by man or beast, god or demon; not by day or night; not inside or outside; not on earth or in the sky; not by any weapon.
Brahmā granted it all. Hiraṇyakaśipu conquered the three worlds and ordered that only he be worshipped.
He believed he had shut every door on death. One small door remained, and it was in his own house.

ZAPPY: He thought of everything!

PROF. BOLT: Everything his pride could imagine, Zappy. Devotion found the gap.

Every door but one.

THE CHILD WHO WOULD NOT BEND

Prahlāda

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 7, chapters 5–8.

Hiraṇyakaśipu’s son Prahlāda had heard of Viṣṇu while still in his mother’s womb, from the sage Nārada. At school he praised Hari instead of his father.
Asked by his father what he had learned, the boy answered: the best learning is to hear of Viṣṇu, sing of him, remember him, serve him. He taught the same to his classmates.
Furious, the king ordered him killed. Prahlāda was thrown from cliffs, trampled by elephants, bitten by snakes, poisoned, thrown into fire; each time, absorbed in Hari, he was unharmed.
In one telling the king’s sister Holikā, immune to fire, sat in a fire with the boy on her lap; she burned and he did not. That is the bonfire of Holī.
The boy never hated his father. He only refused to stop loving God.

ZAPPY: He wasn’t angry with his father?

PROF. BOLT: Never, Zappy. That’s what makes him the model devotee: unbending, but without hatred.

Unbending, without hatred.

“IS HE IN THIS PILLAR?”

Twilight on the threshold

The story

Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 7, chapter 8.

“Where is this Viṣṇu of yours?” the king demanded. “Everywhere,” said Prahlāda. “Is he in this pillar?” “Yes.”
The king struck the pillar with his mace. It split with a roar, and out came a form no one had ever seen: the face and mane of a lion, the body of a man: Narasiṃha.
He seized the king and carried him to the threshold of the hall, neither inside nor outside, at twilight, neither day nor night.
He laid him across his lap, neither earth nor sky, and tore him with his claws, which are no weapon. Narasiṃha was neither man nor beast, neither god nor demon.
Not one condition of Brahmā’s boon was broken. Every one was fulfilled to the letter.

ZAPPY: He kept the boon and still won!

PROF. BOLT: God doesn’t break promises, Zappy, even to a tyrant. He keeps them better than the tyrant expected.

The boon kept to the letter.

THE FIERCE MADE CALM

Prahlāda’s prayer

Too fierce to approach. Narasiṃha’s anger did not cool. Brahmā, Śiva and the gods dared not come near. In many tellings even Lakṣmī hesitated.

The child is sent. The gods sent Prahlāda. The boy bowed and sang a long hymn (Bhāgavata 7.9): he asked for no boon but devotion, and prayed for his father’s release.

Calm returns. Narasiṃha’s fierce (ugra) form became gentle (saumya). He placed his hand on the boy’s head, granted him a long and righteous reign, and assured him his father was freed.

Other tellings. Some Purāṇas add later episodes, such as Śiva taking the form of Śarabha to subdue him; Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva traditions read these very differently. Traditions differ.

ZAPPY: A child calmed the lion?

PROF. BOLT: The only one he would listen to, Zappy. Love tames what power cannot.

Love tames the lion.

HIS MANY FORMS

From fury to meditation

🔥 Ugra Narasiṃha

The fierce form: bursting from the pillar or tearing Hiraṇyakaśipu, worshipped for protection against danger and evil.

🧘 Yoga Narasiṃha

Seated with knees raised and a band (yogapaṭṭa) round them, in deep meditation: the lion as master of yoga. Mēlukōṭe’s hill shrine is this form.

🪷 Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha

Calm, with Lakṣmī seated on his lap: the protector as householder, approachable and kind. The most common form in homes.

🦁 Nava Narasiṃha

At Ahobilam, nine shrines show nine forms, among them Jvālā (flaming), Ahobila, Mālola (with Lakṣmī), Krodha, Kāraṃja, Bhārgava, Yogānanda, Chatravaṭa and Pāvana.

ZAPPY: Same god, so different!

PROF. BOLT: The lion who tears tyrants and the yogī who sits still, Zappy. Both protect.

Fury and stillness.

OLDER ROOTS

The lion in the Veda

Viṣṇu like a lion. The Ṛgveda (1.154.2) praises Viṣṇu as “like a fearsome beast that roams the mountains”; many read an early hint of the man-lion here. (Interpretation.)

Neither-nor. In the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, Indra kills the demon Namuci at twilight, with foam, neither wet nor dry: scholars note the same “neither-nor” logic as Narasiṃha’s story.

Early images. Stone images of Narasiṃha are among the earliest Vaiṣṇava sculptures, from Mathurā (around the second to fourth centuries) and from Kondamotu in Andhra (fourth century).

A southern favourite. His worship is especially strong in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka, often on hills and in caves.

ZAPPY: He’s that old?

PROF. BOLT: The idea is, Zappy. The story grew over centuries into the one we tell.

An old lion, a long story.

IN KARNATAKA

Mēlukōṭe and the Hampi monolith

Mēlukōṭe. On the rocky hill above the town, Yoga-Narasiṃha sits in meditation; the shrine is one of the oldest on the hill, and Prahlāda is said to have installed the image. See Mēlukōṭe.

Hampi. A great monolith of Narasiṃha seated in yoga, about 6.7 metres high, carved from one boulder; Lakṣmī once sat on his lap, and her broken image survives. Tradition dates it to 1528, in the reign of Kṛṣṇadevarāya. See Hampi.

Elsewhere in the state. Narasiṃha temples stand at Devarāyanadurga near Tumakūru, Ghāṭi Subrahmaṇya (with Narasiṃha beside Subrahmaṇya), Sāligrāma near Udupi, and in countless Mādhva homes.

Mantrālaya. Rāghavendra Tīrtha, believed by devotees to have been Prahlāda, makes Narasiṃha central to his maṭha; see Rāghavendra Tīrtha.

ZAPPY: A Narasiṃha carved from one rock?

PROF. BOLT: Six and a half metres, Zappy, and still sitting in yoga after five centuries.

Carved from one rock.

IN THE VERSE

Karāvalamba Stotra 1

“Give me your hand”
śrīmatpayonidhiniketana cakrapāṇebhogīndrabhogamaṇirañjitapuṇyamūrteyogīśa śāśvata śaraṇya bhavābdhipotalakṣmīnṛsiṃha mama dehi karāvalambam
You who dwell in the ocean of milk, discus in hand, whose holy form shines with the jewels of the serpent-king’s hoods; lord of yogīs, eternal, the refuge, the boat across the sea of becoming: O Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha, give me the support of your hand.
WordMeaning
payonidhithe ocean of milk
bhogīndraking of serpents, Śeṣa
yogīśalord of yogīs

A cry for help. Tradition says Śaṅkara composed this hymn when in danger, and that Narasiṃha rescued him. Each verse ends with the same plea: give me your hand. The fierce lion is addressed with Lakṣmī, as the gentlest refuge.

Text as given by shlokam.org (Lakshmi Narasimha Karavalambam), verse 1; attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara.

NARASIṂHA JAYANTĪ

The day at dusk

When. On the fourteenth day of the bright fortnight of Vaiśākha (April–May), at sandhyā, the twilight hour when he appeared.

How. A fast until dusk; worship at sunset; reading the Prahlāda story from the Bhāgavata; the Karāvalamba Stotra or the Narasiṃha Kavaca.

Pānaka. A drink of jaggery water with pepper and cardamom, pānaka, is offered to cool the fierce god in the summer heat; at Maṅgalagiri in Andhra the image famously “drinks” it.

In temples. Great festivals at Ahobilam, Siṃhācalam (where the image, covered all year in sandal paste, is shown in its true form on Akṣaya Tṛtīyā), Yādagirigutta and Mēlukōṭe.

ZAPPY: A drink to cool a god?

PROF. BOLT: He came out very angry, Zappy, and it’s summer. Pānaka is the tradition’s kindness.

Cool the lion at dusk.

WHAT NARASIṂHA TEACHES

God in the pillar, God for the child

God is everywhere. “Is he in the pillar?” “Yes.” Prahlāda’s answer is the story’s theology: no place is empty of God.

Protection of the faithful. Narasiṃha comes not for an army but for one child. Devotees pray to him in fear and danger.

Pride’s loopholes. No list of safeguards can make the proud safe; the gap is always where they did not look. (Interpretation.)

Fierce love. His fury is aimed at cruelty; his lap holds Lakṣmī and his hand rests on a child’s head. Ferocity in the service of love.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Narasiṃha?

PROF. BOLT: Stand up for one person being bullied, Zappy, without hating the bully. That’s Prahlāda’s way and Narasiṃha’s.

Fierce for the weak.
