Rāma

The Prince of Ayodhyā and the Ideal Man · explained and illustrated

The ideal man

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Rāma is: the prince of Ayodhyā, the bow, the fourteen-year exile, Sītā’s abduction, Hanumān and the war in Laṅkā, the return and Rāma Rājya, and how his name is loved.

WHERE WE ARE

Rāma is called maryādā-puruṣottama, the perfect man who never crossed the line: obedient son, loving husband, loyal friend and just king. For millions his name is the simplest prayer.

This guide tells his life from the Rāmāyaṇa in a series of story pages, then looks at his image, his devotees, the many Rāmāyaṇas and how his name is chanted.

Hanumān’s side of the story is in our Hanumān stories · 9 articles; Rāma among the avatāras in Daśāvatāra: Rāma.

STORY 1 · THE PRINCE AND THE BOW

Ayodhyā and Mithilā

The story

From the Bāla Kāṇḍa of Vālmīki’s Rāmāyaṇa.

King Daśaratha of Ayodhyā had no sons. After a great fire rite his three queens bore four: Rāma to Kausalyā, Bharata to Kaikeyī, Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna to Sumitrā.
The sage Viśvāmitra took young Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa to protect his sacrifice from demons and taught them divine weapons.
On the way Rāma freed Ahalyā, who had been cursed to stillness, by his touch.
At Mithilā King Janaka had promised his daughter Sītā to whoever could string Śiva’s mighty bow.
Rāma lifted it and, stringing it, broke it in two. Rāma and Sītā were married.

ZAPPY: He broke Śiva’s bow?

PROF. BOLT: Just by stringing it, Zappy. The hall shook.

The bow breaks.

STORY 2 · THE EXILE

A promise kept

The story

From the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa.

The day before Rāma was to be crowned, Queen Kaikeyī claimed two old boons: her son Bharata as king, and Rāma exiled to the forest for fourteen years.
Bound by his word, Daśaratha could not refuse. Rāma accepted calmly, to keep his father’s promise.
Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa insisted on going with him. Daśaratha died of grief.
Bharata, horrified, went to the forest to bring Rāma back. Rāma would not break his father’s word.
Bharata took Rāma’s sandals, placed them on the throne and ruled from a village outside Ayodhyā as Rāma’s servant.

ZAPPY: Bharata didn’t take the throne?

PROF. BOLT: He ruled with the sandals, Zappy. Brothers like that are rare.

A promise kept.

STORY 3 · THE GOLDEN DEER

Sītā taken

The story

From the Araṇya Kāṇḍa.

In the forest of Pañcavaṭī, Rāvaṇa’s sister Śūrpaṇakhā was rebuffed and wounded; Rāvaṇa swore revenge.
The demon Mārīca took the form of a golden deer. Sītā longed for it, and Rāma went after it.
Lakṣmaṇa followed at Sītā’s urging. Rāvaṇa, disguised as a wandering monk, came to the hut and carried her off in his flying chariot.
The old vulture Jaṭāyu fought him and fell. Dying, he told Rāma which way Sītā had gone.
Searching, Rāma met Śabarī, the old forest woman who had waited years for him and offered him berries she had tasted first to choose the sweetest.

ZAPPY: Rāma ate tasted berries?

PROF. BOLT: Because they were tasted with love, Zappy.

Love in tasted berries.

STORY 4 · HANUMĀN AND LAṄKĀ

The bridge and the war

The story

From the Kiṣkindhā, Sundara and Yuddha Kāṇḍas.

In Kiṣkindhā Rāma befriended the monkey king Sugrīva and his minister Hanumān; read our Kiṣkindhā guide.
Hanumān leapt the ocean, found Sītā in Laṅkā and brought back her jewel; read Sundara.
The monkey army built a bridge of stones across the sea to Laṅkā.
In the great war Rāma killed Rāvaṇa; read Yuddha.
Rāma returned to Ayodhyā with Sītā; lamps were lit to welcome them, a story many remember at Dīpāvalī.

ZAPPY: Stones that floated?

PROF. BOLT: With Rāma’s name written on them, says the popular telling, Zappy.

Victory and return.

RĀMA RĀJYA

The just king

Coronation. Rāma was crowned in Ayodhyā with Sītā beside him; Hanumān knelt at his feet.

Rāma Rājya. His reign became the ideal of good government: justice, no hunger, no fear. Gandhi used the phrase for his dream of India.

The hard part. The last book (Uttara Kāṇḍa) tells how Rāma sent Sītā away because of public gossip, a story that has troubled and divided readers for centuries. Traditions read it very differently.

Ayodhyā. His city; read our Ayodhyā guide.

ZAPPY: He sent Sītā away?

PROF. BOLT: In the last book, Zappy. Many devotees find it painful. It’s fair to say so.

The ideal, and its cost.

MANY RĀMĀYAṆAS

One story, many tellings

Vālmīki. The Sanskrit original, the ādikāvya or first poem, in seven books (kāṇḍas).

Kamban. The Tamil Rāmāvatāram, a masterpiece of devotion.

Tulsīdās. The Hindi Rāmcaritmānas, sung in homes across north India.

Kannada and beyond. Kumāra Vālmīki’s Torave Rāmāyaṇa in Kannada; tellings across Southeast Asia, from Thailand’s Ramakien to Indonesia’s dance-dramas. The story varies widely.

ZAPPY: Which is the right one?

PROF. BOLT: Each is right for those who love it, Zappy. Vālmīki is the source.

One story, many voices.

THE NAME OF RĀMA

Rāma nāma

The name. Rāma means “delight”. Chanting “Rāma” is among the simplest and most loved practices.

Śrī Rāma Jaya Rāma. A common chant; and “Rāma” is said even at the last breath.

Śiva’s word. A well-known verse says chanting Rāma once equals the thousand names of Viṣṇu. (Tradition.)

Hanumān. Wherever Rāma’s name is sung, Hanumān is said to be present; read our Hanumān Cālīsā.

ZAPPY: One name equals a thousand?

PROF. BOLT: So Śiva tells Pārvatī, in the tradition, Zappy.

The name is enough.

HOW HE IS WORSHIPPED

Rāma Navamī and his temples

Rāma Navamī. His birthday at noon on the ninth of Caitra; read our Rāma Navamī guide.

Vijayadaśamī. In the north his victory is marked by burning effigies of Rāvaṇa; read our Vijayadaśamī guide.

Temples. Ayodhyā, Bhadrācalam, Rāmeśvaram where he worshipped Śiva, and Kodaṇḍarāma temples across Karnataka.

Hanumān. Almost every Rāma temple has its Hanumān, and every Hanumān temple honours Rāma.

ZAPPY: Born at noon?

PROF. BOLT: At midday, Zappy, unlike Kṛṣṇa at midnight.

Noon and victory.

WHAT RĀMA TEACHES

Dharma in a human life

Keeping one’s word. He gave up a kingdom to keep his father’s promise.

Dharma under pressure. His life is the test of doing right when it hurts. (Interpretation.)

Love and loyalty. Lakṣmaṇa, Bharata, Hanumān and Śabarī show love answered by love.

Hard questions. The Uttara Kāṇḍa invites devotees to think, not just admire.

ZAPPY: So he’s a model to follow?

PROF. BOLT: And to think about, Zappy. Both.

Dharma lived.
