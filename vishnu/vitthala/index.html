VIṬHOBĀ · PAṆḌHARPUR · THE WALK OF THE VĀRKARĪS

Viṭṭhala

Viṭhobā of Paṇḍharpur and the Vārī · explained and illustrated

The god who waits on a brick

HOW THIS WORKS: Who Viṭṭhala is: the dark god standing on a brick, why he waits there for Puṇḍalīka, his Karnataka roots, the Vārkarī saints from Jñāneśvar to Tukārām, the great walk of the Vārī to Paṇḍharpur, and the Kannada Haridāsas who sang to him.

WHERE WE ARE

Every June and July, hundreds of thousands of people walk for about three weeks across Maharashtra, singing, with saffron flags, toward one small town on the Bhīmā river. They are Vārkarīs, going to see Viṭṭhala (Viṭhobā) of Paṇḍharpur, a dark young god who stands on a brick with his hands on his hips, waiting for his devotees.

This guide tells who he is, why he stands on a brick, how his worship links Maharashtra and Karnataka, who the great Vārkarī saints were, and what the walk, the Vārī, is like. The Ekādaśī that ends it is in our Devaśayanī Ekādaśī guide.

PUṆḌALĪKA AND THE BRICK

Why the god stands and waits

The story

Paṇḍharpur tradition (Pāṇḍuraṅga Māhātmya); versions differ.

Puṇḍalīka had once neglected his parents. Changed by a pilgrimage, he devoted himself entirely to caring for them in their old age.
Moved by this devotion, Kṛṣṇa came from Dvārakā to Puṇḍalīka’s door. In some tellings he came as a cowherd, in others as the child Kṛṣṇa.
Puṇḍalīka was in the middle of serving his father. Without leaving his task, he tossed a brick outside and asked the Lord to stand on it and wait until he had finished.
Delighted that his devotee put his parents’ care first, Kṛṣṇa stood on the brick with his hands on his hips, and waited.
He is waiting still: that is Viṭṭhala, and Rukmiṇī stands nearby as Rakhumāī. Vārkarīs read the name as “viṭ” (brick) and “ṭhala” (standing).

ZAPPY: He made God wait?

PROF. BOLT: And God loved him for it, Zappy. Caring for your parents is worship too.

Service first, then God.

READING HIS IMAGE

Dark, young and waiting

Black stone. At Paṇḍharpur he is a dark basalt boy about 1.14 metres tall, his hands resting on his hips, one holding a conch, the other a lotus or discus.

Ornaments. A tulasī-bead necklace, the kaustubha jewel, fish-shaped earrings, and a tall conical crown that some read as a Śiva-liṅga: Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava meet in him. (Interpretation.)

Rakhumāī. Rukmiṇī stands on her own brick in her own shrine in the temple, as Rakhumāī, “mother Rukmiṇī”.

Pāṇḍuraṅga. The name means “the white one” though he is dark; scholars have linked it to the town’s old name or to a Śiva form there. Devotees simply call him Viṭhū Māulī, Viṭhū the mother.

ZAPPY: A god called mother?

PROF. BOLT: Māulī, Zappy: the Vārkarīs’ tender name for him and for Jñāneśvar. Love doesn’t mind grammar.

Viṭhū, the mother.

WHERE DID HE COME FROM?

Karnataka roots and old inscriptions

A Kannada god? Jñāneśvar called Viṭhobā “Kānaḍā”, which can mean “from Kannada country” (and also “hard to understand”). The scholar R. G. Bhandarkar suggested “Viṭṭhu” is a Kannada form of “Viṣṇu”; others propose that the cult migrated from Karnataka before 1000 CE. (Sources differ.)

Inscriptions. A memorial stone of 1189 records a small Viṭhobā shrine; a Hoysaḷa grant of 1237 provides for offerings. Tradition credits Puṇḍalīka with persuading the Hoysaḷa king Viṣṇuvardhana to build a temple in the twelfth century.

Other theories. Some scholars see a pastoral Kṛṣṇa or a local cattle-hero; Ambedkar argued the image was originally a Buddha. Traditions and scholars differ.

Hampi. Kṛṣṇadevarāya, tradition says, took the image to Hampi, where the famous Viṭṭhala temple with its stone chariot and musical pillars was built; it was later returned to Paṇḍharpur. See Hampi.

ZAPPY: So Viṭṭhala is from Karnataka?

PROF. BOLT: Many think so, Zappy, and Kannada and Marathi devotees share him gladly either way.

A god shared by two languages.

PAṆḌHARPUR

The town on the crescent river

The river. The Bhīmā bends here in a crescent, so it is called the Candrabhāgā, “moon-shaped”. Pilgrims bathe before darśan; Puṇḍalīka’s shrine stands in the river bed.

The temple. Its oldest parts date to the Yādava period (twelfth to thirteenth centuries); most of what stands today was built in the seventeenth century. Rakhumāī has her own shrine within the complex.

Nāmdev’s step. The first step at the entrance is called Nāmdev’s step; tradition says the saint asked to lie there so that every devotee’s feet would touch him. Cokhāmeḷā’s samādhi is near the gate.

Touching the feet. Unusually, devotees may touch Viṭhobā’s feet at Paṇḍharpur, a sign of the tradition’s intimacy with its god. (Practice.)

ZAPPY: A saint who wanted to be stepped on?

PROF. BOLT: So every pilgrim’s dust would bless him, Zappy. That’s Vārkarī humility.

Humility at the threshold.

THE VĀRKARĪ SAINTS

Tailor, grocer, maid and outcaste

📖 Jñāneśvar (c. 1275–1296)

Wrote the Jñāneśvarī, a Marathi retelling of the Gītā, as a youth, and entered samādhi at Āḷandī aged about twenty-one; many count him the founder of the Vārkarī path.

🧵 Nāmdev and Janābāī

Nāmdev, a tailor, sang abhaṅgas (Marathi devotional songs) that reached as far as Punjab; Janābāī, a maidservant in his house, sang of Viṭṭhala helping her grind grain.

📜 Eknāth

Sixteenth-century scholar-saint of Paiṭhaṇ who revived the tradition and edited the Jñāneśvarī.

🛒 Tukārām and Cokhāmeḷā

Tukārām of Dehū, a grocer, whose thousands of abhaṅgas are sung everywhere; Cokhāmeḷā, of a community then treated as untouchable, whose samādhi lies at the temple’s steps.

ZAPPY: A tailor, a grocer and a maid?

PROF. BOLT: That’s the Vārkarī tradition, Zappy: Viṭṭhala listens to everyone, and anyone can be a saint.

Saints from every trade.

THE VĀRĪ

Three weeks on foot

The story

Wikipedia, “Vithoba”; Vārkarī practice.

In Jyeṣṭha, the silver pādukās (sandals) of Jñāneśvar leave Āḷandī near Pune in a palanquin (pālkhī); Tukārām’s leave Dehū. Other saints’ pālkhīs set out from across Maharashtra.
Pilgrims walk in groups called dindīs, each with a flag-bearer, cymbal players and singers, singing abhaṅgas and chanting “Jñānbā Tukārām”.
On the way there are riṅgaṇs: great circles in open fields where a horse, believed to carry the saint’s spirit, runs round the circle and pilgrims rush for the dust of its hooves.
The walk takes about three weeks and covers some 250 kilometres, with towns and villages feeding the walkers along the way.
On Āṣāḍhī Ekādaśī the pālkhīs reach Paṇḍharpur, and hundreds of thousands (over 800,000 by one count) bathe in the Candrabhāgā and seek a glimpse of Viṭhobā.

ZAPPY: Everyone just walks?

PROF. BOLT: Old and young, rich and poor, Zappy, side by side for three weeks. Many say the walk is the darśan.

The walk is the worship.

THE VĀRKARĪ WAY

Singing, walking, no barriers

Bhakti over ritual. The Vārkarīs value darśan and singing God’s name over elaborate ritual. Their chant is simple: “Rām Kṛṣṇa Hari” and “Puṇḍalīka varadā Hari Viṭṭhala”.

Open to all. From its beginnings the tradition welcomed every caste, women and men; its saints include tailors, potters, grocers, maids and those then called untouchable.

The marks. A Vārkarī wears a tulasī necklace, keeps the Ekādaśī fasts, avoids meat and alcohol, and tries to make the Vārī at least once a year.

The other great day. On Kārtikī (Prabodhinī) Ekādaśī, when Viṣṇu wakes from his four-month sleep, Paṇḍharpur fills again.

ZAPPY: So I could be a Vārkarī?

PROF. BOLT: A tulasī mālā, the Ekādaśī and a willingness to walk, Zappy. Viṭṭhala asks for little else.

Sing, walk, belong.

VIṬṬHALA IN KARNATAKA

The Haridāsas’ god

The Haridāsas. Kannada singer-saints of the Mādhva tradition, wandering and singing in simple Kannada: Śrīpādarāja, Vyāsatīrtha, Purandara Dāsa, Kanaka Dāsa and many more. Their tradition traces its beginning to Acalānanda Viṭṭhala (by tradition, about 888).

A signature. Purandara Dāsa ended his songs with the signature (aṅkita) “Puraṃdara Viṭṭhala”; many Haridāsas took Viṭṭhala names as their aṅkitas.

Royal patrons. The Vijayanagara rulers supported the Haridāsas; three Mādhva maṭhas have Viṭṭhala as their presiding deity.

Two traditions, one god. Marathi abhaṅga and Kannada dāsa-pada grew side by side, and the Haridāsas also made pilgrimages to Paṇḍharpur.

ZAPPY: Purandara Dāsa sang to Viṭṭhala?

PROF. BOLT: Thousands of songs, Zappy, each signed with his name. That’s our next guide.

Kannada songs for Viṭṭhala.

WHAT VIṬṬHALA TEACHES

Patience, service and equality

God waits for us. The story turns devotion around: it is God who stands patiently, hands on hips, until we come. (Interpretation.)

Service is worship. Puṇḍalīka put his parents first, and God approved. Caring for others is not a distraction from devotion.

Everyone belongs. Saints from every trade and caste, women and men, walk together to the same door.

Joy. The Vārī is hard, but it is sung and danced. Devotion here is a festival, not a burden.

ZAPPY: If I could do one thing for Viṭṭhala?

PROF. BOLT: Do one long, patient job for someone older than you, Zappy, before asking God for anything.

He waits; we walk.
