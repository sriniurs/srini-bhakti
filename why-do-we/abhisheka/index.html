Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka?

Bathing the Deity · explained and illustrated

Bathing the deity

HOW THIS WORKS: Why the deity is bathed: the word abhiṣeka and its royal roots, the five nectars, the contrasts of Śiva, Viṣṇu and Devī with their hymns, Rāma’s coronation and Kaṇṇappa, Karnataka’s great abhiṣekas, the bath at home, and the tīrtha that comes back.

WHERE WE ARE

At dawn in a Śiva temple, a priest pours water, milk and honey over the liṅga while the Rudram rolls on; a copper pot hangs above it, dripping without stopping. In a Viṣṇu temple the deity is bathed behind a closed curtain and then revealed glittering with jewels. At the Goddess’s shrine the bath is turmeric and vermilion, and she emerges in a new sari. All three are abhiṣeka, the bathing of the deity, done three different ways.

This part explains the word and its roots in royal consecration, the materials, the hymns that go with each bath, and the contrasts between Śiva, Viṣṇu and Devī worship, with Rāma’s coronation and the hunter Kaṇṇappa as stories, the great abhiṣekas of Karnataka, abhiṣeka at home, and what the tīrtha means.

The previous part was The Nine Forms of Bhakti; the next is What Makes a Mūrti Sacred?. The bath as one step of the sixteen offerings is in A Simple Home Pūjā.

THE WORD

Abhi-ṣic: to pour over

The word. Abhiṣeka comes from ṣic, “to sprinkle, pour”, with abhi, “over, towards”. It means pouring liquid over someone to consecrate them.

A royal rite. Its oldest use is for kings. In the Vedic rājasūya and in the epics, a king was made king by being bathed in waters gathered from holy rivers and seas, to the sound of hymns. Coronation in India is literally an abhiṣeka: rājyābhiṣeka, paṭṭābhiṣeka.

From king to god. When temple worship grew, the deity was honoured as the king of kings, and the royal bath became a daily rite. Every abhiṣeka in a temple is a small coronation, renewing the god’s presence and reign.

Also hospitality. In the sixteen offerings (upacāras) of pūjā, the bath (snāna) follows welcome, water for the feet and water to sip. In the home it is the gentle bath of an honoured guest; in the temple it can grow into a great ceremony.

ZAPPY: So the god is crowned every morning?

PROF. BOLT: In a way, Zappy. Every morning he is reminded, and so are we, who is king.

A daily coronation.

WHAT IS POURED

The materials of abhiṣeka

Each tradition and temple has its own list and order. These are the common ones.

💧 Water

Always first and last: clean water, ideally from a river or well, sanctified with the river verse (see Sacred Rivers).

🥛 Pañcāmṛta

The “five nectars”: milk, curd, ghee, honey and sugar, poured one by one or mixed. Afterwards it is shared as prasāda; see Prasāda.

🥥 Coconut water

Pure and sealed by nature; a favourite in south Indian temples.

🌼 Turmeric and kuṅkuma

Powdered or as paste, above all for the Goddess.

🪵 Sandal paste

Cooling and fragrant; often the last layer before dressing.

🍯 Others

Sugar-cane juice, fruit juices, rosewater, vibhūti for Śiva, sometimes cooked rice (annābhiṣeka) at certain shrines.

ZAPPY: Isn’t it a waste of milk?

PROF. BOLT: Many temples send it on as prasāda, Zappy, and some devotees offer only a little and give the rest to the hungry. The texts care about the love, not the litres.

Nectar, poured and shared.

ŚIVA, VIṢṆU, DEVĪ

Three moods of the same bath

A popular saying. A well-known line says that Viṣṇu loves adornment (alaṅkāra), Śiva loves abhiṣeka, and Sūrya loves salutation. It is a proverb, not scripture, but it captures real temple practice. (Traditional saying.)

Śiva: cooling. For Śiva the bath itself is the centre. The popular explanation ties it to the poison he swallowed at the churning of the ocean, which burns in his throat: water, milk and honey cool him. The liṅga is bathed again and again, often without stopping.

Viṣṇu: preparing. For Viṣṇu the bath is the prelude. It is often done privately, then the curtain opens on a deity dressed, crowned and garlanded. The bath is the preparation for the darśan of beauty; see What Is Darśan?.

Devī: nourishing and adorning. For the Goddess, turmeric and kuṅkuma, the colours of a married woman’s auspiciousness, join water and milk, and she is dressed in a new sari as a beloved daughter or queen. Many households offer this bath on Fridays.

ZAPPY: So every god has a favourite way to be bathed?

PROF. BOLT: A favourite emphasis, Zappy. All three get bathed and dressed; the spotlight falls differently.

Cool, adorn, nourish.

ŚIVA: RUDRĀBHIṢEKA

The dripping pot and the Rudram

Rudrābhiṣeka. The classic Śaiva bath is the Rudrābhiṣeka: the liṅga is bathed while priests chant the Śrī Rudram from the Yajurveda, its eleven sections repeated many times for great occasions. The hymn is explained in Śrī Rudram.

The dhārā pot. Over many liṅgas hangs a pot with a small hole, the dhārā-pātra, dripping water onto the liṅga all day. Śiva is pictured as never wishing to be dry.

Śivarātri. On Mahāśivarātri the liṅga is bathed in each of the four watches of the night, with milk, curd, ghee and honey in turn, while devotees keep vigil; see Mahāśivarātri.

Then the leaves. After the bath come vibhūti, sandal and bilva leaves, three-lobed like his three eyes; see Bilvāṣṭakam.

ZAPPY: A pot dripping all day? Doesn’t the priest get tired?

PROF. BOLT: The pot does the work, Zappy. That is the idea: a bath that never stops.

A bath that never stops.

IN THE VERSE

Namas te rudra manyave

ŚRĪ RUDRAM 1.1
namas te rudra manyavauto ta iṣave namaḥ |namas te astu dhanvanebāhubhyām uta te namaḥ ||
Salutation to your anger, O Rudra, and to your arrow; salutation to your bow, and to your two arms.
WordMeaning
namaḥsalutation
manyaveto your wrath
iṣaveto the arrow
dhanvaneto the bow
bāhubhyāmto the two arms

Calming as well as cooling. The Rudram opens by saluting Rudra’s anger and weapons, asking them to be gentle. Poured with water, the hymn pictures the abhiṣeka as soothing the fierce god into Śiva, “the auspicious one”. It is the verse heard first in countless Śaiva abhiṣekas.

Text: Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.5.1, as in this site’s Śrī Rudram, part 1.
The fierce god, soothed.

VIṢṆU: TIRUMAÑJANAM AND ALAṄKĀRA

The bath behind the curtain

The bath. In Vaiṣṇava temples the deity is bathed with the Puruṣa Sūkta and other hymns, often with a conch used to pour; Tamil Vaiṣṇavism calls the bath tirumañjanam. A śālagrāma stone at home is bathed the same way in small.

Then the adornment. The bath is followed by alaṅkāra: silks, crowns, jewels, flowers and sandal, which can take longer than the bath. Viṣṇu as king is the god of beauty and order, and the devotee comes for that beauty.

Weekly great baths. Many Vaiṣṇava temples set aside a day for a fuller abhiṣeka with pañcāmṛta, often Friday or Saturday; at Tirupati, the Friday abhiṣeka of Veṅkaṭeśvara is among the most sought-after sevās.

Kṛṣṇa’s birthday. At midnight on Janmāṣṭamī the child Kṛṣṇa is bathed in pañcāmṛta, rocked in a cradle and dressed, a birth bath for the newborn god; see Janmāṣṭamī.

ZAPPY: Why hide the bath?

PROF. BOLT: A king is not bathed in public, Zappy. You see him when he is ready to receive you.

Bathed in private, seen in splendour.

IN THE VERSE

Sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥ

PURUṢA SŪKTA 1
sahasraśīrṣā puruṣaḥsahasrākṣaḥ sahasrapāt |sa bhūmiṃ viśvato vṛtvāaty atiṣṭhad daśāṅgulam ||
The Person has a thousand heads, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet. Covering the earth on every side, he stood beyond it by ten finger-breadths.
WordMeaning
sahasraa thousand, countless
puruṣaḥthe cosmic Person
vṛtvācovering
daśāṅgulamten finger-breadths

Bathing the infinite. Vaiṣṇava abhiṣekas often use the Puruṣa Sūkta, one verse per offering. The paradox is deliberate: the devotee pours water over a small image while chanting that the Lord is larger than the whole earth. Love does not wait to be able to reach everywhere.

Text: Ṛgveda 10.90.1, as in this site’s Puruṣa Sūktam.
The vast one, bathed by hand.

DEVĪ: TURMERIC, KUṄKUMA AND A NEW SARI

Bathing the Mother

Colours of auspiciousness. For the Goddess the bath often uses turmeric water and kuṅkuma, the yellow and red that mark a married woman’s good fortune in South India. She is honoured as the auspicious Mother of the household.

The hymn. Lakṣmī is bathed with the Śrī Sūkta, the Ṛgveda appendix that asks Fire to bring her, golden and moon-bright; Durgā and Pārvatī with the Devī Sūkta and verses of the Devī Māhātmya.

Dressed as family. After the bath she receives a new sari, bangles, flowers and the mirror, the gifts a mother gives a daughter or a family gives its queen. At Navarātri the Goddess is dressed in a new form each day; see Navarātri.

Fridays. In many Karnataka homes Friday is the Goddess’s day: a small abhiṣeka for Lakṣmī with milk and turmeric, a lamp, and women of the house receiving turmeric and kuṅkuma in turn.

ZAPPY: Why does she get a mirror?

PROF. BOLT: Because a mother dressing her daughter shows her how lovely she looks, Zappy. The love goes both ways.

Bathed as family.

Inviting her into the water. Chanted over the bath, the Śrī Sūkta turns the abhiṣeka into an invitation: the golden goddess is asked to come and be present. The milk and turmeric poured are a welcome, as for a bride entering a house.

Text: Śrī Sūkta 1 (Ṛgveda khila), as in this site’s Aṣṭa Lakṣmī guide.
Golden Mother, be welcome.

RĀMA’S CORONATION

The abhiṣeka of a king

The story

From the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa (the coronation chapter near its end).

Home at last. After fourteen years of exile and the war in Laṅkā, Rāma returned to Ayodhyā, and Bharata handed back the kingdom he had held in trust.
Waters from everywhere. For the consecration, Sugrīva sent his fastest monkeys to fetch water from the four oceans and from the holy rivers, so that the whole land would be present in the bath.
The pouring. The sage Vasiṣṭha, with the other sages, seated Rāma and Sītā on a jewelled throne and poured the waters over them, as the priests, the ministers, the maidens and the citizens joined in.
Crowned. Then Rāma was crowned and adorned, and gave gifts to all; Hanumān received a pearl necklace from Sītā’s own hands.
The pattern. This is the template for every temple abhiṣeka: waters gathered from holy places, poured with hymns, followed by adornment and gifts. Karnataka homes set up the coronation scene in their Navarātri doll display; see Gombe Habba.

ZAPPY: So every abhiṣeka is a little coronation of Rāma?

PROF. BOLT: Of whichever god is in front of you, Zappy. Rāma’s just set the pattern.

Waters of the whole land, poured on a king.

KAṆṆAPPA’S WATER

A hunter’s abhiṣeka

The story

From the Periya Purāṇam (12th century), at Śrīkāḷahasti; see Śrīkāḷahasti.

A hunter finds Śiva. Tiṇṇan, a young hunter of the hills, came upon a liṅga in the forest and was overwhelmed with love for it, though he knew no rites at all.
His own abhiṣeka. Every day he brought water carried in his mouth and poured it over the liṅga, flowers tucked in his hair, and meat he had tasted to be sure it was good. A temple priest, Śivakōcariyār, found the shrine defiled each morning and was horrified.
The test. One day, as the priest watched in hiding, blood began to flow from one eye of the liṅga. Tiṇṇan tried every herb he knew; then he cut out his own eye and placed it on the liṅga, and the bleeding stopped.
The second eye. When the other eye began to bleed, he set his foot on the liṅga to mark the spot, so as not to miss it once he was blind, and raised the arrow to his second eye. Śiva’s hand came out of the liṅga and held his: “Stop, Kaṇṇappa.”
Love over rule. He became Kaṇṇappa, “the one who gave his eye”, one of the sixty-three Nāyanmārs. The story says what abhiṣeka is for: love, not the correctness of the materials.

ZAPPY: Water from his mouth? That breaks every rule!

PROF. BOLT: Every rule, Zappy, and Śiva stopped the priest’s horror with one word. Love outranked the manual.

Love outranks the manual.

GREAT ABHIṢEKAS IN KARNATAKA

From Śravaṇabeḷagoḷa to the home shrine

Karnataka’s abhiṣekas range from a colossus on a hill to a spoon of milk on a family stone.

🗿 Bāhubali

The Jain Mahāmastakābhiṣeka, the “great head-anointing” of the colossal Bāhubali at Śravaṇabeḷagoḷa, about every twelve years: milk, turmeric, sandal and flowers poured from a scaffold. Dharmasthaḷa’s Bāhubali receives the same; see Dharmasthaḷa.

🌙 Śivarātri nights

All-night abhiṣekas at Śiva temples across the state, from Gōkarṇa to Nañjanagūḍu.

🐍 Kukke

Abhiṣeka of Subrahmaṇya with pañcāmṛta, much sought by families.

🛕 Uḍupi

The daily bath of Kṛṣṇa by the presiding swāmi of the Paryāya.

👑 Melkote

Celuvanārāyaṇa’s great baths at the annual festival; see Melkote.

🏠 Home

A small śālagrāma, liṅga or Lakṣmī bathed on Fridays and festival days.

ZAPPY: A Jain statue in a Hindu article?

PROF. BOLT: The rite is shared, Zappy. Karnataka’s most famous abhiṣeka is Jain, and Hindu neighbours come to watch.

The state’s great baths.

ABHIṢEKA AT HOME

A simple bath for a small image

What you need. A small metal image or stone (a liṅga, śālagrāma, Gaṇeśa or Lakṣmī), a plate to catch the water, a small vessel, clean water, and if you wish, a little milk or the five nectars.

How. Say the deity’s name or a short hymn, pour water slowly, then milk or pañcāmṛta, then water again. Wipe gently with a clean cloth, apply sandal or kuṅkuma, offer a flower and light the lamp. The full sequence is in A Simple Home Pūjā.

The water afterwards. The bath water becomes tīrtha: a few drops are sipped and sprinkled on the head by the family, and the rest is poured at the foot of a tulasī or a tree, never down a drain. See Tulasī.

Large images. Painted pictures and large clay or wooden images are not bathed; a few drops of water are offered or sprinkled instead. Abhiṣeka is for images made to be bathed.

ZAPPY: Can I bathe a photo of God?

PROF. BOLT: Sprinkle a drop of water and offer a flower, Zappy. Photos don’t like baths.

A spoon of milk, a lot of love.

WHAT THE BATH GIVES BACK

Tīrtha, cleansing and calm

Tīrtha. The water that has bathed the deity is given back to devotees as tīrtha, “holy water”: a spoonful in the right palm, sipped and touched to the eyes and head. The god’s bath becomes the devotee’s purification.

Cooling the devotee. Teachers read the Śiva abhiṣeka inwardly too: as the liṅga is cooled, the worshipper’s own anger and heat are cooled. Watching a slow abhiṣeka is famously calming. (Interpretation.)

Cleansing the image, renewing the presence. Practically, the bath cleans the image of yesterday’s flowers and oil; ritually, the āgamas treat it as renewing the deity’s presence in the image, which is the subject of What Makes a Mūrti Sacred?.

A pattern for life. Bathe, adorn, offer, share: the sequence of abhiṣeka, alaṅkāra and prasāda is also a picture of how to treat anyone we love. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: So the water goes from God to me?

PROF. BOLT: After it has been near him, Zappy. That is why you sip it so carefully.

The bath comes back as blessing.
