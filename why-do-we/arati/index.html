Why Do We Do Ārati?

The Wave of Light · explained and illustrated

Light given, light returned

HOW THIS WORKS: Why we do ārati: the name, its place in pūjā, the lamps and camphor, how the lamp is waved and received, ārati for people, the songs and the meanings.

WHERE WE ARE

At the end of almost every Hindu worship, at home or in a temple, someone lifts a lamp and waves it in circles before the god while a bell rings and people sing. This is ārati. Then the plate comes round, and everyone passes their palms over the flame and touches their eyes.

This first part of Why Do We… explains the name, where ārati sits in pūjā, what is waved and how, why camphor, what the gesture of receiving means, the ārati done for people, the songs and the times of day, and the readings teachers give it.

Ārati at home is part of Home Pūjā. Next in this series: tilaka.

With the blessings of the Lord,
srini.presents 🙏

ZAPPY: Is ārati the most important part of pūjā?

PROF. BOLT: It is the most visible. Every part matters; ārati is where the whole room joins in.

ZAPPY: Why at the end?

PROF. BOLT: The god has been bathed, dressed and fed. Now we look at him in the light.

Light waved, god seen.

THE NAME

Ārātrika and nīrājana

Ārātrika. Ārati comes from the Sanskrit ārātrika. It is commonly explained through rātri, “night”: light that drives away darkness. (Common explanation; etymologies differ.)

Nīrājana. The formal Sanskrit name in pūjā manuals is nīrājana, the waving of lights before the deity.

Regional names. In Kannaḍa homes it is often maṅgaḷārati, the auspicious ārati.

One word, many forms. The name covers a single camphor flame at home and a great temple ārati with many lamps.

Both meanings. Light offered to the god, and light by which the god is seen.

ZAPPY: So ārati means “against the night”?

PROF. BOLT: That is the usual explanation. Whatever the root, the act is plain: bring light.

Light against the dark.

IN THE PŪJĀ

Where ārati comes

Upacāras. Pūjā is a set of services (upacāras) offered to the god as to an honoured guest: welcome, seat, water, bath, clothes, sandal, flowers, incense, lamp, food.

Sixteen. The fullest common form has sixteen, the ṣoḍaśopacāra pūjā; shorter forms have five.

After food. Nīrājana usually follows the food offering (naivedya), near the close.

Dīpa and nīrājana. The lamp (dīpa) shown earlier is a single offering; nīrājana is the waving that crowns the pūjā.

Lists differ. The order and number vary between manuals and traditions.

ZAPPY: Lamp twice?

PROF. BOLT: First shown, then waved. One is courtesy, the other celebration.

The crown of the pūjā.

WHAT IS WAVED

Lamps and more

What is waved depends on the home, the temple and the occasion.

🪔 Ghee lamp

One wick or many; ghee is preferred, oil also used.

✋ Pañcārati

Five wicks, often linked to the five elements. (Interpretation.)

⚪ Camphor

Karpūra, a clean white flame.

🛕 Many-tiered lamps

Temples use lamps of many levels, shaped like serpents, pots or trees.

🐚 Not only fire

In full temple nīrājana, water in a conch, cloth, flowers and a whisk may also be waved.

🔔 With it

Bell, conch and song.

Light first, then the rest.

HOW IT IS WAVED

Circles before the god

Clockwise. The lamp moves in clockwise circles, the auspicious direction.

A common pattern. A widely taught pattern waves the lamp at the feet, then the navel, then the face, then over the whole form, with a set number of turns for each. (Practices differ.)

Right hand. The lamp is held in the right hand; the left often rings the bell.

Steady. The movement is slow and full, so the god’s form is lit part by part.

Close. The plate is then set down or carried to the devotees.

ZAPPY: What if I wave anticlockwise?

PROF. BOLT: The god will forgive you. The tradition says clockwise; learn it.

Feet, heart, face, all of him.

RECEIVING THE LIGHT

Palms over the flame

The gesture. When the plate comes round, people pass their cupped hands over the flame and bring them to their eyes and head.

Taking the blessing. The light has touched the god; the hands carry it back to the devotee.

An offering in the plate. Many put a coin or note in the ārati plate; it goes to the priest or the temple.

Only the hands. No one blows out an ārati flame; it is allowed to burn out or is put out by hand.

For the sick. Families often carry the blessing home to those who could not come.

ZAPPY: Why the eyes?

PROF. BOLT: The eyes have just seen the god. Touch them with his light.

Take the light home in your hands.

WHY CAMPHOR

A flame that leaves nothing

Bright and clean. Camphor (karpūra) burns with a bright white flame and a fragrance.

No residue. It burns away almost completely, leaving no ash.

The reading. Teachers often say: so should the ego burn away before God, leaving nothing behind. (Interpretation.)

Practical. Easy to light, needs no wick, quick to finish at the end of worship.

Pure camphor. Many prefer edible-grade camphor (pacca karpūra) for worship, as used in temple sweets.

ZAPPY: So the camphor is me?

PROF. BOLT: Your pride, says the teacher. Watch it go.

Burn and leave no ash.

LIGHT IN THE SANCTUM

Seeing the god

Dark by design. The inner sanctum (garbhagṛha) of a temple is small and dark, lit only by lamps.

Darśana. Worship centres on darśana, seeing the deity and being seen. In the dark, the waved lamp shows the image part by part.

A plain origin. This is a likely practical root of ārati: to let worshippers see the god. (Common explanation.)

The moment. In great temples the curtain opens for ārati and the crowd sees the god lit up all at once.

See more. How a temple is laid out: How to read a Hindu temple.

ZAPPY: Couldn’t they just add windows?

PROF. BOLT: Then it would be a hall. The dark makes the light mean something.

In the dark, light shows the god.

ĀRATI FOR PEOPLE

Brides, guests and children

Not only gods. Ārati is also done for people at important moments.

Red water. In Karnataka and much of the south, women wave a plate of water reddened with turmeric and lime (or kuṅkuma), sometimes with a lamp, before a bride and groom, a new mother and baby, or someone returning from a journey.

Dṛṣṭi. It is said to ward off the evil eye; the water is then thrown away outside. (Popular belief.)

Festivals. Children get ārati on birthdays and festivals in many homes.

Respect. A great guest or teacher may be welcomed with ārati.

ZAPPY: Why throw the water away?

PROF. BOLT: Whatever bad it caught is thrown out with it, so the saying goes.

Light for the honoured guest.

THE DAY’S ĀRATIS

From waking to sleep

Temple schedules differ; these are the common points of the day.

🌅 Maṅgaḷārati

At dawn, waking the god; in many temples the first ārati of the day.

☀️ Midday

After the noon food offering.

🌇 Sandhyā ārati

At dusk, when the lamps are lit; the most attended at home.

🌙 Śayana ārati

At night, before the god rests.

🎉 Festivals

Extra āratis on festival days and processions.

🏠 At home

Most homes keep at least the evening ārati.

Light through the day.

THE SONGS

Singing while the lamp turns

Ārati songs. Each region and deity has ārati songs; families learn them by heart.

Om Jaya Jagadīśa Hare. The best known across north India was composed in the 19th century by Śraddhārām Phillauri.

In Karnataka. Kannaḍa homes sing maṅgaḷārati songs, many from the Haridāsa tradition.

Sanskrit. Temples often recite Sanskrit verses at nīrājana; some sing Karpūragauraṃ for Śiva.

Sound matters. Bell, conch and clapping fill the room; see why we ring the bell.

ZAPPY: Do I need to know the songs?

PROF. BOLT: Clap and listen. You will know them by the tenth time.

Everyone sings at ārati.

AN EVENING ĀRATI

Ammu learns to wave the lamp

The story

An evening at home (an illustrative story).

Ajji lights the camphor and gives the plate to Ammu: “Slowly. Round, like the clock.”
Ammu waves it at the feet of the little Kṛṣṇa, then his face, then all of him, while her brother rings the bell.
Then everyone holds out their hands. “Now you have given him light,” says Ajji, “and he gives it back.”

WHAT IT MEANS

Readings of the teachers

Seeing. Ārati lets us see the god clearly, at least for a moment. (Common teaching.)

Returning light. The sun and fire are the god’s light; we offer a little of it back. (Interpretation.)

All of the god. Lighting each part in turn teaches attention: feet, heart, face, whole. (Interpretation.)

Ego as camphor. The flame that leaves no ash is the self that leaves no pride. (Interpretation.)

Together. Ārati turns private worship into a shared moment: everyone sees, sings and receives.

ZAPPY: Which meaning is right?

PROF. BOLT: All of them help. Pick the one that keeps your hands steady.

See, offer, receive.

COMMON QUESTIONS

What tradition says and does not say

Must I have a priest? No. Anyone may do ārati at home.

Ghee or oil? Ghee is preferred in most traditions; oil is acceptable.

Can I blow it out? Custom says no; let it burn out or snuff it with the hand or a leaf.

Electric lamps? Some temples use them for light, but ārati itself is done with a flame.

Women during periods? Customs differ between families and communities; this site does not prescribe.

ZAPPY: So there are no strict rules?

PROF. BOLT: There are customs. Learn your family’s, respect the temple’s.

Custom, not fear.
