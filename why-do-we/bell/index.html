Why Do We Ring the Bell?

The Sound Before the God · explained and illustrated

The first offering is sound

HOW THIS WORKS: Why we ring the bell: the ghaṇṭā and its metal, the doorway and hand bells, the emblems on the handle, when it rings and why, the sound itself and the vow bells.

WHERE WE ARE

Walk into a temple and the first thing many people do is reach up and ring the great bell at the door. In the pūjā at home, the left hand rings a small bell while the right waves the lamp. The ghaṇṭā is one of the oldest objects of worship, and its sound is the first offering.

This part explains the bell’s name and metal, the bells of the doorway and of the hand, the emblems on the handle, when it is rung and why, what is said about sound driving away evil and calling the gods, the vow bells, and the teachers’ readings.

Previous: the coconut. Next: pradakṣiṇā. The bell rings at ārati.

NAME AND METAL

Ghaṇṭā and bell metal

Ghaṇṭā. The Sanskrit word for bell; Kannaḍa gaṇṭe, Tamiḻ maṇi.

Bell metal. Temple bells are cast in bronze (kaṃsa), an alloy of copper and tin, prized for a long, clear ring.

Pañcaloha. Some are cast in pañcaloha, a five-metal alloy used for icons. (As described.)

Craft. Families of bell-founders in many regions keep the old casting methods.

Old. Bells appear across Hindu, Buddhist and Jain worship.

ZAPPY: Does the metal matter?

PROF. BOLT: Ring a tin can and a bronze bell. You will hear the answer.

A metal that sings.

THE DOORWAY BELL

Announcing yourself

At the entrance. Most temples hang one or more large bells at the door or in front of the sanctum.

Announcing. Ringing it is like knocking: the devotee announces his arrival.

Turning inward. Teachers say the sound also clears the mind of outside thoughts as you enter. (Interpretation.)

Gently. One or a few rings; not a contest.

Leaving. Some ring again on leaving; others do not. (Customs differ.)

ZAPPY: Can kids ring it?

PROF. BOLT: They love to. Lift them up, one ring each.

Knock on the god’s door.

THE HAND BELL

The bell of the pūjā

Small bell. Home and temple pūjās use a hand bell, held by the handle.

Left hand. It is commonly rung with the left hand, while the right hand offers.

At key moments. It rings during the bath (abhiṣeka), incense, lamp, food offering and ārati.

Steady. A steady, unbroken ring is preferred to loud jangling. (Common teaching.)

Kept clean. The bell is part of the pūjā set, cleaned with the lamps.

ZAPPY: How long should it ring?

PROF. BOLT: Through the offering. When the offering stops, the bell stops.

The bell keeps the pūjā’s time.

THE HANDLE

Who sits on top

The figure on the handle usually matches the deity worshipped.

🐂 Nandi

For Śiva worship, the bull Nandi sits on the handle.

🦅 Garuḍa

For Viṣṇu worship, Garuḍa; sometimes Hanumān.

🐚 Conch and discus

Viṣṇu’s emblems on some bells.

🔱 Trident

Some Śiva and Devī bells carry the trident.

🔔 Plain

Many household bells are plain.

🛕 Matching

Families keep the bell of their own deity.

The god’s mount rides the bell.

WHY RING IT

Driving away, inviting in

A common verse. A verse widely recited while ringing the bell says it is rung so that the gods may come and the demons may go. (Paraphrase of a common pūjā verse.)

Clearing. The sound is believed to clear the place of inauspicious forces.

Inviting. It welcomes the divine presence into the worship.

Covering noise. Practically, it drowns out other sounds, so the worshipper is not distracted.

The god as listener. Pūjā offers to all senses: sight (lamp), smell (incense), taste (food), touch (flowers) and hearing (bell).

ZAPPY: So the bell is a guard?

PROF. BOLT: A doorman for the gods. Friends in, troublemakers out.

Gods in, trouble out.

THE SOUND

Resonance and attention

Nāda. Indian thought gives sound a high place: creation itself is linked with sound, and Oṃ with the first sound. (Teaching.)

Resonance. A good bell’s ring fades slowly, like the hum at the end of Oṃ; teachers compare them. (Interpretation.)

Attention. Following the fading ring draws the mind into the present moment. (Common teaching.)

Not science claims. Popular claims that bells “balance the brain” are not established; we leave them aside.

Simple. The plain point: it is a beautiful sound offered to God.

ZAPPY: Does it really calm the mind?

PROF. BOLT: Try it: ring once and listen until it is gone. Then tell me.

Listen till it fades.

WHEN IT IS RUNG

Moments of the pūjā

Practice varies; these are the moments the bell is most often heard.

💧 Abhiṣeka

While the deity is bathed.

🌫️ Dhūpa

While incense is offered.

🪔 Dīpa

While the lamp is shown.

🍚 Naivedya

While the food is offered, often with the curtain drawn.

🔥 Ārati

Throughout the waving of lights.

🚪 Entering

At the temple door.

The bell marks each offering.

VOW BELLS

Thousands of bells

Offered bells. At some shrines devotees tie or hang bells after a wish is granted.

Golu Devatā. The shrines of Golu Devatā in Uttarākhaṇḍ are famous for their tens of thousands of bells.

Temple gifts. Families donate large bells to temples, often inscribed with their names.

Old bells. Some temple bells are centuries old and treasured.

Elsewhere. Bells are offered in Buddhist and Jain shrines too.

ZAPPY: Thousands of bells? Isn’t it noisy?

PROF. BOLT: When the wind blows, the hill sings.

Wishes that ring.

THE QUIET BELL

Arjun and the doorway bell

The story

A temple visit (an illustrative story).

Arjun swings the temple bell again and again. Clang, clang, clang.
Ajji takes his hand. “Once. Then listen until it is gone.”
He rings once. The sound fades and fades. When it is gone, the temple is very quiet, and so is Arjun.
