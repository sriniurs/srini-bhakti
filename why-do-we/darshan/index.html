What Is Darśan?

Seeing and Being Seen · explained and illustrated

Seeing and being seen

HOW THIS WORKS: The central act of Hindu worship: the word darśan, the deity’s eyes, the curtain and the threshold, Arjuna’s divine eye and the verse that says love sees, Nandanār and the moving Nandi, darśan beyond the temple, manners, and Karnataka’s great darśans.

WHERE WE ARE

The temple bell rings, the priest draws back the curtain, the camphor flame lifts, and for a few seconds a crowd that was pushing and chatting goes completely still. Everyone is looking at the same small, lamp-lit face in the dark. That moment is darśan.

Darśan means seeing, but in Hindu worship it is seeing both ways: the devotee sees the deity, and the deity sees the devotee. This part explains the word, the eyes of the image, the curtain and the threshold, the Gītā’s chapter of the great vision and the verse that says love alone makes God visible, the story of Nandanār and the moving Nandi, darśan of saints, rivers and mountains, the times of day, temple manners, and Karnataka’s great darśans.

The previous part was Who Is Your Iṣṭa Devatā?; the next is Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa. For the architecture that leads up to this moment, read How to Read a Hindu Temple; for how the image itself is made sacred, What Makes a Mūrti Sacred?.

Seeing, and being seen.

THE WORD

Dṛś: to see

The root. Darśana comes from dṛś, “to see”. In everyday Hindi and Kannada one “takes” darśan, and a deity, a saint or even a great person “gives” it. The grammar already says that the seeing is a gift from the other side.

Seeing and being seen. The historian of religion Diana Eck, in her book on the subject, called seeing the central act of Hindu worship: the devotee comes to behold the image and to be beheld by it. The exchange of glances, not a sermon, is the heart of a temple visit.

A philosophy is also a darśana. The same word names the six schools of Indian philosophy, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta: each is a “view”, a way of seeing reality. Worship and philosophy share the vocabulary of sight.

Not only eyes. Darśan uses the whole body: the bell for the ears, incense and flowers for the nose, the flame for the hands, the prasāda for the tongue. But the eyes lead, and the rest follows.

ZAPPY: So when Grandma says “I had good darśan”, she means a good look?

PROF. BOLT: A good meeting, Zappy. She saw him and felt seen.

A gift of sight.

THE EYES OF THE DEITY

Opened, painted, unblinking

Opening the eyes. An image becomes a living presence through consecration, and one of its central rites is the opening of the eyes, netronmīlana: the eyes are ritually opened, often with a gold needle and ghee, and the first gaze of the deity is directed at something auspicious. The full rite is explained in What Makes a Mūrti Sacred?.

Eyes that never close. Because the deity’s gaze is the blessing, temples make the eyes vivid: painted on a stone liṅga for a festival, set in silver or gold on the Goddess, enlarged on the great wooden form of Jagannātha at Purī. Mīnākṣī of Madurai is “the fish-eyed one”: like a fish, she never closes her eyes on her devotees.

Facing the devotee. The sanctum is built so that the deity looks straight down the axis of the temple, through the halls and out of the door. The worshipper walking in is walking into that gaze.

Nandi’s line. In Śiva temples the bull Nandi sits on that axis, eyes fixed on the liṅga. Many devotees look at Śiva between Nandi’s horns, joining the gaze of the most devoted of watchers.

ZAPPY: Is the stone really looking at me?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition says the Lord is, through the image, Zappy. That is why the eyes are opened with such care.

The god looks back.

THE CURTAIN AND THE THRESHOLD

The moment of darśan

The closed curtain. Many sanctums are curtained or doored during the deity’s bath, dressing and meal. The god is being cared for in private, as a king or a beloved guest would be. Devotees wait outside, sometimes singing.

The opening. Then the bells ring, the curtain is drawn back, and the priest raises the camphor or ghee lamps in circles before the image. That flame lights up the face for the crowd; for many it is the single most intense moment of a visit. See Why Do We Do Ārati?.

At the threshold. Most devotees stand just outside the sanctum doorway, the garbhagṛha, the “womb-house”. Only the priest enters. The threshold is where the human world stops and the divine house begins; people touch it, bow over it, and step back.

A few seconds. In great temples the queue moves fast and a darśan may last only seconds. Devotees say that is enough: the point is not to examine the image but to receive its glance. Many close their eyes after seeing, to hold the image inside.

ZAPPY: Why close your eyes after waiting so long to see?

PROF. BOLT: To keep it, Zappy. You look, then you shut the door so it doesn’t get out.

Curtain, flame, a glance.

IN THE VERSE

Divyaṃ dadāmi te cakṣuḥ

GĪTĀ 11.8 · A DIVINE EYE
na tu māṃ śakyase draṣṭumanenaiva svacakṣuṣā |divyaṃ dadāmi te cakṣuḥpaśya me yogam aiśvaram ||
But you cannot see Me with these eyes of yours. I give you a divine eye: behold My lordly power.
WordMeaning
draṣṭumto see
sva-cakṣuṣāwith your own eye
divyam cakṣuḥa divine eye
paśyabehold
yogam aiśvaramlordly power

Seeing needs a gift. Arjuna has asked to see Kṛṣṇa’s cosmic form. Kṛṣṇa answers that ordinary eyes cannot do it, and gives him a divine eye. The grammar of “giving darśan” starts here: the vision of God is something God grants.

Text: the Gītā Press text as in this site’s Gītā, chapter 11.
God gives the eye.

THE GREAT VISION

Arjuna sees the cosmic form

The story

From the Bhagavad Gītā, chapter 11. The full chapter is in this site’s Gītā, chapter 11.

The request. After hearing Kṛṣṇa describe his glories, Arjuna asks: if you think I can bear it, show me your imperishable form.
The divine eye. Kṛṣṇa gives him a divine eye, and Sañjaya, narrating to the blind king, describes what Arjuna sees: a form with countless faces, eyes and arms, brighter than a thousand suns rising at once.
Terror. Arjuna sees the whole universe in that body, and then sees the warriors of both armies rushing into its mouths. Trembling, he praises Kṛṣṇa and begs forgiveness for having treated him as a friend.
Back to gentleness. At Arjuna’s request, Kṛṣṇa takes back his four-armed and then his gentle human form, and Arjuna breathes again.
The lesson. Kṛṣṇa ends by saying that this form cannot be seen through Vedas, penance, gifts or sacrifice, but by devotion alone (11.53–54). The greatest darśan in the scriptures closes with love as its key.

ZAPPY: If seeing God is so frightening, why do we want darśan?

PROF. BOLT: Because in the temple, Zappy, he shows the gentle face, the one Arjuna asked to have back.

The vast form, and the gentle one.

IN THE VERSE

Bhaktyā tv ananyayā śakyaḥ

GĪTĀ 11.54 · LOVE SEES
bhaktyā tv ananyayā śakyaaham evaṃvidho ’rjuna |jñātuṃ draṣṭuṃ ca tattvenapraveṣṭuṃ ca paraṃtapa ||
But by single-minded devotion, Arjuna, I can be known in this way, seen in truth, and entered into, O scorcher of foes.
WordMeaning
bhaktyāby devotion
ananyayāundivided, with no other
jñātumto be known
draṣṭumto be seen
praveṣṭumto be entered

Three steps. Know, see, enter. Darśan is the middle step: after knowing about God and before becoming one with him. The verse puts seeing firmly inside bhakti, and says its key is love that has no other object.

Text: the Gītā Press text as in this site’s Gītā, chapter 11.
Love is the eye.

NANDANĀR AND THE MOVING NANDI

A devotee kept at the gate

The story

From the Periya Purāṇam of Cēkkiḻār (12th century), the lives of the sixty-three Nāyanmārs; Nandanār is usually dated to the 7th or 8th century.

A labourer and singer. Nandanār, born into a community of farm labourers in the Cōḻa village of Ātanūr, was a devotee of Śiva who supplied leather for temple drums and strings for temple harps.
Outside the gate. Because of his birth he was not allowed inside the temples. At Tiruppuṅkūr he stood at the gate, longing to see the liṅga of Śiva, the Lord of Śiva’s world.
The stone bull. But the great stone Nandi sat in the middle of the axis between the gate and the sanctum, and blocked the view.
Śiva speaks. The Periya Purāṇam says Śiva himself ordered Nandi to move aside, and the bull shifted. Nandanār saw his Lord and sang with joy, then dug a tank for the temple before going home.
Still to one side. The Nandi at Tiruppuṅkūr is still set off the axis, and devotees point to it as the story’s witness. Nandanār went on to his great darśan at Cidambaram; see Cidambaram.

ZAPPY: Even Nandi had to make room?

PROF. BOLT: Even Nandi, Zappy. Nobody stands between a devotee and darśan when the god decides.

Make room for the devotee.

DARŚAN BEYOND THE TEMPLE

Saints, rivers, mountains, processions

Saints and gurus. People travel long distances simply for the darśan of a holy person: to sit in their presence, see them and be seen. A Jagadguru’s procession through a town is greeted exactly as a deity’s is.

Rivers and mountains. Some holy places are darśans in themselves. The Purāṇas say the Narmadā purifies by sight alone; pilgrims to Kailāsa walk round the mountain for its darśan. See Sacred Rivers of India.

When the god comes out. At festivals the deity leaves the sanctum on a chariot or palanquin so that everyone, including those who cannot or may not enter, can have darśan in the street. The great car festivals exist for that.

Every face. The Gītā says that the one who sees Kṛṣṇa everywhere, and everything in him, is never lost to him (6.30). The last darśan, the teachers say, is to see God in every face. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: So darśan of a person is allowed?

PROF. BOLT: Of a holy one, Zappy, yes. And the Gītā says, of every one, eventually.

Darśan comes out to meet you.

IN THE VERSE

Yo māṃ paśyati sarvatra

GĪTĀ 6.30 · SEEING EVERYWHERE
yo māṃ paśyati sarvatrasarvaṃ ca mayi paśyati |tasyāhaṃ na praṇaśyāmisa ca me na praṇaśyati ||
Whoever sees Me everywhere and sees everything in Me, I am never lost to him, nor is he lost to Me.
WordMeaning
paśyatisees
sarvatraeverywhere
mayiin Me
na praṇaśyāmiI am not lost

Darśan without a temple. The temple trains the eye on one image; this verse says where the training leads: to seeing the same presence everywhere. The two meet in the devotee who bows to the image and also to the person standing beside him in the queue.

Text: the Gītā Press text as in this site’s Gītā, chapter 6.
See Him in everything.

TIMES OF DARŚAN

From the first glance at dawn to the last at night

Waking the god. The day begins with the suprabhātam, the dawn hymn that wakes the deity, and the first darśan of the morning. Some temples bring a cow and calf before the sanctum so that the deity’s first sight is auspicious; at others the first darśan shows the god still wearing the previous night’s flowers.

Bath and dress. Through the morning the deity is bathed (abhiṣeka) and dressed (alaṅkāra). Some devotees love the plain darśan right after the bath; others wait for the full adornment. Abhiṣeka is explained in Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka?.

Rare darśans. Some forms are seen only on special days: a deity’s original form without ornaments, or a crown worn once a year. These draw huge crowds precisely because they are rare.

Closing. The day ends with the last ārati and a lullaby or a final lamp before the sanctum closes. Devotees who come for that last darśan say goodnight to the god as to a family elder.

ZAPPY: So the god keeps a timetable?

PROF. BOLT: Like a beloved elder in a big house, Zappy. Waking, bathing, eating, resting, receiving visitors.

A day with the god.

TEMPLE MANNERS FOR DARŚAN

Six simple courtesies

Customs vary from temple to temple; these are the common courtesies most observe.

👟 Clean and simple

Shoes outside, a wash of hands and feet, simple clothes. The house belongs to someone.

↔️ Stand to the side

Many temples ask devotees not to stand straight in front of the sanctum door, especially in Śiva temples, so as not to block Nandi’s view or the deity’s gaze.

🙏 Joined palms, open eyes

Look first, then pray. Darśan is for seeing; closing the eyes before seeing misses the point.

🤫 Quiet

Let the moment be quiet for everyone. Phones away; many temples forbid photography of the sanctum.

🔙 Don’t turn your back hastily

Step back a little before turning, as one would from an elder.

🪔 Receive what is given

Take the flame’s warmth with both hands, the tīrtha and prasāda with the right hand. See Prasāda.

ZAPPY: Is there a wrong way to do darśan?

PROF. BOLT: Rushing, pushing and filming, Zappy. Almost everything else is forgiven.

Courtesy before a host.

GREAT DARŚANS OF KARNATAKA

Six places and moments

A few darśans that Karnataka families plan their year around.

🪟 Uḍupi

Kṛṣṇa is seen through the latticed window on the west, the window linked with Kanaka Dāsa; see What Is Bhakti? and Uḍupi.

👑 Melkote

Once a year Celuvanārāyaṇa wears the Vairamuḍi diamond crown in a night procession, and crowds gather for that one darśan; see Melkote.

🐘 Mysuru Dasarā

Cāmuṇḍeśvarī rides through Mysuru in a golden howdah on an elephant, and the whole city takes her darśan in the street.

⛰️ Cāmuṇḍī Hills

The daily darśan of the Nāḍa Devī on her hill; see Cāmuṇḍeśvarī.

🌊 Śṛṅgeri

The darśan of Śāradā and of the Jagadguru, on the bank of the Tuṅgā.

🐍 Kukke

Subrahmaṇya’s darśan, seen across the Garuḍa pillar.

ZAPPY: Which is the best?

PROF. BOLT: The one you go back to, Zappy.

Karnataka’s windows on God.

THE INNER DARŚAN

Holding the image within

Taking it home. Many devotees try to fix the image in memory during darśan and recall it later in meditation: the face, the ornaments, the flame. The outer darśan feeds an inner one.

Dhyāna ślokas. Hymns often begin with a dhyāna śloka, a verse describing the deity’s form in detail, so that the reciter can build the image in the mind before praying. It is darśan by description.

Seeing within. The Upaniṣads and the yogis speak of seeing the Lord in the cave of the heart. The temple image and the inner vision are, for most traditions, two ends of the same practice. (Interpretation.)

At home. The home shrine gives a daily darśan without a queue. Lighting the lamp and simply looking at the image for a minute is darśan as much as a pilgrimage; see A Simple Home Pūjā.

ZAPPY: Can I do darśan with my eyes closed?

PROF. BOLT: After you have done it with them open, Zappy. First the temple, then the heart.

From the eyes to the heart.

WHAT DARŚAN IS NOT

Three mix-ups

Not a photograph. A darśan is a meeting, not an image to capture. Many temples ban photography of the sanctum for that reason, and devotees who have waited hours rarely want a picture instead of a glance.

Not a race. The fastest queue is not the best darśan. In crowded temples a few seconds are all one gets; the tradition says they are enough if the heart is ready.

Not sightseeing. Admiring the sculpture is good; How to Read a Hindu Temple teaches how. But darśan is not looking at art. It is the moment when the art falls away and someone looks back.

ZAPPY: So I should look, not snap?

PROF. BOLT: Look, Zappy. Your memory keeps better pictures than your phone.

A meeting, not a picture.
