Who Is Your Iṣṭa Devatā?

The Form You Hold Dear · explained and illustrated

The form you hold dear

HOW THIS WORKS: The cherished form of God: the word iṣṭa devatā and Yoga Sūtra 2.44, one truth and many names, how an iṣṭa comes to you, family, village and chosen deities, the five-deity shrine, Tulasīdās and Harihara, and Karnataka’s iṣṭaliṅga.

WHERE WE ARE

Walk into ten Hindu homes and you will find ten different shrines. One has Veṅkaṭeśvara in the middle, another Gaṇeśa, another the Goddess, another a small liṅga, another a picture of a guru. Ask the family why, and the answer is usually not a theory but a story: our family has always gone to Tirupati; my grandmother prayed to Cāmuṇḍī; I felt something the first time I saw Kṛṣṇa at Uḍupi.

That chosen form is the iṣṭa devatā, “the deity one holds dear”. This part explains the word and its root in the Yoga Sūtra, the Veda’s “one truth, many names”, how an iṣṭa comes to a person, the difference between a family, village and chosen deity, the five-deity shrine, two stories, Karnataka’s own forms of it including the iṣṭaliṅga, and how to find your own.

The previous part was What Is Bhakti?; the next is What Is Darśan?. Every deity named here has its own guide in the Gods and Goddesses collection.

THE WORD

Iṣṭa: wished for, cherished, chosen

Iṣṭa. The word comes from the root iṣ, “to wish, to desire”. Iṣṭa means what is wished for, dear, chosen. An iṣṭa devatā is the form of God a person is drawn to, worships most closely and turns to first.

A face for the formless. Hindu thought holds that the highest reality is beyond all form. But love needs a face. The iṣṭa is the face through which a person meets the formless: Viṣṇu, Śiva, the Goddess, Gaṇeśa, Murugan, Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Hanumān, or a guru seen as divine.

Three kinds. Indian homes often speak of three deities at once: the kula devatā, the family’s deity handed down; the grāma devatā, the protecting mother of the village; and the iṣṭa devatā, the person’s own chosen form. They may be one god or three different ones.

Not a rank. Choosing an iṣṭa does not mean the others are smaller. It means this is the door one walks through. The rest of the house is still the same house.

ZAPPY: So iṣṭa just means favourite?

PROF. BOLT: Favourite is close, Zappy, but deeper: it is the form your heart opens to without being told.

The door you walk through.

IN THE VERSE

Svādhyāyād iṣṭa-devatā-saṃprayogaḥ

YOGA SŪTRA 2.44 · STUDY
svādhyāyādiṣṭa-devatā-saṃprayogaḥ ||
From study and recitation comes union with one’s chosen deity.
WordMeaning
svādhyāyātfrom one’s own study, recitation
iṣṭachosen, cherished
devatādeity
saṃprayogaḥunion, close contact

The oldest use. Patañjali’s Yoga Sūtra lists svādhyāya, study of scripture and repetition of sacred words, among the observances, and this sūtra names its fruit: closeness to one’s iṣṭa devatā. The commentators read it as study and recitation that bring the chosen form near. The word iṣṭa devatā is here already, assumed and unexplained.

Text: Yoga Sūtra 2.44 as in the Bhoja Vṛtti and Vyāsa Bhāṣya editions (wisdomlib.org), transliterated into IAST.
Study brings the chosen one near.

ONE TRUTH, MANY FORMS

Why choosing one does not deny the others

The Vedic sentence. The Ṛgveda says that the inspired poets call the one reality by many names: Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Agni (1.164.46). The verse is explained on its own page in The Vedic Gods. Here it gives the principle behind every iṣṭa: one truth, many names.

Kṛṣṇa’s promise. In the Gītā Kṛṣṇa says that whatever form a devotee wishes to worship with faith, he himself makes that faith firm (7.21). The faith is real; the form is the devotee’s choice; the giver of the faith is one.

Even other gods. The Gītā goes further: those who worship other deities with faith also worship Him, though not in the prescribed way (9.23). Different schools read the line differently; all agree it does not make the devotee of another form an enemy.

The sectarian view. It is fair to add that each great tradition, Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Śākta and others, holds its own deity supreme and sees the rest as part of it. This library describes each on its own terms and does not rank them; see The Sampradāyas.

ZAPPY: If they all reach the same God, why do people argue?

PROF. BOLT: People argue about everything they love, Zappy. The texts are calmer than the arguments.

One sun, many windows.

IN THE VERSE

Yo yo yāṃ yāṃ tanuṃ bhaktaḥ

GĪTĀ 7.21 · FIRM FAITH
yo yo yāṃ yāṃ tanuṃ bhaktaḥśraddhayārcitum icchati |tasya tasyācalāṃ śraddhāṃtām eva vidadhāmy aham ||
Whatever form any devotee wishes to worship with faith, that very faith of his I make unshakeable.
WordMeaning
yo yaḥwhoever
yāṃ yāṃ tanumwhatever form
śraddhayāwith faith
acalāmunshakeable
vidadhāmiI make, I grant

A generous line. The doubled words, “whoever, whatever form”, leave no one out. Kṛṣṇa does not ask the devotee to change his god; he strengthens the faith already there. The next verse adds that the devotee receives his wishes through that faith, but they are granted by Him (7.22).

Text: the Gītā Press text as given in this site’s Gītā, chapter 7.
Your faith, made firm.

HOW AN IṢṬA COMES TO YOU

Six common ways

Nobody is assigned an iṣṭa by a rule. It arrives, usually by one of these roads.

🏠 Family tradition

The most common way: the family’s deity becomes one’s own, visited at every milestone, first haircut, thread ceremony, wedding.

🛕 The temple of one’s place

Growing up near a temple, Mysuru’s Cāmuṇḍī, Uḍupi’s Kṛṣṇa, Dharmasthaḷa’s Mañjunātha, often settles it.

💗 A pull of the heart

Some people are simply drawn: a picture, an image, a hymn heard once. The texts say the attraction itself is grace.

⚡ A turning point

An illness, a loss or a rescue, and the god prayed to at that moment stays.

🧘 A teacher

A guru may give a form and a name in initiation (dīkṣā). Such mantras are kept private; this library does not print them.

📖 A story or a text

A child who loves the Rāmāyaṇa grows up loving Rāma; a student of the Devī Māhātmya comes to the Goddess.

ZAPPY: Mine came from my grandmother. Is that a lesser way?

PROF. BOLT: It is the commonest and one of the strongest, Zappy. A god who has heard three generations of your family is no stranger.

Many roads to one form.

FAMILY, VILLAGE, CHOSEN

Kula devatā, grāma devatā, iṣṭa devatā

The family deity. The kula devatā, in Kannada the mane devaru, “the house god”, is handed down through the father’s line, sometimes the mother’s. Families go to that temple for the first haircut, before weddings and after births. Many Kannada families count Tirupati’s Veṅkaṭeśvara as theirs; the Woḍeyars of Mysuru count Cāmuṇḍeśvarī as their family goddess.

The village deity. The grāma devatā protects a place: usually a Mother with a local name, Māramma, Cauḍeśvarī, Durgamma, honoured at the village festival by everyone who lives there, whatever their own iṣṭa. See Grāma Devatās.

The chosen deity. The iṣṭa devatā is personal. It may be the same as the family deity, or quite different: a Śaiva family’s daughter who loves Kṛṣṇa, a Vaiṣṇava’s son devoted to Hanumān.

All three at once. Nothing stops a person from going to the family temple for a wedding, joining the village Mother’s festival, and praying to their own iṣṭa every morning. That is how most Hindus actually live.

ZAPPY: So I can have three gods and still be devoted to one?

PROF. BOLT: You can have three relationships, Zappy: family, neighbourhood and heart. Each is honest.

Family, place, heart.

THE FIVE IN ONE SHRINE

Pañcāyatana pūjā

Five together. In pañcāyatana pūjā, “worship of the five seats”, five deities are worshipped together: Śiva, Viṣṇu, the Goddess, Gaṇeśa and Sūrya. Tradition credits Śaṅkara with spreading it among Smārta households; a sixth, Skanda, makes the ṣaṇmata, the six paths.

The chosen one in the middle. The deity a family holds dearest sits in the centre; the other four take the corners. Turn the iṣṭa, and the layout turns with it: a Śiva pañcāyatana, a Gaṇeśa pañcāyatana, a Devī pañcāyatana.

Five stones. Many families keep the five as natural stones rather than images: a bāṇaliṅga from the Narmadā for Śiva, a śālagrāma from the Gaṇḍakī for Viṣṇu, and other stones for the Goddess, Gaṇeśa and Sūrya. (Traditions differ on the stones.)

Older than Śaṅkara. The pattern is old: a five-deity set from Nand, near Ajmer, is dated to the Kuṣāṇa period, and many temples of the first millennium, from Odisha to Karnataka, are laid out as pañcāyatanas. The idea of “one dear, all honoured” was built in stone long before it was written down.

ZAPPY: So the shrine itself shows how to love one and respect all?

PROF. BOLT: Exactly, Zappy. The centre is yours; the corners are never empty.

Your iṣṭa in the centre.

TULASĪDĀS AT VṚNDĀVANA

A devotee who bowed only to Rāma

The story

Told by Priyādās in his commentary on the Bhaktamāl (18th century) and retold everywhere; the couplet’s authorship is doubted by some scholars.

A Rāma devotee. Tulasīdās, the sixteenth-century poet of the Rāmcaritmānas, gave his whole life to Rāma.
A test. Visiting a Kṛṣṇa temple in Vṛndāvana, he was teased by the temple’s head: a devotee who bows to a deity other than his own is a fool, so would he bow to Kṛṣṇa?
The couplet. Tulasīdās answered in a Hindi couplet: “How shall I describe your beauty today, Lord? But Tulasī will bow his head only when you take the bow and arrow in your hands.”
The change. The story says the image of Kṛṣṇa laid down his flute and held a bow and arrows, and Tulasīdās saw Rāma on the altar and bowed.
The point. A shrine at Vṛndāvana still marks the place. The story is loved not because Tulasīdās refused Kṛṣṇa, but because Kṛṣṇa agreed to be Rāma for him: the Lord meets each devotee in the form he loves.

ZAPPY: Wasn’t that a bit rude of Tulasīdās?

PROF. BOLT: Kṛṣṇa didn’t seem to mind, Zappy. That is the moral: God is not jealous of himself.

God takes the form you love.

HARIHARA OF HARIHAR

Two gods in one image

The story

The local legend of Harihar, on the Tuṅgabhadrā in Davanagere district; the temple’s inscription is dated 1224 CE.

A clever boon. A demon named Guha, or Guhāsura, won a boon from Brahmā that neither Hari (Viṣṇu) nor Hara (Śiva) could kill him on his own, and he began to torment the land along the Tuṅgabhadrā.
A gap in the boon. The sages appealed for help. The boon had a gap: it protected him from each god singly, not from both at once.
One body. So Viṣṇu and Śiva joined into one form, Harihara, half Śiva and half Viṣṇu, and destroyed the demon. Local tellings place the descent at the meeting of the Tuṅgabhadrā and the Haridrā.
The image. In the temple at Harihar the image is split down the middle: the right half Śiva, with the trident and matted hair, and the left half Viṣṇu, with conch, discus and crown. The temple was built about 1224 CE by Polāḷva, a minister of the Hoysaḷa king Vīra Narasiṃha II.
Older still. The Harihara form was carved much earlier too, in the sixth-century cave temples of Bādāmi. Karnataka has many Śaṅkaranārāyaṇa and Harihara shrines.

ZAPPY: So the gods solved the problem by joining up?

PROF. BOLT: And left a picture for people who argue about whose god is greater, Zappy.

Two gods, one image.

ONE DEAR, ALL HONOURED

How devotion to one sits with reverence for others

Gods honour each other. In the epics the gods themselves model it. Rāma worships Śiva at Rāmeśvaram before crossing to Laṅkā; Kṛṣṇa, in the Mahābhārata, does penance to Śiva for a son. Their devotees can hardly do less.

Gaṇeśa first. Whatever a family’s iṣṭa, almost every Hindu rite begins with Gaṇeśa. A Vaiṣṇava wedding and a Śaiva temple festival open with the same invocation. One door for everyone, before each goes to his own room.

Hanumān’s answer. A well-known verse has Hanumān say that he knows the Lord of Śrī (Viṣṇu) and the Lord of Jānakī (Rāma) are one Supreme Self, and still, his all is lotus-eyed Rāma. It puts the whole idea in two lines: knowing the unity, loving the one. (The verse is traditional and found in popular collections.)

In practice. Most devotees greet every deity they pass in a temple, bow to a roadside shrine, join a neighbour’s festival, and still say their own god’s name when frightened. Exclusiveness has existed in history, but it is not the usual temper of Hindu devotion.

ZAPPY: So loving one god doesn’t mean disliking the others?

PROF. BOLT: Loving your mother doesn’t mean disliking your aunts, Zappy.

Know the one, love your own.

IN A KARNATAKA HOME

Mane devaru, Nāḍa Devī and the house shrine

A typical shelf. Open the shrine cupboard in a Mysuru home and you will often find Gaṇeśa at the top, the family god in the middle, perhaps Veṅkaṭaramaṇa or Mañjunātha, a picture of Cāmuṇḍī, and a guru: Rāghavēndra Svāmi, Śaṅkara of Śṛṅgeri, or another teacher of the family’s tradition.

Nāḍa Devī. Cāmuṇḍeśvarī is not only the Woḍeyars’ family goddess but the Nāḍa Devī, the goddess of the whole land, honoured at Dasarā by everyone in Mysuru whatever their iṣṭa. See Cāmuṇḍeśvarī.

Traditions in one street. A Mādhva home centres on Kṛṣṇa and Vāyu, a Smārta home keeps the pañcāyatana, a Śrīvaiṣṇava home Viṣṇu and Lakṣmī with the Āḻvārs, a Liṅgāyat home the liṅga. Neighbours visit each other’s festivals without a second thought.

Pilgrimage map. The kula devatā decides where a family travels: Tirupati, Kukke, Dharmasthaḷa, Melkote, Male Mahadeshwara Hills, Śṛṅgeri. The map of a family’s pilgrimages is a map of its gods.

ZAPPY: Is it normal to have so many pictures?

PROF. BOLT: In Karnataka it’s practically the rule, Zappy. The iṣṭa is the one you talk to first.

Many forms, one first.

THE IṢṬALIṄGA

A chosen god held in the palm

The word used literally. Karnataka’s Liṅgāyat (Vīraśaiva) tradition gives the word iṣṭa its most literal form: the iṣṭaliṅga, a small personal liṅga given to each member at initiation and worn on the body, usually in a case on a cord.

Worship in the hand. In daily worship the devotee holds the iṣṭaliṅga in the left palm and gazes at it steadily. The temple has come to the body; the chosen god is never left at home.

Basavaṇṇa’s reform. Basavaṇṇa and the twelfth-century Vacanakāras made the iṣṭaliṅga central, turning devotion away from temple hierarchy and towards a god every person carries, of any birth. See Basavaṇṇa.

Three liṅgas. Liṅgāyat teaching speaks of the iṣṭaliṅga in the hand, the prāṇaliṅga within, and the bhāvaliṅga beyond all form, a ladder from outer worship to inner realisation. (Simplified; the tradition has a fuller scheme.)

ZAPPY: A god you can carry in your pocket?

PROF. BOLT: On a cord near your heart, Zappy. No queue, no closing time.

The chosen god, always with you.

WHEN THE IṢṬA GROWS OR CHANGES

No guilt in the journey

Lifelong. For some the iṣṭa never changes. Mīrā sang to Kṛṣṇa from childhood to the end; Hanumān knew only Rāma. Constancy is honoured as a great virtue of bhakti.

Growing. For others it grows. A child of Gaṇeśa becomes a youth drawn to the Goddess and an old man at peace with Śiva. The texts do not count this as betrayal. Kṛṣṇa’s promise to make any faith firm (7.21) covers each stage.

Many paths tried. Some teachers deliberately practised several forms of devotion to test whether they led to the same place, and said they did. Others warn against hopping from god to god without depth. Both are wise; the difference is sincerity.

Life events. Marriage brings a new family deity into the home; a move brings a new village goddess. Most families simply add, rather than replace.

ZAPPY: If I change my iṣṭa, is the old one offended?

PROF. BOLT: Has the sun ever been offended because you moved to a different window, Zappy?

Faith that keeps growing.

WHAT AN IṢṬA IS NOT

Four misunderstandings

Not a team. Having an iṣṭa is not supporting a side. History does record quarrels between sects, and they make sad reading; the great teachers of each school also wrote with respect for others’ devotion.

Not a lucky charm. An iṣṭa is not a god kept for results. The relationship is love, not insurance; prayers for help are natural, but they are not the point.

Not a contract. No penalty follows for visiting another temple or praying to another form. Tulasīdās’s story shows a god changing shape to meet a devotee, not punishing him.

Not a closed door. The iṣṭa is a way in, not a wall. The Upaniṣads, the Gītā and the saints all point past the form to what it reveals. The form is loved precisely because it opens.

ZAPPY: Then why bother having one at all?

PROF. BOLT: Because love needs somewhere to land, Zappy. Read the next page.

A way in, not a wall.

WHY HAVE ONE AT ALL?

Love needs a face

Focus. A mind spread over every god is like light spread over a wall; gathered on one form, it burns. Teachers compare the iṣṭa to the single point on which a lens focuses the sun. (Interpretation.)

Relationship. Love grows with a particular face, a particular name, particular stories. One can admire “the Divine” in general; one talks to Kṛṣṇa, Cāmuṇḍī or Gaṇeśa.

Steadiness. In hard times the iṣṭa is the name that comes first. Nurses and doctors know the patient who whispers one god’s name before surgery; that steadiness is real.

A beginning, not an end. The Advaita teachers say the form leads beyond form; the Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva teachers say the form is the Lord himself, eternally. Either way, the journey starts with a face you love.

ZAPPY: So the iṣṭa is like a best friend among many friends?

PROF. BOLT: A best friend who also happens to be everyone’s friend, Zappy.

Love needs a face.

FINDING YOURS

Six gentle steps

There is no test and no deadline. These steps simply give the relationship room to grow.

1️⃣ Ask your family

Which temple do we go to? Whose picture is in the middle? Start with what you have inherited.

2️⃣ Notice the pull

Which image, hymn or story moves you most? That is worth attention.

3️⃣ Read the guides

Spend a week with one god’s story: Gods and Goddesses.

4️⃣ Visit and look

Go to that god’s temple and simply look for a while: Part 3.

5️⃣ Take the name

Say that god’s name daily for a month: Part 4.

6️⃣ Keep the others

Go on greeting every deity you meet. Your iṣṭa will not mind.

ZAPPY: What if nothing pulls me?

PROF. BOLT: Then start with your family’s god, Zappy, and let the relationship grow. Most people find the pull comes after the habit.

Inherit, notice, keep going.
