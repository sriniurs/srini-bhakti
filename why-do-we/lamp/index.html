The Lamp

Why Do We Light a Dīpa? · explained and illustrated

A small flame, a big idea

HOW THIS WORKS: Why Hindus light a lamp every morning and evening: what the flame stands for, the dīpa ślokas, oil or ghee, how many wicks and which way they face, the brass kuthuvilakku and the clay diya, why we never blow a lamp out, and the great festivals of light.

WHERE WE ARE

In almost every Hindu home, the day begins and ends with a small flame. Before a pūjā, before a wedding, before a school function or a business opening, someone lights a lamp (dīpa). It is the simplest act of worship and one of the oldest.

This guide explains what the lamp means, the ślokas said while lighting it, the practical customs families follow, and the festivals built around it. It joins our other “Why do we…” answers, such as ārati, which is the lamp waved before God.

WHAT THE FLAME MEANS

Light over darkness

Knowledge over ignorance. Darkness stands for ignorance (avidyā) and light for knowledge. Lighting a lamp is a small prayer to see clearly.

“Lead me from darkness to light.” The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad (1.3.28) prays: asato mā sad gamaya, tamaso mā jyotir gamaya, mṛtyor mā amṛtaṃ gamaya, “from the unreal to the real, from darkness to light, from death to the deathless.”

Agni. The flame is Agni, the fire god of the Vedas, the messenger who carries offerings to the gods. A household lamp is a tiny sacred fire.

The upward flame. A flame always rises, however the lamp is turned; teachers use it as a picture of a mind turned towards God. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: A whole Upaniṣad in one lamp?

PROF. BOLT: In one line of it, Zappy: from darkness to light.

From darkness to light.

IN THE VERSE

The lamp śloka

Said while lighting the lamp
śubhaṃ karoti kalyāṇam ārogyaṃ dhanasampadaḥśatrubuddhivināśāya dīpajyotir namo’stu te
You bring what is good and auspicious, health and wealth; you destroy hostile thinking: O light of the lamp, I bow to you.
WordMeaning
śubhamgood, auspicious
kalyāṇamwell-being
ārogyamhealth
dhana-sampadaḥriches and prosperity
śatru-buddhihostile thinking
dīpa-jyotiḥlight of the lamp

A family verse. This is the śloka most children learn first for the evening lamp. Read gently, the “enemy” it destroys is the hostile mind, our own included.

A popular household verse; the text is widely printed with small variations.
Bow to the light.

IN THE VERSE

The light is God

A second lamp verse
dīpajyotiḥ parabrahma dīpajyotir janārdanaḥdīpo haratu me pāpaṃ dīpajyotir namo’stu te
The light of the lamp is the supreme Brahman; the light of the lamp is Janārdana (Viṣṇu). May the lamp take away my wrongdoing. O light of the lamp, I bow to you.
WordMeaning
parabrahmathe supreme reality
janārdanaḥViṣṇu, “who moves people”
haratumay it remove
pāpamwrongdoing

Seeing God in the flame. Here the lamp is not only a symbol: it is worshipped as God present. Many families say both verses together each evening.

Popular household verse, widely printed.
The flame as God.

LIGHTING IT WELL

Customs families follow

🪔 When

At dawn and at dusk (sandhyā), the two meeting-times of day, and before any pūjā, journey or new beginning.

🧈 Ghee or oil

Ghee is the most sacred fuel; sesame oil (til) is the everyday one. Many families use ghee for the deity and oil for the household lamp.

🧵 Wicks

Cotton wicks, single or twisted in pairs. Five-wick lamps are lit for festivals. Customs about which direction the wick faces vary; east or north is commonly preferred.

🌼 Putting it out

Lamps are not blown out with the breath; they are left to burn, or gently put out with a flower or by drawing the wick into the oil.

ZAPPY: Why not blow it out?

PROF. BOLT: Breath on a sacred flame feels disrespectful, Zappy. Families vary on the rest; that one is nearly universal.

Light it with care.

KINDS OF LAMPS

From clay to bronze

🟤 Clay diya

The commonest lamp: kiln-fired earthenware with one wick, lit in rows for Dīpāvalī.

🟡 Nilavilakku and kuthuvilakku

Tall brass standing lamps of Kerala and Tamil Nadu. The nilavilakku is lit at every Kerala function; the kuthuvilakku has several faces.

⬜ Stone lamps

Stone lamps (kalvilakku) and tall lamp-pillars stand before many temples, lit on festival nights.

💧 Floating lamps

Leaf, banana-stem or bamboo lamps set on rivers and tanks, as at Kārttika Pūrṇimā.

ZAPPY: A lamp-pillar outside a temple?

PROF. BOLT: Look for one on your next visit, Zappy, especially in Karnataka and Kerala.

Clay or bronze, the same flame.

THE LAMP THROUGH LIFE

From birth to farewell

Birth. A lamp burns near a newborn, and the naming and other childhood rites begin with lighting one; see the sixteen saṃskāras.

Weddings. The couple’s rituals take place before the sacred fire and the lit lamp; in many families the bride brings a lamp to her new home.

New beginnings. Schools, offices, conferences and films in India are still “inaugurated” by lighting a tall lamp.

Farewell. A lamp is kept burning beside one who has died, and in rites for ancestors; see Pitṛ Pakṣa.

ZAPPY: Even government functions light a lamp!

PROF. BOLT: It’s become India’s way of saying “we begin”, Zappy.

A flame for every start.

FESTIVALS OF LIGHT

When the whole land is lit

🪔 Dīpāvalī

“Row of lamps”: homes outlined with diyas for Lakṣmī and the victory of light; see Dīpāvalī.

🔥 Kārttika

The whole month of Kārttika is the month of lamps, ending with Kārttikai Dīpam and Kārttika Pūrṇimā.

✨ Lakṣadīpotsava

“A hundred thousand lamps”: temple festivals where every ledge glows, as at Kārttika Somavāra.

🌙 Every evening

The daily sandhyā lamp is the quiet festival of light in every home.

ZAPPY: Is there a lamp festival every month?

PROF. BOLT: Nearly, Zappy, but Kārttika is the king of them.

A month of lamps.

THE LAMP WITHIN

What teachers say

The steady flame. The Bhagavad Gītā (6.19) compares a disciplined mind to a lamp in a windless place that does not flicker; see Gītā chapter 6.

Light that lights others. One lamp can light a thousand without losing its own flame; teachers use this for sharing knowledge. (A popular image.)

Self-giving. The wick and oil are used up to give light. Some read the lamp as a picture of a life spent for others. (Interpretation.)

The inner light. In Vedānta the self (ātman) is “self-luminous”: the light by which everything else is known.

ZAPPY: A lamp that doesn’t flicker…

PROF. BOLT: That’s the goal of meditation, Zappy. Watching a real one helps.

Be the steady flame.
