What Makes a Mūrti Sacred?

The Image Where God Is Invited · explained and illustrated

The image where God is invited

HOW THIS WORKS: What makes a consecrated image different: the words for the mūrti, the Bhāgavata’s eight kinds, the sculptor’s rules, the rite of prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā, self-manifest stones, the Lord’s descent into the image, Ekalavya and Jakaṇācāri, temporary images, renewal, and the temple centred on its deity.

WHERE WE ARE

Every temple guide on this site ends, sooner or later, at a small dark room and the image inside it. Visitors photograph the carvings on the outer walls; devotees walk past all of them to stand before one figure in lamplight. What makes that figure different from the hundred carved gods outside? The answer is a rite and a belief: the Lord has been invited into it, and has agreed to stay.

This part explains the mūrti: the words for it, the eight kinds of image the Bhāgavata lists, how a sculptor makes one, the consecration called prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā, images that need no consecration, the Śrīvaiṣṇava idea of God descending into the image, Ekalavya’s clay teacher and Jakaṇācāri’s frog, temporary images and their farewell, renewal and repair, and how worship centres on the deity.

The previous part was Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka?. This is the last of the seven; the set begins with What Is Bhakti?. For the building around the image, read How to Read a Hindu Temple.

THE WORDS

Mūrti, vigraha, pratimā, arcā

Mūrti. From mūrch, “to take shape, solidify”: a mūrti is a form, an embodiment, something formless made solid. It is the most common word and the one used for both a temple image and the embodied form of a quality (“she is the mūrti of patience”).

Vigraha. “That which holds”: the body that holds the divine presence. Temple people speak of the mūla-vigraha, the main fixed image, and the utsava-vigraha, the metal image carried in processions.

Pratimā. “Likeness, image”, from prati-mā, “to measure against”. It stresses that the image is made to a measure, according to the rules for each deity.

Arcā. “That which is worshipped”. Śrīvaiṣṇavas use arcā for the Lord present in the image, as one of his five forms; that idea has its own page later. Not one of these words means “idol” in the sense of a false god.

ZAPPY: So “idol” is the wrong word?

PROF. BOLT: It is the word outsiders used, Zappy. Devotees mean something closer to “embodiment”.

Form, body, likeness, presence.

Even the mind. Kṛṣṇa, teaching Uddhava the way of image-worship, lists eight materials, and the seventh is the mind: an image built in meditation is as real a support as one carved in granite. The list frees worship from wealth: sand on a riverbank will do. The next verse distinguishes fixed images from movable ones (11.27.13).

Stone, sand or the mind.

HOW A MŪRTI IS MADE

The sthapati and the śilpa śāstras

The maker. Temple images are made by the sthapati, the master sculptor, in lineages that pass down skill and texts. The work begins with rites: choosing and testing the stone, purifying the sculptor, and a prayer before the first cut.

The rules. The śilpa śāstras and āgamas, texts such as the Mānasāra and Mayamata, set the proportions of each deity in units called tāla (face-lengths), the number of arms, the weapons and gestures, so that the image is recognisably that god.

The stone. Texts and sculptors distinguish “male”, “female” and “neuter” stones by sound and grain, and look for flaws: cracks, veins, hollows. A flawed stone is not used for a main image, as the story of Jakaṇācāri shows.

Metal and wood. Processional images are often cast in five-metal alloy (pañcaloha) by the lost-wax method, still practised in famous workshops of the south. Wood is used in Kerala and for the great forms at Purī, where the images are periodically renewed.

ZAPPY: Does the sculptor think he is making a god?

PROF. BOLT: He thinks he is making a perfect body, Zappy. The priest invites the god in.

A body made to measure.

PRĀṆA-PRATIṢṬHĀ

Establishing the breath

The word. Prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā means “establishing the breath”: the rite by which hymns and mantras invite the deity to dwell in the image as a resident guest, and the image’s eyes are opened. Temple images are usually consecrated once, for a lasting presence.

Living in the elements. Before the main rite the image “lives” for a time in different elements, the adhivāsas: in water (jalādhivāsa), in grain (dhānyādhivāsa), and on a bed (śayyādhivāsa), purifying and preparing it. Fire offerings run alongside for days in great consecrations.

Opening the eyes. The eyes are opened (netronmīlana), often with a golden needle and honey or ghee, and the first gaze is turned towards auspicious things. Then, through nyāsa, the priest touches the image limb by limb with mantras, placing the deity’s presence in every part, and the deity is seated facing east.

Then the bath. The consecration ends with a great abhiṣeka, often from pots charged with mantras over the fires, poured from the tower top in a temple’s kumbhābhiṣeka; see Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka?. From that moment the image is worshipped as the deity.

ZAPPY: Why live in grain and water first?

PROF. BOLT: Like a guest washing and resting before taking his seat, Zappy. And the stone is cooled and settled for its new life.

Breath invited into stone.

IMAGES THAT NEED NO CONSECRATION

Svayambhū, śālagrāma, bāṇaliṅga

Self-manifest. Some images are not made but found: svayambhū, “self-born”. Many great Śiva liṅgas, Goddess rocks and hill shrines are of this kind; they are believed to be where the deity chose to appear, and their worship begins without any consecration.

Śālagrāma. The black fossil ammonite stones of the Gaṇḍakī river in Nepal are worshipped as Viṣṇu himself. Tradition holds that a śālagrāma needs no prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā; it is kept in many homes and bathed daily.

Bāṇaliṅga. Smooth egg-shaped stones from the Narmadā, the bāṇaliṅgas, are similarly worshipped as Śiva without consecration. Śālagrāma and bāṇaliṅga together sit in many home pañcāyatanas; see Who Is Your Iṣṭa Devatā?.

Found images. Many famous images are said to have been found rather than made: Uḍupi’s Kṛṣṇa, which tradition says Madhvācārya drew out of a block of gopīcandana clay at Malpe, is one; see Madhvācārya.

ZAPPY: So nature makes gods too?

PROF. BOLT: The tradition says God sometimes chooses a stone himself, Zappy. Then no priest is needed to invite him.

Found, not made.

THE LORD DESCENDS

Arcāvatāra in Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching

Five forms. Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching, drawing on the Pāñcarātra tradition, speaks of five forms of the Lord: para, the supreme form in Vaikuṇṭha; vyūha, his emanations; vibhava, his avatāras such as Rāma and Kṛṣṇa; antaryāmī, the inner controller in every heart; and arcā, his presence in the consecrated image.

The most accessible. Of the five, the teachers say, the arcā is the easiest to reach: the supreme form is far, the avatāras lived long ago, the inner one is hard to see, but the temple image is here, now, for anyone who comes.

God’s own humility. The Āḻvārs and Śrīvaiṣṇava ācāryas describe the arcā form as an act of the Lord’s accessibility (saulabhya): he agrees to be bathed, dressed, carried and fed by devotees, as though dependent on them. (Interpretation.)

Other schools. Śaiva Siddhānta speaks of Śiva present in the liṅga through the consecration; Advaita teachers often describe the image as a support for the mind (pratīka) that leads beyond form. Traditions explain the presence differently, but all worship with the same care.

ZAPPY: God agrees to be carried around?

PROF. BOLT: In a palanquin on festival days, Zappy. The ācāryas found that the most moving thing of all.

The Lord within reach.

EKALAVYA’S CLAY TEACHER

An image that taught

The story

From the Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva. The ending is painful and much discussed; the image is the point here.

A forest boy. Ekalavya, son of a Niṣāda chief of the forest, came to Droṇa, the royal teacher of archery, and asked to be his student. Droṇa refused him because of his birth.
The clay Droṇa. Ekalavya went back into the forest, made an image of Droṇa out of clay, and worshipped it as his teacher.
Practice before the image. Every day he practised archery before the clay figure with total devotion, and became so skilled that he outshone Arjuna, Droṇa’s favourite.
Discovered. When the Pāṇḍavas found him, he told them simply that Droṇa was his teacher. The epic’s ending, in which Droṇa demands his right thumb as fee and Ekalavya gives it, has troubled readers for centuries and is read in many ways.
The point for us. Whatever one makes of the ending, the story shows what devotion to an image can do: a lump of clay, honoured as the teacher, became a real presence that taught. This is the belief behind every mūrti.

ZAPPY: A clay statue taught him archery?

PROF. BOLT: His devotion to it did, Zappy. The image made the teacher present.

Devotion makes the image speak.

JAKAṆĀCĀRI AND THE FROG

A flaw in the stone at Belur

The story

Hoysaḷa-era Karnataka folklore, told at Belur and Kaidala. See Belur and Halebīḍu.

The master. Jakaṇācāri of Krīḍāpura was the most famous sculptor of the Hoysaḷa age. He had left home young, leaving a wife and an unborn son.
A stranger’s challenge. As the image of Ceṇṇakeśava for Belur neared completion, a young sculptor said the stone was flawed. Jakaṇācāri swore to cut off his right hand if that were true.
The test. The image was covered with sandal paste. It dried everywhere except at the navel. Opened, the spot held a hollow with water, sand and a live frog.
The price. Jakaṇācāri kept his word and cut off his hand; only then did he learn that the young man was his own son, Ḍaṅkaṇa. The image is still called Kappe Cenniga, “the frog Cenniga”, in a shrine beside the main temple.
The restored hand. Told in a vision to carve one more Ceṇṇakeśava at home, he did, and his hand was restored; the village came to be called Kaidala, “the hand restored”.

ZAPPY: Why does a frog matter?

PROF. BOLT: Because a mūrti must be flawless to hold the Lord, Zappy. The story is about how seriously the makers took that.

Only a flawless stone will do.

IMAGES THAT COME AND GO

Āvāhana and visarjana

Invited for a while. Not every image is consecrated for ever. In festival worship a clay Gaṇeśa or Durgā is invited in with āvāhana (“calling in”), worshipped for some days as fully present, and then bid farewell with visarjana (“sending off”).

Into the water. The farewell usually ends with immersing the clay image in a lake, river or sea, returning the earth to the earth. Karnataka’s Gaṇeśa Habba ends this way in thousands of homes and street pandals. Many families now use plain clay and immerse at home in a bucket, out of care for lakes.

Daily invitations. Even in a home pūjā, some traditions call the deity into a flower, a pot (kalaśa) or a picture at the start and send him back at the end. The presence is real while the worship lasts.

What remains. The tradition teaches that the deity departs; the clay is simply clay again. That is why immersion is not an insult but a courteous parting.

ZAPPY: Isn’t it sad to put Gaṇeśa in the lake?

PROF. BOLT: People cry a little, Zappy, and shout “come again next year!” That is how you say goodbye to a guest you love.

Welcome, worship, farewell.

RENEWAL AND REPAIR

Kumbhābhiṣeka and jīrṇoddhāra

Renewing the presence. Temples renew their consecration periodically, traditionally about every twelve years, with a kumbhābhiṣeka or saṃprokṣaṇa: repairs, fresh paint, days of fire rites, and water poured over the tower finials and the image. Crowds gather for it as for a great festival.

When an image is damaged. The āgamas treat a broken or badly worn main image seriously. Through jīrṇoddhāra, “lifting up the old”, the presence is first drawn out of the image into a pot by rites, a new image is made and consecrated, and the old one is respectfully immersed or kept.

Purī’s new bodies. At Purī the wooden images of Jagannātha, Balabhadra and Subhadrā are replaced at long intervals in the Navakalevara, “new body”, rite, a vivid case of the same principle.

Home images. A cracked home image is not thrown away; families place it in a river or under a sacred tree with a prayer, and bring a new one with a simple invitation.

ZAPPY: So even God gets a renovation?

PROF. BOLT: His house does, Zappy, and the invitation is renewed. Like repainting the home and having the family elder bless it again.

Renewed, never discarded.

THE TEMPLE CENTRES ON THE DEITY

How worship flows from the image

The axis. A temple is built outward from the sanctum. The tower rises above the image; the halls, flagstaff and gateways line up on its gaze; the circumambulation path goes round it. Everything in How to Read a Hindu Temple points back to this one room.

The day. The temple day follows the deity’s day: waking, bath, adornment, meals, audiences, rest. The priests are the household staff of a living king; see What Is Darśan? for the times of darśan.

Fixed and moving. The main image (mūla-mūrti) never leaves the sanctum; the festival image (utsava-mūrti) goes out in processions, on chariots and boats, so that the whole town can see the god. The Bhāgavata’s distinction of fixed and movable images (11.27.13) lives on in every festival.

The town. In Karnataka towns like Melkote, Uḍupi and Nañjanagūḍu, the deity’s calendar is the town’s calendar: the car festival, the float festival, the crown day. The mūrti is the town’s first citizen.

ZAPPY: So the whole temple is built around one small image?

PROF. BOLT: Around one guest, Zappy. The house is big because the guest is great.

Everything points to the guest.

WHY WORSHIP THROUGH AN IMAGE?

The teachers’ answers

Love needs a form. Few people can love the formless directly. An image gives the senses something to rest on, so the heart can follow. It is the iṣṭa of Who Is Your Iṣṭa Devatā? given a body.

Not the stone, the presence. Devotees do not worship stone as stone. They worship the Lord believed present in it by his own consent. The difference is like that between paper and a letter from someone you love.

A discipline of care. Bathing, dressing, feeding and greeting a mūrti trains attention, gentleness and regularity. Many teachers say the worshipper is shaped more than the image. (Interpretation.)

Beyond the image. The texts that teach image-worship also teach that God is everywhere; the eighth kind of image in the Bhāgavata is the one made in the mind. The temple image is a door, and the Lord is on both sides of it.

ZAPPY: So the image is like a photo of Grandma?

PROF. BOLT: Closer than that, Zappy. A photo reminds you. The tradition says the mūrti is where he actually is.

A door with God on both sides.
