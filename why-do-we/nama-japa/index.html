Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa

Repeating and Remembering the Name · explained and illustrated

Repeating and remembering

HOW THIS WORKS: Holding on to God through his name: japa and smaraṇa, the three ways from loud to silent, the mālā, the Gītā’s offering of japa, the Bhāgavata’s promise for this age, Ajāmila, the Rāma koṭi notebook, singing the names together, and Karnataka’s Haridāsa tradition.

WHERE WE ARE

Many grandmothers in Karnataka say a divine name under their breath all day, while cooking, walking, waiting for the bus, and do not think of it as a practice at all. Others sit each morning with a mālā and count rounds. Others sing the names together on an Ekādaśī night until dawn. All of these are the same thing seen from different sides: holding on to God through his name.

This part explains nāma japa, the repetition of a divine name, and nāma smaraṇa, keeping it in mind. It covers the words, the three ways of japa from loud to silent, the mālā, the Gītā’s “of offerings I am japa”, the Bhāgavata’s promise for this age, Ajāmila’s story, names written in notebooks, singing the names together, Karnataka’s bhajan tradition, and how to begin.

The previous part was What Is Darśan?; the next is The Nine Forms of Bhakti. The counting of japa on 108 beads is explained in Why 108?.

JAPA AND SMARAṆA

Repeating and remembering

Japa. The word comes from jap, “to mutter, to repeat in a low voice”. Japa is the deliberate repetition of a divine name, a short prayer or a mantra, usually a set number of times. It is something one does, with a beginning and an end.

Smaraṇa. From smṛ, “to remember”. Nāma smaraṇa is keeping the name in mind through the day, not as an exercise but as a background, the way one keeps a loved person in mind while doing other things. Remembrance is one of the four faces of bhakti in What Is Bhakti?.

How they meet. Japa trains smaraṇa. A person who counts a round of the name each morning finds it surfacing by itself later, in traffic, in a hospital corridor, at a funeral. Teachers say the japa is the practice; the smaraṇa is the fruit.

Name and mantra. A divine name (Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Śiva, Nārāyaṇa) can be said by anyone. Some mantras are given by a teacher in initiation and kept private; this library describes them but does not print them. The open names are enough for a lifetime.

ZAPPY: So japa is the exercise and smaraṇa is the fitness?

PROF. BOLT: Nicely put, Zappy. You train in the morning so the name is there when you need it.

Repeat, then remember.

THREE WAYS OF JAPA

Loud, whispered, silent

Spoken. In vācika japa the name is said aloud, clearly enough to be heard. It holds a wandering mind and suits beginners and noisy places. Group chanting is always vācika.

Whispered. In upāṃśu japa the lips and tongue move but no one nearby hears; the sound is almost inside.

Silent. In mānasa japa there is no movement at all; the name is repeated in the mind alone. It is the hardest to keep steady and the deepest when it works.

Manu’s scale. The Manusmṛti says the offering of japa excels the ritual sacrifice tenfold, the whispered kind excels it a hundredfold, and the mental kind a thousandfold (2.85). The commentator Medhātithi warns that this praises japa rather than abolishing ritual. Many teachers say: begin aloud, let it sink.

ZAPPY: Why would silent be better than loud?

PROF. BOLT: Because it has to come from inside, Zappy. Nobody can fake a silent japa for an audience.

From the lips to the mind.

THE MĀLĀ

Beads that keep the count

Why beads. A mālā frees the mind from counting. The fingers keep track while attention stays on the name. A full mālā has 108 beads and a larger meru or guru bead; the reasons for 108 are in Why 108?.

Materials. Tradition matches material to deity: rudrākṣa seeds for Śiva, tulasī wood for Viṣṇu and Kṛṣṇa, crystal (sphaṭika) or red sandal for the Goddess, lotus seeds for Lakṣmī. Any clean mālā will do for a beginner.

The fingers. Commonly the mālā hangs over the middle finger of the right hand and the thumb pulls each bead; the index finger, said to stand for the ego, is kept away. At the meru one turns the mālā rather than crossing it. (Customs vary.)

Kept close. Many keep the hand and mālā inside a cloth bag, the gomukhī, while counting, and do not display the mālā as an ornament. It is a tool of intimacy, not a sign of piety.

ZAPPY: Do I need a mālā to do japa?

PROF. BOLT: No, Zappy. Fingers will do, or nothing at all. The mālā just keeps the count honest.

The beads count, you love.

IN THE VERSE

Yajñānāṃ japayajño ’smi

GĪTĀ 10.25 · THE OFFERING OF JAPA
maharṣīṇāṃ bhṛgur ahaṃgirām asmy ekam akṣaram |yajñānāṃ japa-yajño ’smisthāvarāṇāṃ himālayaḥ ||
Among the great sages I am Bhṛgu; among words I am the single syllable; among offerings I am the offering of japa; among things that stand still, the Himālaya.
WordMeaning
girāmamong words
ekam akṣaramthe one syllable, Oṃ
yajñānāmamong offerings
japa-yajñaḥthe offering of repetition

The cheapest offering, ranked highest. In the chapter where Kṛṣṇa names the best of every kind, he chooses japa as the best of sacrifices. A fire offering needs ghee, fuel, priests and rules; japa needs breath and attention. Teachers note that it harms no one and can be done anywhere.

Text: the Gītā Press text as in this site’s Gītā, chapter 10.
The best offering costs nothing.

IN THE VERSE

Kalau tad dhari-kīrtanāt

BHĀGAVATA 12.3.52 · FOR THIS AGE
kṛte yad dhyāyato viṣṇuṃtretāyāṃ yajato makhaiḥ |dvāpare paricaryāyāṃkalau tad dhari-kīrtanāt ||
What was won in the Kṛta age by meditating on Viṣṇu, in the Tretā by sacrifices, and in the Dvāpara by temple worship, is won in the Kali age by singing the name of Hari.
WordMeaning
kṛtein the first age
dhyāyataḥby one who meditates
makhaiḥby sacrifices
paricaryāyāmby worship, service
hari-kīrtanātby singing Hari’s name

Four ages, one goal. The verse lays the four ages side by side and gives each its method; for ours, the name. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa (6.2.17) makes the same promise in other words. Every devotional school of the last thousand years has leaned on it.

Text: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 12.3.52 as on vedabase.io and wisdomlib.org (Sanskrit), transliterated into IAST.
For this age, the name.

AJĀMILA AND THE NAME

Saved by calling his son

The story

From the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 6, chapters 1–2.

A fallen life. Ajāmila was a learned young brāhmaṇa who gave up his duties, fell into bad company and lived by cheating and theft. In old age his greatest joy was his youngest son, whom he had named Nārāyaṇa.
The last cry. As he lay dying, he saw three terrifying figures come for him, the messengers of Yama. In fear he cried out for his little boy: “Nārāyaṇa!”
Another set of messengers. At that name, the messengers of Viṣṇu appeared and stopped Yama’s servants. The two sides argued over the dying man: had he not sinned all his life?
The ruling. Viṣṇu’s messengers said that the name of the Lord, said even without meaning it, as a joke or by accident, destroys sin as fire burns grass. Yama later confirmed this to his own servants.
A second life. Ajāmila recovered, understood what had happened, and spent the rest of his days in devotion at Haridvār. The story is told everywhere to show the power of the name.

ZAPPY: He didn’t even mean God. Does that count?

PROF. BOLT: The Bhāgavata says it did, Zappy. That is why parents name children after gods: the name gets said a thousand times a day.

Even an accident of the name.

WHY A NAME?

The teachers’ reasons

The name and the named. Most devotional schools teach that the Lord’s name is not a label but the Lord himself in sound: nāma and nāmī are one. Saying it is not describing God; it is calling him.

A call is answered. A child who calls “Amma!” is not giving information; she is asking for attention, and the mother turns. The teachers use exactly this picture for japa. (Interpretation.)

Open to all. The name needs no priest, no money, no learning, no special place. It can be said by a child, a labourer, a patient in a bed. That is why the saints of every region made it central.

A one-name teacher. The Rāma Navamī verse “śrī rāma rāma rāmeti” has Śiva tell Pārvatī that the name of Rāma equals the thousand names of Viṣṇu; it is explained in Rāma Navamī.

ZAPPY: If the name is God, is it all right to say it in the bathroom?

PROF. BOLT: The saints said it everywhere, Zappy. God is not fussy about rooms; people are.

The name is a call.

NAMES ON PAPER

Likhita japa and the Rāma koṭi

Japa with a pen. Likhita japa is writing the name over and over, a practice that suits restless minds: the hand, the eye and the mind are all held by one word.

The Rāma koṭi. Many homes in Karnataka and Āndhra keep a Rāma koṭi notebook, aiming to write “Śrī Rāma” a crore (ten million) times over years, often across generations. Finished books are offered at a Rāma temple, and some temples have stores of them.

Slow and careful. Writing forces slowness: each letter is a small act of attention. Grandmothers who could not sit still for japa have filled shelves this way.

Respect. Books of written names are treated as sacred: not left on the floor, not thrown away, but offered or kept.

ZAPPY: A crore times? That’s impossible!

PROF. BOLT: Not over a lifetime, Zappy. A page a day, and a family that keeps going after you.

The name, written by hand.

SINGING THE NAMES TOGETHER

Nāma saṅkīrtana and bhajans

Quiet and loud. Japa is usually private and quiet; nāma saṅkīrtana is the same name sung together, aloud, with rhythm. Both are counted as forms of devotion; the Bhāgavata’s list of nine places kīrtana, singing the Lord’s names and glories, second, right after hearing. See The Nine Forms of Bhakti.

Why together. A group carries the weak and the tired. A voice that would trail off alone keeps going when twenty others are singing. Many people who cannot sit for silent japa find their way in through a bhajan.

Call and response. Most bhajans are simple: a leader sings a line of names, the group repeats it, the tempo rises. The music is meant to be easy enough for anyone to join.

Quiet after loud. Teachers often pair the two: sing the names together, then sit in silence and let the name continue inside. Group singing and private japa feed each other. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: Is singing as good as silent japa?

PROF. BOLT: For the person singing with their whole heart, Zappy, it may be better. The texts praise both.

Many voices, one name.

THE NAME IN KARNATAKA

Haridāsas, bhajana maṇḍalis and Ekādaśī nights

The Haridāsas. From the fifteenth century the Kannada Haridāsas, Purandara Dāsa, Kanaka Dāsa and others, walked from village to village singing the names of Hari in simple Kannada. Purandara Dāsa is remembered as the father of Karnāṭaka music, and many of his songs are about the name itself; see Purandara Dāsa.

Bhajana maṇḍalis. Towns and villages across Karnataka have bhajana maṇḍalis, singing groups that meet in a temple or a home on a set evening, Thursday for Rāghavēndra or the guru, Saturday for Veṅkaṭeśvara or Hanumān.

Ekādaśī nights. On Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī and other great nights, groups sing the names until dawn as an act of vigil, jāgaraṇa. See Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī.

Śiva’s names too. The Liṅgāyat and Śaiva households of the state keep their own five-syllable invocation of Śiva, and Vacana singing in the evenings carries the same spirit in Śiva’s name.

ZAPPY: So Karnataka’s classical music grew out of singing God’s name?

PROF. BOLT: Much of it did, Zappy. Purandara Dāsa’s first lessons are still taught to every beginner.

A state that sings the name.

HOW TO BEGIN

A practical starting plan

No initiation is needed to begin with an open divine name. A teacher can deepen it later.

🕰️ Pick a time

Same time daily, usually after the bath in the morning or before sleep. Habit matters more than length.

📛 Pick a name

Your iṣṭa’s name, as simple as possible: Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Śiva, Nārāyaṇa, Gaṇeśa, Durgā. See Who Is Your Iṣṭa Devatā?.

📿 Pick a count

One round of 108 takes about ten minutes. Start with one; do not raise it until it is steady for a month.

🗣️ Start aloud

Begin softly aloud, then let it become a whisper, then silent, as Manu’s scale suggests.

🌫️ When the mind wanders

It will. Notice, smile, and come back to the next bead. That return is the practice.

🚶 Carry it

Through the day, say the name at small gaps: lights, queues, waiting rooms. That is smaraṇa.

ZAPPY: What if I lose count?

PROF. BOLT: Then you’ve lost count, Zappy, not God. Start the round again or just keep going.

One round, every day.

WHAT JAPA IS NOT

Three mix-ups

Not parroting. The old saints joked about the parrot that says “Rāma” all day and thinks of fruit. Japa without any attention is still better than nothing, the Ajāmila story suggests, but the teachers ask for the heart to come along.

Not scorekeeping. Counting helps; boasting of crores of japa does not. The count is between the devotee and God.

Not a slot machine. Japa is sometimes taken up for a result, and the tradition allows it. But the name is meant to change the one who says it, not to buy something. Most people who start for a wish keep going for love.

ZAPPY: So I should mean it?

PROF. BOLT: As much as you can, Zappy. And on days you can’t, say it anyway. Meaning has a way of catching up.

Attention, not arithmetic.
