Why Do We Do Namaskāra?

The Joined Palms · explained and illustrated

The bow of joined hands

HOW THIS WORKS: Why we do namaskāra: the word, the joined palms of añjali, levels of the gesture, full and kneeling prostration, touching elders’ feet, abhivādana and bows to sun, earth and stage.

WHERE WE ARE

Two palms pressed together at the chest, a slight bow of the head: namaskāra is the most familiar Hindu gesture, used for God, for elders and for a stranger at the door. In the temple it can grow into a full-length prostration on the floor; before an elder it becomes a touch of the feet.

This part explains the word, the joined palms, the levels of the gesture, the full prostration and its eight limbs, touching elders’ feet and their blessing, the formal greeting with one’s lineage, namaskāra to the sun, the earth and the stage, and what it means.

ZAPPY: Are there rules for it?

PROF. BOLT: Some, by custom. The bow matters more than the rules.

The bow of joined hands.

THE WORD

Namaḥ: I bow

Namaḥ. Sanskrit namaḥ (from the root nam, to bend) means a bow or salutation.

Namaste. Namaḥ + te: “a bow to you”.

Namaskāra. Namaḥ + kāra, “making”: the act of bowing.

In mantras. Namaḥ appears in countless prayers: “Oṃ namaḥ Śivāya”, salutations to Śiva.

Regional. Kannaḍa namaskāra, Tamiḻ vaṇakkam, Hindi namaste, praṇām.

ZAPPY: So “namaḥ” is in every prayer?

PROF. BOLT: Very many. Before you ask the god anything, you bow.

First bend.

AÑJALI

The joined palms

Añjali mudrā. The palms are pressed together, fingers pointing up, thumbs near the chest.

Offering. Añjali also means a double handful offered; the gesture is like holding out an offering. (Common explanation.)

Two made one. Teachers read the two palms as two persons, or self and God, meeting. (Interpretation.)

Empty hands. Joined palms hold no weapon: a gesture of peace. (Common explanation.)

In art. Devotees, Garuḍa and Hanumān are shown with joined palms before the god.

ZAPPY: Does the left or right go in front?

PROF. BOLT: Neither. Palm to palm, evenly.

Two hands, one gesture.

HOW HIGH

Levels of the gesture

Many communities teach these levels; practice varies.

🙏 Chest

Greeting equals, guests, strangers.

🧑‍🏫 Face

For teachers and elders, in some customs.

🛕 Above the head

Before God, in many customs.

🙇 With a bow

Head bowed adds humility at any level.

👀 Eyes closed

Often closed before God, open to people.

🧭 Customs differ

These are common customs, not fixed laws.

Higher hands, deeper bow.

SĀṢṬĀṄGA

The full prostration

Eight limbs. Sāṣṭāṅga means “with eight limbs”: the body touches the ground at many points while mind, speech and sight are also offered. (Lists of the eight differ.)

Like a stick. It is also called daṇḍa-praṇāma: falling flat like a stick.

Where. In temples, before the guru and before parents on special days.

Not inside the sanctum. In many temples it is done outside the sanctum door, toward the god.

Rising. One rises slowly and joins the palms.

ZAPPY: Like a stick?

PROF. BOLT: Fall straight, without holding anything back. That is the idea.

Fall like a stick.

PAÑCĀṄGA

The kneeling bow

Five limbs. Pañcāṅga: the knees, the hands and the forehead touch the ground.

By custom. In many traditions women bow this way rather than lying full length.

Same honour. It carries the same reverence as the full prostration.

Everyone. Men too use it where space is short.

At home. Many families end the daily pūjā with a kneeling bow.

ZAPPY: Why different for women?

PROF. BOLT: Custom, and dress. The reverence is the same.

A bow on the knees.

TOUCHING THE FEET

The elders’ blessing

The gesture. Bending to touch the feet of parents, grandparents, teachers and holy people.

When. At festivals, birthdays, before exams, journeys and weddings, and on meeting after a long time.

Blessing. The elder places a hand on the head and blesses: long life, success.

Why the feet. The feet are the humblest part; touching them shows humility, and the elder’s grace is said to flow back. (Common explanation.)

Children. Families teach it early.

ZAPPY: What if the elder says “no, no”?

PROF. BOLT: They always say that. Do it anyway, gently.

Humility returned as blessing.

ABHIVĀDANA

The formal greeting

Abhivādana. In traditional settings, a formal greeting to a teacher or elder in which one states one’s lineage (ṛṣis and gotra), Veda branch and name.

Who. Kept mainly in Vedic families, maṭhas and pāṭhaśālās.

Then the bow. The greeting is followed by touching the feet or a full bow.

Reply. The elder answers with a blessing.

Here. We describe it; the exact formula is learned from one’s family or teacher.

ZAPPY: Why say your ancestors’ names?

PROF. BOLT: So the teacher knows whose child stands before him. You bring your line with you.

You bow with your whole line.

NAMASKĀRA TO ALL

Sun, earth, stage, tools

☀️ Sūrya namaskāra

A sequence of postures saluting the sun, practised in yoga.

🌍 The earth

Many touch the ground on waking, asking pardon for stepping on her.

💃 The stage

Dancers and musicians bow to the stage and instruments before performing.

📚 Books

A book touched by the foot is touched to the forehead.

🛠️ Tools

Tools and vehicles are honoured at Āyudha Pūjā.

🙏 Every person

Namaste to anyone: the divine in all.

Everything worthy of a bow.

THE BOW

Kavya’s exam morning

The story

A family morning (an illustrative story).

Kavya has her exam. Bag packed, pens checked.
At the door she stops and bends to touch Ajji’s feet.
Ajji puts a hand on her head: “Go well, come back well.” Kavya walks out lighter.

WHAT IT MEANS

Humility and the divine in all

Humility. Bowing lowers the head and the ego. (Common teaching.)

The divine in the other. Namaste is often read as “the divine in me bows to the divine in you”. (Modern interpretation, widely used.)

Respect. It shows respect without touching, suitable for anyone.

Surrender. Before God, the full prostration is surrender of the whole self.

Gratitude. To parents, teachers, earth and sun: thanks.

ZAPPY: Is the “divine in you” meaning in the texts?

PROF. BOLT: It is a modern reading of an old gesture. A good one.

Bow, and see God in the other.
