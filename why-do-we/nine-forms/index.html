The Nine Forms of Bhakti

Prahlāda’s Teaching · explained and illustrated

Prahlāda’s nine ways

HOW THIS WORKS: The best-known map of devotion: Prahlāda’s answer in the Bhāgavata, the verse of nine, a model devotee for each, and the nine forms one by one, from hearing to offering oneself, with everyday and Karnataka examples.

WHERE WE ARE

Asked by his furious father what was the best thing he had learned at school, the boy Prahlāda did not mention grammar or statecraft. He listed nine ways of loving Viṣṇu. That short answer in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa became the most quoted map of devotion in India, the navavidhā bhakti, the nine forms.

This part tells Prahlāda’s story, gives his verse and the traditional list of a model devotee for each form, then takes the nine one by one: hearing, singing, remembering, serving the feet, worship, salutation, service, friendship and offering oneself, each with the scripture’s example, an everyday example and a Karnataka one.

The previous part was Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa; the next is Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka?. Prahlāda’s whole story and Narasiṃha’s appearance are told in Narasiṃha.

Nine doors, one house.

PRAHLĀDA’S ANSWER

A boy’s syllabus

The story

From the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, book 7, chapter 5.

A demon’s son. Hiraṇyakaśipu, king of the daityas, hated Viṣṇu, who had killed his brother. His own son Prahlāda, though, had loved Viṣṇu since before birth, having heard the sage Nārada’s teaching while still in his mother’s womb.
School. The king sent the boy to the sons of Śukra, the demons’ teachers, to learn statecraft, politics and the ways of power, hoping to cure him.
The test. One day the king sat his son on his lap and asked him to recite the best thing he had learned.
The answer. Prahlāda said: hearing about Viṣṇu, singing of him, remembering him, serving his feet, worshipping, saluting, serving, being his friend and offering oneself to him; whoever offers devotion with these nine marks has learned the highest lesson.
The storm. The king flung him down in rage and turned on the teachers, who protested they had taught him no such thing. So began the attempts on the boy’s life that ended with Narasiṃha; see Narasiṃha and Holī.

ZAPPY: A five-year-old taught this?

PROF. BOLT: He learned it in the womb from Nārada, Zappy. The story says love can be learned before anything else.

The best lesson is love.

IN THE VERSE

Śravaṇaṃ kīrtanaṃ viṣṇoḥ

BHĀGAVATA 7.5.23–24
śravaṇaṃ kīrtanaṃ viṣṇoḥ smaraṇaṃ pāda-sevanam |arcanaṃ vandanaṃ dāsyaṃ sakhyam ātma-nivedanam ||iti puṃsārpitā viṣṇau bhaktiś cen nava-lakṣaṇā |kriyeta bhagavaty addhā tan manye ’dhītam uttamam ||
Hearing about Viṣṇu, singing of him, remembering him, serving his feet, worship, salutation, service, friendship and the offering of oneself: if a person offers devotion to Viṣṇu with these nine marks and does it directly, I consider that the highest learning.
WordMeaning
śravaṇamhearing
kīrtanamsinging, praising
pāda-sevanamserving the feet
ātma-nivedanamoffering oneself
nava-lakṣaṇāwith nine marks

The second verse matters. Most people quote only the list. The next line says what makes it count: the nine must be offered to the Lord and done directly, not as show. And Prahlāda calls it the highest learning, answering his father in the language of school.

Text: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.23–24 as given by greenmesg.org and vedabase.io; verse 23 also appears in this site’s Holī guide.
Nine marks, offered directly.

IN THE VERSE

Śrī-viṣṇoḥ śravaṇe parīkṣit

A MODEL FOR EACH FORM
śrī-viṣṇoḥ śravaṇe parīkṣid abhavad vaiyāsakiḥ kīrtaneprahlādaḥ smaraṇe tad-aṅghri-bhajane lakṣmīḥ pṛthuḥ pūjane |akrūras tv abhivandane kapi-patir dāsye ’tha sakhye ’rjunaḥsarvasvātma-nivedane balir abhūt kṛṣṇāptir eṣāṃ parā ||
In hearing of Śrī Viṣṇu it was Parīkṣit; in singing, Śuka, son of Vyāsa; in remembering, Prahlāda; in serving his feet, Lakṣmī; in worship, Pṛthu; in salutation, Akrūra; in service, the monkey-king Hanumān; in friendship, Arjuna; in offering all and himself, Bali. For each of them the attainment of Kṛṣṇa was supreme.
WordMeaning
vaiyāsakiḥŚuka, Vyāsa’s son
aṅghri-bhajanein serving his feet
abhivandanein salutation
kapi-patiḥlord of monkeys, Hanumān
kṛṣṇāptiḥreaching Kṛṣṇa

Nine portraits. This verse, found in Rūpa Gosvāmī’s Padyāvalī and Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu (1.2.265), gives each form a face. Its point is that every one of these nine reached the highest goal through a single form of devotion. The next nine pages begin with these models.

Text: Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.265 (wisdomlib.org; also Padyāvalī 53), transliterated into IAST.
One form was enough.

1 · ŚRAVAṆA

Hearing about God

What it is. Śravaṇa is listening to the names, stories and teachings of God: the epics, the Purāṇas, the hymns, a teacher’s talk. It comes first because love begins with hearing about someone.

The model. King Parīkṣit, cursed to die in seven days, sat on the bank of the Gaṅgā and heard the whole Bhāgavata from the sage Śuka. The tradition says that hearing alone carried him to liberation.

Every day. A grandparent telling a child the Rāmāyaṇa at bedtime, a podcast of a Gītā talk on the way to work, a temple discourse on a festival evening: all are śravaṇa.

In Karnataka. The state has its own art of devotional hearing: gamaka vācana, in which one person sings verses of Kumāravyāsa’s Kannada Mahābhārata or the Jaimini Bhārata and another explains them, and harikathā, story-sermons with song, both performed in temples for audiences who come simply to listen.

ZAPPY: So listening counts as devotion even if I just sit there?

PROF. BOLT: If you are really listening, Zappy. Ask Parīkṣit.

Love begins with listening.

2 · KĪRTANA

Singing his names and glories

What it is. Kīrtana is speaking or singing about God: his names, his deeds, his qualities. It can be a solo hymn, a group bhajan, a recitation or simply telling a story with love.

The model. Śuka, the son of Vyāsa, is the model, because he recited the Bhāgavata to Parīkṣit. The listener and the speaker reached the same goal through two ends of the same act.

Every day. Singing an evening bhajan, reciting a sahasranāma with family, joining the chorus at a temple festival, even humming a devotional song in the kitchen. Singing the name is explained more fully in Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa.

In Karnataka. The Haridāsa songs in Kannada, and on the coast the all-night Yakṣagāna theatre, which retells the epics with song and dance before temple audiences, are kīrtana on a grand scale.

ZAPPY: Do I need a good voice?

PROF. BOLT: God listens for the heart, Zappy, not the pitch. The Haridāsas sang in village lanes.

Sing what you love.

3 · SMARAṆA

Remembering

What it is. Smaraṇa is keeping God in mind: through the name, through his form, through his stories, until remembrance runs under everything else, like Rāmānuja’s unbroken stream of oil.

The model. Prahlāda himself. Thrown from cliffs, trampled by elephants, given poison and seated in fire, he simply went on remembering Viṣṇu, and none of it harmed him.

Every day. A short name said on waking, before a meal, before a hard conversation, before sleep. A picture on the desk that pulls the mind back for a second. Remembrance is the face of bhakti that fits into the busiest life.

In Karnataka. Many Kannada families name children after gods, Veṅkaṭeśa, Lakṣmī, Rāghavēndra, Śaṅkara, so that the name is said a hundred times a day. The Ajāmila story in Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa explains why that matters.

ZAPPY: Is remembering really enough?

PROF. BOLT: It kept Prahlāda alive in a fire, Zappy. That is the Bhāgavata’s answer.

Remember through everything.

4 · PĀDA-SEVANA

Serving the Lord’s feet

What it is. Pāda-sevana is service at the feet of the Lord: humility made physical. It includes washing the deity’s feet in worship, honouring his footprints and sandals, and serving the people and places his feet have touched.

The model. Lakṣmī, goddess of fortune herself, is pictured seated at Viṣṇu’s feet as he rests on the serpent Śeṣa. The wealthiest being in the universe chooses the humblest place.

Every day. Touching the feet of parents and teachers, cleaning the shrine, going on pilgrimage on foot, serving at a temple. The feet stand for the place where the Lord meets the ground.

In Karnataka. Monasteries honour the pādukā, the sandals of their founding teachers, as the teachers themselves: at Śṛṅgeri for Śaṅkara’s line, in the Mādhva maṭhas for Madhva’s. Devotees bow to the sandals before the living guru.

ZAPPY: Why the feet and not the face?

PROF. BOLT: Because the feet are the lowest point, Zappy, and love goes there first. Lakṣmī could sit anywhere.

The humblest place is the highest.

5 · ARCANA

Worship with offerings

What it is. Arcana is formal worship: offering water, flowers, incense, lamp and food to the deity, often in the sixteen traditional steps, at home or in the temple. It is love expressed as hospitality.

The model. King Pṛthu, the first consecrated king of the earth in the Purāṇas, is the model of arcana: a ruler who worshipped Viṣṇu with great sacrifices and daily offerings, and received the Lord in person.

Every day. The morning lamp and flower at the home shrine, the evening ārati, the 108-name archana with flowers. The simplest home pūjā is arcana; see A Simple Home Pūjā. The bath of the deity is in Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka?.

In Karnataka. During Navarātri, Mysuru homes and temples offer the Goddess kuṅkuma-arcana, a pinch of vermilion with each of her thousand names, and every temple counter sells “archana” tickets for a name-offering in the devotee’s name.

ZAPPY: Is arcana just ritual, then?

PROF. BOLT: Ritual with love in it, Zappy. Without love it is housekeeping; with love it is hospitality to God.

Worship as hospitality.

6 · VANDANA

Salutation and prayer

What it is. Vandana is bowing and praying: folded hands, a prostration, a hymn of praise, a quiet word of thanks. It is the body and the voice saying “you are great, I am yours”.

The model. Akrūra, sent by Kaṃsa to fetch Kṛṣṇa from Vraja, saw the marks of Kṛṣṇa’s feet in the dust of the road and leapt from his chariot to roll in them, weeping with joy (Bhāgavata 10.38). He came as an enemy’s messenger and arrived as a devotee.

Every day. A namaskāra to the shrine before leaving home, a bow to the sun, a prostration at the temple, a prayer said with joined palms. The meaning of the gesture is in Why Do We Do Namaskāra?.

In Karnataka. Tulunāḍu keeps a whole rite of salutation, the Durgā namaskāra, an offering of repeated bows to the Goddess with lamps and flowers; see Durgā Namaskāra.

ZAPPY: Rolling in the dust? Really?

PROF. BOLT: Out of joy, Zappy. Akrūra could not wait to meet Kṛṣṇa, so he greeted the footprints first.

Bow with your whole self.

7 · DĀSYA

Service as a servant

What it is. Dāsya is the mood of the loyal servant: doing what the Lord wants, without asking for reward, glad simply to be useful. It is service with the self set aside.

The model. Hanumān. He leapt the ocean, burned Laṅkā and carried a mountain of herbs for Rāma, and when offered any reward, asked only to go on serving. His joy is to be called Rāma’s servant.

Every day. Volunteering at a temple, cooking for a festival, cleaning up after a satsaṅga, caring for the sick as service to God. Bhakti as service is explored in What Is Bhakti?.

In Karnataka. Hanumān is everywhere in the state: at the entrance to villages, on the hill of Añjanādri near Hampi that tradition counts as his birthplace, and in the Mādhva teaching that Madhva himself was an incarnation of Vāyu, Hanumān’s father.

ZAPPY: Isn’t calling yourself a servant a bit low?

PROF. BOLT: Hanumān didn’t think so, Zappy, and he could lift mountains.

Glad to be useful.

8 · SAKHYA

Friendship with God

What it is. Sakhya is friendship: relating to God as a companion, with trust, honesty and even teasing. It is the mood that lets a devotee argue, complain and laugh with the Lord.

The model. Arjuna, who travelled, joked, ate and fought beside Kṛṣṇa, and asked him to drive his chariot. After seeing the cosmic form he apologised for every careless word; but the friendship went on. The cowherd boys of Vraja are the other great models.

Every day. Talking to God plainly in the evening about the day, as to a friend; sharing a joy or a worry without ceremony. The texts allow it: Kṛṣṇa calls Arjuna his friend and devotee in the same breath (Gītā 4.3).

In Karnataka. Purandara Dāsa and Kanaka Dāsa often address Kṛṣṇa with the frankness of a friend, scolding him, bargaining with him, telling him what they think, in songs still sung everywhere in the state.

ZAPPY: Can I really talk to God like a friend?

PROF. BOLT: Arjuna did, Zappy, and he got a whole Gītā out of it.

A friend who is also God.

9 · ĀTMA-NIVEDANA

Offering oneself

What it is. Ātma-nivedana is the last and fullest form: offering everything, including oneself, to God. It is the surrender of What Is Bhakti? made complete, holding nothing back.

The model. King Bali, who gave the dwarf Vāmana the three steps of land he asked for. When Vāmana grew to fill the universe in two strides and had nowhere for the third, Bali offered his own head. Viṣṇu blessed him and became the guard at his door.

Every day. Ending each day by handing it back: “all I did today, I offer to you.” The Gītā’s advice is the same: whatever you do, eat, offer, give or endure, do it as an offering to me (9.27).

In Karnataka. Bali is remembered with love on Bali Pāḍyami, the day after Dīpāvali’s new moon, when Karnataka and Tulunāḍu homes welcome him back for a day with lamps; see Dīpāvali.

ZAPPY: Why is the last one the hardest?

PROF. BOLT: Because the first eight still have a “me” doing them, Zappy. The ninth gives the “me” away too.

Give the giver.

WHERE TO START

Matching the nine to a temperament

The Gauḍīya teacher Jīva Gosvāmī notes that one of the nine practised fully is enough. Most people start with the one that fits them.

👂 If you like to learn

Begin with śravaṇa: a Purāṇa or a Gītā talk, a little each day.

🎶 If you like music

Kīrtana: a bhajan group, or a sahasranāma sung at home.

⏱️ If you are busy

Smaraṇa: a name at small gaps in the day.

🙌 If you like doing

Dāsya and pāda-sevana: temple service, care for elders.

🪔 If you like ritual

Arcana and vandana: a daily pūjā and namaskāra.

💬 If you like honesty

Sakhya: talk to God plainly, as a friend. Ātma-nivedana grows out of all of these.

ZAPPY: Will I have to move on to the others?

PROF. BOLT: They tend to arrive on their own, Zappy. Hearing leads to singing, singing to remembering, and so on.

Start where you are.
