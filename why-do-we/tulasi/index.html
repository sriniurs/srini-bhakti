Why Do We Worship Tulasī?

The Sacred Basil · explained and illustrated

A goddess in a pot

HOW THIS WORKS: Why we worship tulasī: the plant, the tulasī kaṭṭe, the daily lamp, the story of Vṛndā, the Tulasī vivāha, leaves in Viṣṇu’s worship, picking rules, beads and the last rites.

WHERE WE ARE

In the courtyard of countless Hindu homes stands a small raised pot with a bushy plant: the tulasī, holy basil. Every evening a lamp is lit before it and the women of the house walk round it. Its leaves go into every worship of Viṣṇu, its wood becomes prayer beads, and once a year it is married to the Lord.

This part explains the plant and its kinds, the tulasī platform, the daily worship, the story of Vṛndā, the wedding of Tulasī, the leaves in Viṣṇu’s worship, the rules for picking them, the beads, and what tulasī means.

THE PLANT

Holy basil

Holy basil. Tulasī is Ocimum tenuiflorum, a fragrant herb related to sweet basil.

Kṛṣṇa tulasī. The dark, purple-leaved kind, named for Kṛṣṇa’s dark colour.

Rāma tulasī. The bright green kind.

Fragrance. Its strong scent is part of its place in worship.

In medicine. It is widely used in traditional remedies; this site does not give medical advice.

ZAPPY: Can I grow it?

PROF. BOLT: Sun, water, no frost. It is easy, and it will reward you.

A fragrant sacred herb.

THE TULASĪ KAṬṬE

The platform in the courtyard

Platform. Tulasī is grown in a raised masonry platform, the tulasī kaṭṭe (Kannaḍa) or vṛndāvana.

Place. Often in the centre of the courtyard or in front of the house.

Niche. A small niche holds the lamp.

Decoration. Painted, marked with turmeric and kuṅkuma, with a kōlam or rangoli before it.

Small homes. A clay pot on a balcony serves the same purpose.

ZAPPY: Why the courtyard?

PROF. BOLT: It is the heart of the house, and the plant needs sun.

The heart of the courtyard.

DAILY WORSHIP

Water, lamp, circling

Morning. After bathing, water is poured at the plant’s root, often with a prayer.

Dusk. A lamp is lit before it at twilight.

Circling. The plant is circled, usually three or more times (see pradakṣiṇā).

Women’s worship. In many homes it is mainly kept by the women of the house.

Marks. Turmeric and kuṅkuma are applied to the platform.

ZAPPY: Every single day?

PROF. BOLT: Every day, in homes that keep it. It takes five minutes.

Water at dawn, light at dusk.

THE STORY OF VṚNDĀ

How a devoted wife became tulasī

The story

A Purāṇic story; versions in the Padma, Devī Bhāgavata and other Purāṇas differ.

Vṛndā was the faithful wife of the asura Jalandhara; her devotion made him unbeatable, even by the gods.
To end his tyranny, Viṣṇu took Jalandhara’s form and broke her vow; Jalandhara was then defeated.
Vṛndā cursed Viṣṇu to become a stone (the śāligrāma) and gave up her life; Viṣṇu blessed her to be worshipped as tulasī and to be dear to him forever.

TULASĪ VIVĀHA

The wedding of the plant

When. In the month of Kārtika, on or around the twelfth day of the bright half (Utthāna Dvādaśī) when Viṣṇu wakes from his sleep. (Days vary by region.)

The groom. Tulasī is married to Viṣṇu, Kṛṣṇa or the śāligrāma stone.

Like a real wedding. The plant is dressed as a bride; a canopy of sugarcane is set up; songs, garlands and a feast follow.

Karnataka. Kept in many homes as Tulasī habba, with lamps and gooseberry branches.

Season. It marks the start of the wedding season in many communities. See Kārtika.

ZAPPY: A wedding for a plant?

PROF. BOLT: For the goddess in the plant. And for the family, a festival.

The goddess marries the Lord.

IN VIṢṆU’S WORSHIP

No pūjā without a leaf

Essential. Tulasī leaves are offered in the worship of Viṣṇu, Kṛṣṇa and their forms; many say naivedya to Viṣṇu is incomplete without one.

On the food. A leaf is placed on the food offering before it is offered.

In tīrtha. Tulasī is added to the holy water in Vaiṣṇava temples.

Not for every god. By common belief, tulasī is not offered to Gaṇeśa. (Tradition, linked to a story.)

Garlands. Kṛṣṇa’s idols wear tulasī garlands, as at Uḍupi.

ZAPPY: Why not to Gaṇeśa?

PROF. BOLT: A story of a quarrel and a curse. The custom stands.

Viṣṇu’s favourite leaf.

PICKING THE LEAVES

When and how

With respect. Leaves are picked gently, after bathing, often with a prayer asking permission.

Not at night. By common custom, tulasī is not picked after sunset.

Certain days. Many avoid picking on dvādaśī and some other days; leaves are gathered the day before. (Customs vary.)

Reusable. A tulasī leaf offered once may be washed and offered again, it is said. (Tradition.)

Never thrown. Dried leaves are put in water or the earth, not in the rubbish.

ZAPPY: So many rules for a leaf?

PROF. BOLT: It is a goddess, remember. You would ask before you took.

Ask before you pick.

TULASĪ BEADS

The mālā of the Vaiṣṇavas

Beads. The woody stem of tulasī is cut into beads for prayer strings (japa mālā).

Worn. Many Vaiṣṇavas wear a tulasī necklace (kaṇṭhī) as a sign of devotion.

Japa. The mālā is used to count the Lord’s names, often 108 times (see Part 9).

Śaivas. Śaivas use rudrākṣa instead.

Care. Mālās are kept clean and treated with respect.

ZAPPY: Can anyone wear one?

PROF. BOLT: Traditions differ; many teachers give it at initiation.

Counting on the goddess’s wood.

AT THE END OF LIFE

A leaf for the dying

Last water. By tradition, water with a tulasī leaf, often Gaṅgā water, is placed on the lips of the dying.

Peace. It is believed to bring the presence of Viṣṇu at the last moment. (Belief.)

Final rites. Tulasī leaves are used in funeral and ancestral rites in many communities.

Comfort. For families it is a simple act of love.

See. Pitṛ Pakṣa.

ZAPPY: Why at the end?

PROF. BOLT: The plant dear to Viṣṇu goes with the soul to Viṣṇu.

A leaf for the last journey.

WHAT IT MEANS

Devotion that grows

Devotion. Vṛndā’s faithfulness is honoured in the plant: tulasī stands for single-minded devotion. (Interpretation.)

Household guardian. The tulasī in the courtyard is seen as the protecting goddess of the home.

Daily discipline. Watering and lighting it every day keeps worship in the rhythm of the house.

Living worship. Unlike an image, the plant grows, flowers and needs care: a living goddess. (Interpretation.)

Nature. Many see in it the Hindu reverence for plants and the earth.

ZAPPY: A goddess that needs watering?

PROF. BOLT: The best kind: you look after her, she looks after you.

Look after her, she looks after you.
