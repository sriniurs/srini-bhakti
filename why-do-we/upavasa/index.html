Why Do We Fast?

Upavāsa: Staying Near · explained and illustrated

Make room for God

HOW THIS WORKS: Why we fast: upavāsa and vrata, the kinds of fast, the days kept, fasting foods, how a fast is begun and broken, the Gītā on balance, who should not fast and the inner fast.

WHERE WE ARE

On Ekādaśī many skip rice. On Mahāśivarātri people stay awake without food. Every Monday someone’s grandmother eats only once. Fasting, upavāsa, is one of the most widely kept Hindu practices, and one of the most varied: from a full day without water to simply giving up one food.

This part explains the word, the kinds of fast, the days kept, what is eaten and what avoided, how a fast is begun and broken, what the Gītā says about balance, who should not fast, and the inner fast of mind and speech.

ZAPPY: Which kind is best?

PROF. BOLT: The one you can keep with a calm mind. A fast with a bad temper is just hunger.

Make room for God.

THE WORD

Staying near

Upavāsa. Upa (near) + vāsa (dwelling): “staying near”, that is, near God. (Traditional explanation.)

Vrata. A vrata is a vow kept for a deity or purpose; fasting is often part of it.

Not punishment. The point is to spend the day close to the divine, with less attention on food.

Words. Kannaḍa upavāsa, Hindi vrat or upvās.

Old. Fasting appears in Vedic ritual and in the Purāṇas’ many vratas.

ZAPPY: So a fast day is a God day?

PROF. BOLT: That is the idea. Less kitchen, more prayer.

Less food, more nearness.

KINDS OF FAST

From strict to gentle

Families and communities choose different forms for the same day.

💧 Nirjala

No food and no water, as on Nirjalā Ekādaśī; the strictest.

🍽️ Nirāhāra

No food, but water allowed.

🍎 Phalāhāra

Fruit, milk and permitted foods only.

1️⃣ Ekabhukta

One meal in the day.

🌙 Naktavrata

Eating only after nightfall or after seeing the moon, as on Saṅkaṣṭī.

🌾 No grains

Avoiding rice and cereals, as on Ekādaśī.

Choose the fast you can keep well.

DAYS OF FASTING

The calendar of vratas

🌗 Ekādaśī

Twice a month for Viṣṇu; see Ekādaśī.

🔱 Mahāśivarātri

Fast and night vigil for Śiva; see Śivarātri.

🪔 Navarātri

Nine days for the Goddess; see Navarātri.

🌆 Pradoṣa

Evening fast for Śiva; see Pradoṣa.

🐘 Saṅkaṣṭī

Fast until moonrise for Gaṇeśa; see Saṅkaṣṭī.

📅 Weekdays

Monday for Śiva, Tuesday for Hanumān or Devī, Saturday for Śani or Veṅkaṭeśa, by family custom.

Every god has a fast day.

WHAT IS EATEN

Fasting foods

Fruit and milk. Allowed in most partial fasts.

Substitutes. Sago (sābakki), sweet potato, potato, certain flours and millets replace grains, by regional custom.

Avoided. Rice and cereals (on Ekādaśī), onion, garlic, meat and alcohol on most fast days.

Salt. Some use rock salt only; others avoid salt. (Customs vary.)

Simple. The idea is simple, light food, not a feast of fasting snacks. (Common teaching.)

ZAPPY: Are fasting snacks cheating?

PROF. BOLT: A plate of fried sago at a fast? Your grandmother will smile and say nothing.

Light, simple, sattvic.

BEGIN AND BREAK

Saṅkalpa and pāraṇa

Saṅkalpa. A fast begins with a resolve: stating, often with water in the palm, the vow and its purpose.

The day. Prayer, reading, temple visits and the god’s name fill the time saved from meals.

Vigil. Some fasts include staying awake at night (jāgaraṇa), as on Śivarātri.

Pāraṇa. The fast is broken at a set time, after worship, often with prasāda; Ekādaśī is broken on the next morning within a fixed window.

Gently. It is broken with light food, not a heavy meal.

ZAPPY: Can I break it early?

PROF. BOLT: If you must. Say so honestly to God, eat, and try again another day.

Vow, keep, break gently.

THE GĪTĀ ON BALANCE

Neither too much nor too little

Gītā 6.16–17. Kṛṣṇa says yoga is not for one who eats too much, nor for one who does not eat at all; it is for one moderate in food, sleep and action. (Sense, not a quotation.)

Gītā 17.5–6. He criticises austerities that torment the body, done from pride or show. (Sense.)

So. The Gītā’s ideal is balance, not starvation.

Inner purpose. A fast is a tool for steadiness of mind.

Read. Bhagavad Gītā.

ZAPPY: So Kṛṣṇa says don’t overdo it?

PROF. BOLT: Plainly. Moderation is his rule.

Balance, not hardship.

WHO SHOULD NOT FAST

Care comes first

Tradition exempts. Traditional rules excuse the sick, the very young, the elderly and pregnant or nursing women from strict fasts.

Substitutes. They may keep a lighter form, or offer prayer instead.

Health conditions. People with diabetes or on regular medicine should take their doctor’s advice before any strict fast.

No harm. A fast that harms the body goes against the Gītā’s teaching of balance.

Faith counts. Devotion is what is offered, not suffering.

ZAPPY: Isn’t that a soft option?

PROF. BOLT: It is the scriptural option. God asked for your heart, not your hospital bed.

Devotion, not damage.

THE INNER FAST

Fasting of speech and mind

Mauna. Some keep a day of silence (mauna), a fast from speech.

From anger. Teachers say: on a fast day, avoid anger, gossip and harsh words. (Common teaching.)

From screens. Many today add a fast from phone or television: the same spirit.

The senses. Withdrawing the senses, not only the stomach, is the deeper fast. (Interpretation.)

Kindness. Many give the food they did not eat, or its cost, to someone in need.

ZAPPY: A fast from my phone?

PROF. BOLT: The modern upavāsa. Your stomach might find it easier.

Fast from more than food.

AMMA’S MONDAY

Why Amma eats once on Mondays

The story

A family story (illustrative).

Every Monday Amma lights the lamp before Śiva at dawn and skips breakfast and lunch.
She works as usual. “Doesn’t it make you cranky?” asks Rahul. “It makes me remember,” she says.
At dusk she prays again, then eats a simple meal. “One day a week for him,” she says. “He gives me the other six.”
