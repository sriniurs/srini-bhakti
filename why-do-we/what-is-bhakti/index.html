What Is Bhakti?

Love, Remembrance, Service, Surrender · explained and illustrated

Love turned towards God

HOW THIS WORKS: The idea behind this whole library: the word bhakti, four teachers’ definitions, its four faces of love, remembrance, service and surrender, Kanaka’s window and Śabarī’s fruit, the saints who carried it, and how to begin.

WHERE WE ARE

This library is called Bhakti, and almost everything in it, the hymns, the temples, the festivals, the stories, is bhakti in one form or another. So the word deserves a page of its own. For me it began not with a book but with watching people: a grandmother lighting the lamp before anyone else woke, a temple cook who would not taste the food until it was offered, a stranger at Uḍupi pressing his face to a small window to see the child Kṛṣṇa.

This part explains what bhakti is, using the words of the texts and the habits of ordinary homes: the meaning of the word, how four great teachers defined it, and its four faces of love, remembrance, service and surrender, with two stories, the saints who carried it across India, and how to begin.

It opens a seven-part set called The Heart of Bhakti: Who Is Your Iṣṭa Devatā?, What Is Darśan?, Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa, The Nine Forms of Bhakti, Why Do We Perform Abhiṣeka? and What Makes a Mūrti Sacred?. Bhakti as one of the four paths is set beside the others in The Four Yogas.

ZAPPY: Is bhakti just being religious?

PROF. BOLT: No, Zappy. Plenty of religious people do rituals without love, and plenty of loving people never call it bhakti. Bhakti is love turned towards God.

THE WORD

Bhaj: to share, to belong, to serve

The root. Bhakti comes from the Sanskrit root bhaj. Its first sense is “to share, to have a portion of”; from there it widens to “belong to”, “resort to”, “serve” and “worship”. The old dictionaries gloss it simply as service and worship.

A share in God. So the word carries a picture. A bhakta is not a spectator of the Divine but a sharer: one who belongs to God and to whom God belongs. The same root gives bhaga, “portion, good fortune”, and Bhagavān, “the one who has every good portion”, the Lord.

Older than its fame. The word is old, but its meaning of loving devotion to a personal God becomes central only in the late Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the Purāṇas. By the time of the Tamil saints it had become a whole way of life.

Not one feeling. In Indian thought bhakti is not a single emotion but a relationship that can take many moods: awe, friendship, a servant’s loyalty, a parent’s tenderness, a lover’s longing. Later teachers made a careful science of those moods.

ZAPPY: So Bhagavān and bhakti are the same word?

PROF. BOLT: Cousins, Zappy, from the same root. The Lord has every portion, and the devotee shares in it.

Bhakti means belonging.

For the great soul who has the highest love for God, and for the guru as for God, these teachings, once told, shine out.
WordMeaning

The first appearance. This closing verse of the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad is counted among the earliest uses of bhakti for love of God. It says something striking: the Upaniṣad’s teaching does not open to cleverness alone. It shines for the one who loves, and who loves the teacher too.

The teaching shines for love.

FOUR TEACHERS, FOUR DEFINITIONS

What the great texts say bhakti is

Each school defines bhakti its own way; each line paraphrases the source named.

💗 Nārada: supreme love

The Nārada Bhakti Sūtra opens by defining bhakti as being of the nature of supreme love for Him, and adds that it is of the nature of nectar (sūtras 2–3).

🔗 Śāṇḍilya: the highest attachment

The Śāṇḍilya Bhakti Sūtra calls it parānurakti, the highest attachment, directed to the Lord (1.2). Nārada himself names Śāṇḍilya among the teachers before him.

🛢️ Rāmānuja: unbroken remembrance

For Rāmānuja the meditation that liberates is steady remembrance, unbroken like a flow of oil (dhruvā smṛti), filled with love. Devotion is knowledge that never lets go.

🪞 Śaṅkara: seeking one’s true nature

The Vivekacūḍāmaṇi, ascribed to Śaṅkara, says that among the means to liberation bhakti is the greatest, and defines it as the search into one’s own real nature (31).

🤝 Do they disagree?

Less than it seems: the commentators say all of them point to the same intensity, aimed at the Lord or at the Self.

🧭 A working definition

Love for God that becomes constant, serves, and finally lets go of the self.

ZAPPY: Śaṅkara the philosopher praises bhakti?

PROF. BOLT: He wrote some of the best-loved hymns in the language, Zappy, from the Bhaja Govindam to the Saundaryalaharī tradition. Knowledge and love were never enemies to him.

Four teachers, one fire.

Love as the definition. Nārada does not begin with rules or rites but with love, and love at its highest. Calling it nectar (amṛta) says two things at once: it is sweet in itself, and it is what makes a person deathless. FIRST FACE: LOVE

Prema, and the moods it takes

Not fear, not trade. The texts insist that bhakti is not fear of punishment and not a deal for favours. Nārada says such love asks for nothing; it is its own reward. A child’s love for a mother is the picture most teachers use.

Moods of love. Vaiṣṇava teachers, above all Rūpa Gosvāmī in the sixteenth century, described love for God in five main moods: peace (śānta), a servant’s loyalty (dāsya), friendship (sakhya), a parent’s tenderness (vātsalya) and a lover’s longing (mādhurya).

Each has its saints. Hanumān is the model servant; Arjuna and the cowherd boys are friends; Yaśodā is the mother; the gopīs and Mīrā are lovers; the sages are at peace. A devotee can find himself in one mood and grow into another.

Everyday love. In ordinary homes it looks simple: saving the first fruit of the tree for the shrine, talking to the image of Kṛṣṇa like a child of the house, feeling homesick for a temple. None of that needs a scripture; the scripture names it.

ZAPPY: Can I love God like a friend? Isn’t that cheeky?

PROF. BOLT: Arjuna did, Zappy, and Kṛṣṇa kept driving his chariot. The tradition says God accepts the mood you bring.

Love in every mood.

SECOND FACE: REMEMBRANCE

Smaraṇa, the thread through the day

Unbroken like oil. Rāmānuja’s image is the best known: remembrance of God should flow like oil poured from one vessel to another, in a single unbroken stream, not in drops. He took the idea from the Chāndogya Upaniṣad’s promise that when remembrance is steady, every knot of the heart is loosened.

Small anchors. In practice, remembrance is held by small habits: a name said on waking, “Śrī” written at the top of a page, “Hari” or “Śiva” before a journey, a pause before eating. Karnataka households end the day with a prayer before sleep.

The name. The easiest anchor is a divine name, repeated aloud or silently. That practice, nāma japa and nāma smaraṇa, has its own part in this set: Nāma Japa and Nāma Smaraṇa.

Not escape. Remembering God does not mean leaving work. The Gītā’s advice to Arjuna is to remember Him and fight: keep the mind fixed while the hands do their duty (8.7). The busiest people in the epics are its examples.

ZAPPY: How do I remember God all day? I forget my keys.

PROF. BOLT: Start with three moments, Zappy: waking, eating, sleeping. The thread gets longer by itself.

Remember, and keep working.

THIRD FACE: SERVICE

Sevā, to God and to his people

Serving God. The temple runs on sevā: sweeping the yard, stringing flowers, carrying the deity in procession, cooking the offering. Hanumān, who wanted nothing but to serve Rāma, is its great example. Many families offer one sevā a year at their own temple.

Serving his people. The Bhāgavata has the sage Kapila say that the Lord lives in every being as its Self, and that worshipping an image while despising living beings is a mockery of worship (3.29.21). Feeding the hungry is therefore not separate from bhakti but part of it.

Karnataka’s word. Basavaṇṇa and the Vacanakāras of twelfth-century Kalyāṇa gave this a Kannada name: kāyaka, honest work as worship, and dāsoha, sharing what one earns. “Kāyakavē kailāsa”: work itself is Śiva’s heaven. See Basavaṇṇa.

Anna dāna. The great temple kitchens of Karnataka, at Dharmasthaḷa, Uḍupi, Kukke and Śṛṅgeri, feed thousands a day as an act of devotion. Anyone who has eaten there off a plantain leaf has received bhakti in its plainest form.

ZAPPY: So serving lunch at the temple counts?

PROF. BOLT: It counts double, Zappy. Once for the god, once for the hungry person.

Serve God by serving his people.

FOURTH FACE: SURRENDER

Śaraṇāgati: doing your best and letting go

The last step. Surrender, śaraṇāgati or prapatti, means taking refuge: “I am yours; you take care of it.” The Gītā’s final teaching is exactly this: give up all other supports and come to Me alone for refuge; I will free you from every sin; do not grieve (18.66).

Rāma’s vow. When Rāvaṇa’s brother Vibhīṣaṇa came over to Rāma’s side, the monkeys suspected a trick. Rāma replied with a vow: to anyone who comes to him even once and says “I am yours”, he gives freedom from fear of every being (Rāmāyaṇa 6.18.33).

The elephant’s cry. In the Bhāgavata, the elephant king Gajendra, caught by a crocodile, fought for a thousand years on his own strength. Only when that ran out did he lift a lotus and call on the Lord; Viṣṇu came at once. The story is the classic picture of surrender after effort.

Not giving up. Surrender is not laziness. The texts picture it as doing one’s best and leaving the result. A surgeon who prepares carefully, operates with full attention and then lets go of the outcome is closer to it than one who does nothing and “leaves it to God”. (Interpretation.)

ZAPPY: If I surrender, do I stop trying?

PROF. BOLT: No, Zappy. Gajendra fought first. You stop carrying the worry, not the work.

Do your best, then let go.

KANAKA’S WINDOW

A devotee kept outside

The story

Uḍupi tradition on Kanaka Dāsa (16th century); historians treat the miracle as a later folk story. See Uḍupi Śrīkṛṣṇa.

A shepherd’s son. Kanaka Dāsa, born into the Kuruba shepherd community and once a soldier, became one of the great Kannada Haridāsas, singing of Kṛṣṇa with his tambura.
Turned away. He came to Uḍupi to see the child Kṛṣṇa installed by Madhvācārya, but because of his birth he was not allowed into the shrine.
Singing behind the wall. He went round to the back of the temple, sat on the far side of the wall and sang to the Lord he could not see, day after day.
The turn. The story says the image, which faced east, turned to face west, and the wall behind it cracked, so that Kanaka saw Kṛṣṇa through the gap. The pontiff Vādirāja Tīrtha is said to have widened the crack into a window instead of plastering it.
The window. Uḍupi’s Kṛṣṇa still faces west, and devotees still look at him through the small latticed window called Kanakana Kiṇḍi, Kanaka’s window, a standing reminder that love does not wait for permission.

ZAPPY: So God came to the back door?

PROF. BOLT: He turned round, Zappy. That is the whole point of the story: the one kept outside got the best view.

Love finds a window.

ŚABARĪ WAITS

Fruit gathered for Rāma

The story

From the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa 73–74. The tasted-berries detail belongs to later devotional retellings.

A servant of sages. Śabarī, a woman of a forest people, served the disciples of the sage Mataṅga in their hermitage on the western shore of Lake Pampā, near today’s Hampi.
A promise. When the sages left this world, they told her that Rāma would one day come to the hermitage, that she should welcome him, and that on seeing him she would reach the highest worlds.
Years of waiting. She kept the hermitage and waited. Every day she gathered forest fruit from the banks of the Pampā in case he came.
He comes. Directed by Kabandha, Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa reached her door. She touched their feet, washed them, and offered the fruit she had gathered (3.74.17). Later tellings add that she tasted each berry to be sure it was sweet, and that Rāma ate them gladly.
Fulfilled. Śabarī showed them the hermitage, then entered the fire and rose to the worlds of the sages. Rāma, the prince, had been fed by a forest woman, and the epic says nothing could be more fitting.

ZAPPY: Why do people love the berries part if Vālmīki doesn’t have it?

PROF. BOLT: Because it says what the whole story means, Zappy: love cares more about sweetness than about rules.

Waiting is also devotion.

BHAKTI IN AN ORDINARY DAY

Six small doors

Bhakti does not need special hours. These are six ordinary places where it shows up in Indian homes.

🪔 At dawn

Lighting the lamp and saying one name before the phone is touched.

🍚 In the kitchen

Cooking as an offering; the first portion set aside for the shrine or for a guest.

💼 At work

Kāyaka: doing the job well, honestly, as service. The Gītā calls it offering every act (9.27).

🎶 In company

Singing bhajans together on a Thursday or Saturday evening; the Haridāsa songs in Karnataka.

🤲 For others

A coin, a meal, an hour of help, given without fuss.

🌙 At night

A short prayer before sleep, handing the day back.

ZAPPY: None of these need a temple.

PROF. BOLT: Exactly, Zappy. The temple is wonderful, but the home is where bhakti lives most of the time.

Six doors in one day.

BHAKTI AND THE OTHER PATHS

Love, work, knowledge, meditation

One of four. Hindu teachers speak of four great paths: work done without selfish desire (karma), knowledge (jñāna), meditation (rāja yoga) and love (bhakti). The Gītā weaves all four together; the full map is in The Four Yogas.

Often called the easiest. Many teachers say bhakti suits most people, because everyone already knows how to love. The Gītā’s twelfth chapter has Kṛṣṇa say that the path to the formless is harder for embodied beings, and praises the devotee who is friendly to all.

Not against knowledge. Bhakti is not anti-intellectual. Rāmānuja and Madhva built entire philosophies on it, Śaṅkara praised it, and the Śvetāśvatara verse ties knowledge to love. In practice most devotees use all four paths without naming them.

Not against ritual. Nor does bhakti throw out ritual. A pūjā done with love is bhakti; the same pūjā done mechanically is only a pūjā. The rest of this set, on darśan, japa, abhiṣeka and the mūrti, shows the rituals with the love put back in.

ZAPPY: Do I have to pick one path?

PROF. BOLT: Most people walk all four on different days, Zappy. Love usually holds the others together.

Four paths, one summit.

THE SAINTS WHO CARRIED IT

From the Tamil land to the whole of India

Born in the south. From about the sixth to the ninth centuries, the Tamil Āḻvārs (for Viṣṇu) and Nāyanmārs (for Śiva) sang in their own language, travelled from temple to temple, and included women and people of every birth. Bhakti as a mass movement begins with them.

Karnataka’s voices. In the twelfth century the Vacanakāras, Basavaṇṇa, Akka Mahādēvī and Allama Prabhu, wrote short Kannada sayings that refused caste and empty ritual. From the fifteenth century the Haridāsas, Śrīpādarāja, Vyāsatīrtha, Purandara Dāsa and Kanaka Dāsa, sang Kṛṣṇa in Kannada; see Purandara Dāsa.

North and west. Over the next centuries the same current flowed through Mahārāṣṭra (Jñāneśvar, Nāmdev, Tukārām), Rājasthān (Mīrā), the Gaṅgā plain (Kabīr, Sūrdās, Tulasīdās) and Bengal (Caitanya).

A Purāṇa’s map. The Padma Purāṇa’s praise of the Bhāgavata personifies Bhakti as a woman who tells her own story; the verse on the next page has her say where she was born and where she grew up. Karnataka is in it.

ZAPPY: Why did it start in the south?

PROF. BOLT: Historians debate it, Zappy. The songs in the people’s own language, sung in the temples, certainly helped.

Sung in every language.

WHAT BHAKTI IS NOT

Four common mix-ups

Not a bargain. “Give me a job and I will break a hundred coconuts” is a prayer, and the tradition does not scorn it; the Gītā counts even the distressed and the wealth-seeking among devotees (7.16). But it calls the one who loves without asking the dearest.

Not fear. Bhakti is not cringing before an angry god. The texts compare it to a child’s trust; the Lord of the Bhāgavata is above all the friend and protector of those who turn to him.

Not blind. Love does not forbid questions. Arjuna asks question after question in the Gītā, and Kṛṣṇa answers every one. The Haridāsas and Vacanakāras mocked empty ritual sharply; devotion and good sense go together.

Not leaving the world. Nārada’s sūtras warn against neglecting one’s duties in the name of devotion until those duties fall away naturally. Most of India’s bhaktas were householders: farmers, traders, cooks, queens and soldiers.

ZAPPY: So I can still ask God for things?

PROF. BOLT: Of course, Zappy. Just don’t make it the whole relationship. Nobody likes a friend who only calls when he needs money.

Love, not a transaction.

HOW TO BEGIN

Small steps, in any order

Bhakti grows by doing, not by deciding. Any one of these is a real beginning.

1️⃣ Choose a form

Pick the form of God you feel closest to, often your family’s. That is your iṣṭa devatā: Part 2.

2️⃣ Light one lamp

A lamp, a flower and a few words each morning. A Simple Home Pūjā shows how.

3️⃣ Take one name

Say a divine name a few times a day, or one round on a mālā: Part 4.

4️⃣ Go and see

Visit a temple for darśan and stand quietly for a minute: Part 3.

5️⃣ Read one story

A story of a devotee, Prahlāda, Śabarī, Kanaka, read slowly. The nine ways in Part 5 give ideas.

6️⃣ Serve once

Help at a temple kitchen or feed someone. Watch what it does to you.

ZAPPY: What if I feel nothing at first?

PROF. BOLT: Most people don’t, Zappy. You water a plant for weeks before it flowers. Keep watering.

Start small, start today.

Stories: Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa 3.73–74 (sanskritdocuments.org; hindutone.com on the berries as a later addition); Wikipedia, “Kanakana kindi”, “Udupi Sri Krishna Matha” (historians on the folk story), “Kanaka Dasa”; Karnataka Tourism (Kanakana Kiṇḍi).

Context: dharmawiki, “Bhakti” (root bhaj, sevā and ārādhanā); Wikipedia, “Bhakti movement”, “Basava”; this site’s Basavaṇṇa, Purandara Dāsa, Four Yogas and Uḍupi guides.

Sources differ / unverified: the exact wording of Nārada’s second sūtra varies by manuscript; the Vivekacūḍāmaṇi’s authorship by Śaṅkara is questioned by some scholars; the Kanaka miracle and Śabarī’s tasted berries are later traditions; the five bhāvas follow Rūpa Gosvāmī’s scheme, and other schools classify differently; dates of the saints are traditional.

Study notes: Sanskrit from the editions named on each verse page. Interpretations are marked as such. Meanings are our own plain-English renderings. Zappy and Prof. Bolt are fictional.
